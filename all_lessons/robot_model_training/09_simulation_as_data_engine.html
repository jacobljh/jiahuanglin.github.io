Simulation as a data engine

A simulator breaks lesson 04's rate limit by four orders of magnitude, computes contact forces exactly, labels everything perfectly, and resets for free. It also gets the friction wrong. Both halves are true, and the useful question is which parameters you must measure before you are allowed to randomise them.

Where we are
Lesson 08 established that contact data is the most valuable and least obtainable. Simulation offers exactly that, at unlimited volume. synthetic_vision owns simulation as a designed data-generating process for perception — appearance, sensors, labels, and the visual transfer contract. This lesson handles the gap it does not: dynamics, which is a different gap with a different fix.
Forced by 08Contact and force data is decisive and nearly unobtainable on real hardware. This stepManufacture it — and calibrate the dynamics parameters before widening the randomisation. Forces 10A simulator supplies rewards and resets too, which finally makes RL affordable.

1 · The leverage, computed

Start a locomotion run at nine in the morning. By the time you have made coffee, the policy has accumulated more walking experience than every physical robot in your building will accumulate this year. That is not a figure of speech and this lesson computes it exactly. What makes the rest of the lesson necessary is the question you are forced to ask immediately afterwards: experience of what?

The number is genuinely large, so it is worth deriving rather than asserting. Modern GPU simulators run thousands of environment copies concurrently. At 4,096 parallel environments stepping at 60 Hz:

4,096 × 60 = 245,760 environment steps per second

Divide by the control rate to convert back into robot-time. At 60 Hz control, one wall-clock second buys 4,096 seconds of experience, so

one wall-clock hour ≈ 4,096 robot-hours ≈ 170 robot-days

Set that beside lesson 04's 60 episodes per operator-hour and the comparison is not close — three to four orders of magnitude. The "Learning to walk in minutes" result made this concrete: locomotion policies trained on 4,096 parallel environments in minutes of wall-clock time, on one GPU.

And the throughput is only the first of five things simulation gives you, of which the last two are the ones people undervalue:

Throughput — 10³–10⁴× wall-clock leveragethe obvious one
Perfect labels — object poses, contact points, forces, segmentation, all exactfree
Free resets — no human puts the scene back (lesson 04: a third of the cycle)free
Free rewards — success is computable, so RL becomes possible (lesson 10)enabling
Counterfactuals — replay the identical scene with one parameter changedimpossible in reality
And the one thing it does not give youthe true dynamics

That counterfactual row is underrated and it is where simulation is uniquely irreplaceable rather than merely cheaper. You cannot run the same real grasp twice with only the friction changed. In simulation you can, which makes causal attribution — what actually made this fail? — tractable in a way it never is on hardware.

2 · What simulators get wrong is exactly what matters

Rank the fidelity of a physics engine by subsystem, and notice the pattern.

subsystemfidelitywhy
rigid-body kinematics, geometry, collision detectionessentially exactSolved mathematics.
gravity, inertia, free-space motionexcellentWell-posed integration.
friction and stictionpoorCoulomb friction is a crude model of a contact-mechanics problem; stiction is discontinuous.
actuator dynamics and latencypoor by defaultReal motors have backlash, compliance, current limits, and delay — usually unmodelled.
deformables — cloth, cable, food, skinpoor to unusableExpensive, unstable, and highly parameter-sensitive.
the contact solver itselfapproximateSimultaneous multi-point contact is solved by an approximation whose artifacts differ per engine.
The pattern, stated bluntly
Simulation is excellent at free-space motion — which lesson 08 identified as the easy part — and poor at contact, which is the part that decides the task. So the naive plan "train in sim because contact data is expensive" walks directly into the one place the simulator is least trustworthy. The plan still works; it just needs the calibration step that this lesson is really about.

3 · Calibrate, then randomise — in that order

Domain randomisation trains across a distribution of dynamics parameters so the policy becomes robust to not knowing the true value. That is a good idea with a failure mode: width cannot fix a wrong centre.

Model it. Let the simulator's parameter mean be μsim, reality be μreal, and the randomisation width be w. Two effects compete. Coverage — how much training mass lands near reality — is roughly Gaussian in the offset:

coverage(w) ≈ exp( −(μreal−μsim)² / 2w² )

and specificity falls as the policy is asked to work across a wider range, so it hedges:

specificity(w) ≈ 1/(1 + w/w0)   ·   success ≈ coverage(w) · specificity(w)

The product has an interior maximum — the familiar inverted U. But look at what happens to it when the offset shrinks. With a large offset, the optimum w is large, because you need enormous width just to cover the truth, and the peak is low because you paid the whole specificity penalty. Identify the parameter first and the offset nearly vanishes: the optimum w becomes small and the peak becomes high. System identification does not merely improve the centre — it lowers the price of robustness.

Which makes the operational order unambiguous, and it is the reverse of what is convenient:

  1. Measure what you can measure. Link masses from the datasheet; joint friction from a slow constant-velocity sweep reading current; actuator delay from a step-response test; payload mass on a scale. An afternoon of experiments, and it shrinks the offset on the parameters that dominate.
  2. Fit what you cannot measure. Roll out a scripted trajectory on the real robot, replay it in simulation, and optimise the remaining parameters to minimise trajectory divergence. This is classical system identification and it is far cheaper than the training run it improves.
  3. Model the actuator as a learned residual. Where the analytic model is inadequate — and for series-elastic or cable-driven arms it is — fit a small network mapping commanded to achieved torque from real data, and insert it into the simulator. This is the highest-value single fix for a poor sim-to-real gap on real hardware.
  4. Then randomise, narrowly, around the calibrated values. Width for the residual uncertainty only, not width as a substitute for knowledge.
  5. Randomise the right things. Friction, mass, damping, latency, and payload — the dynamics parameters. Visual randomisation is a different axis with a different purpose, and synthetic_vision/06 owns it.
The seductive shortcut, named
Randomisation is easy to add and calibration is tedious, so the standard failure is to skip step 1–3 and compensate with enormous width. It produces a policy that is robust to everything and good at nothing — visible as a policy that moves cautiously, slowly, and with excessive margins, and that plateaus well below what the hardware can do. If your sim-trained policy is timid, suspect an uncalibrated centre before you suspect the algorithm.

4 · What still will not transfer, and the honest split

Some things stay broken after every calibration, and knowing which lets you allocate correctly rather than optimistically.

stays broken
Deformables and fine stiction
Cloth, cable, food, and the exact moment a part stops sliding. The models are structurally inadequate, not just mis-parameterised.
stays broken
The unmodelled long tail
A sticky drawer, a worn gripper pad, a cable snagging. Not in the scene description at all, so no parameter covers it.
transfers well
Reach, transport, place
Free-space motion and gross positioning — the majority of most episodes by duration, and where the throughput leverage pays in full.

So the correct division of labour follows the fidelity table rather than a preference: simulate the free-space majority of the episode, and spend real robot hours on the contact-rich minority. That is the same decomposition lesson 04 recommended for scripted collection, arrived at independently — script or simulate what is easy, spend humans and real hardware on what is hard. When two arguments from different directions land on the same split, it is probably the right one.

Takeaway
Simulation's leverage is real and large: 4,096 environments at 60 Hz is 245,760 steps/s, about 170 robot-days per wall-clock hour — plus perfect labels, free resets, free rewards, and counterfactual replay, which is the one thing hardware can never offer. But its fidelity is inversely ordered against usefulness: exact for kinematics and free-space motion, poor for friction, actuator dynamics, deformables, and the contact solver — i.e. poor exactly where tasks are decided. So calibrate, then randomise: identification does not just fix the centre, it makes the optimal width narrower and better. Measure, fit, learn an actuator residual, then randomise narrowly around calibrated values. A timid sim-trained policy is a symptom of an uncalibrated centre. And split by the fidelity table: simulate free space, spend real hours on contact.

Where this points next

The list of what simulation provides included one item we have not used: a computable reward. Everything so far has been imitation, because reward-free data is what humans produce. With a simulator, reward-driven learning becomes affordable — thousands of parallel environments, free resets, no hardware risk. Lesson 10 asks where reinforcement learning actually belongs in this stack, and the sample arithmetic gives a sharper answer than the usual arguments do.
