Capstone: spec a training programme

One brief, all thirteen constraints, real numbers. The exercise produces a plan — and it also produces a surprise that reorders the plan: the largest line item is not the training, not the collection, and not the GPUs.

Where we are

1 · The brief

Specification
A single-arm manipulator with a parallel gripper must perform 40 tabletop tasks in a light-industrial kitchen, instructed in natural language, in scenes it has not seen. About a quarter of the tasks involve a connector, lid, or fastener with ~2 mm clearance. Hardware: two scene cameras, a wrist camera, joint encoders, and a wrist force-torque sensor. Control at 50 Hz. Team: 4 operators, 2 robots, 6 months, real budget.

2 · Fix the hardware and the action space first (lesson 01, lesson 08)

Two checks before anything is trained, both pure arithmetic, both irreversible if wrong.

Action resolution. The task range must cover reaching, so ±300 mm. At 8 bits that is 600/256 = 2.3 mm per bin against a 2 mm clearance — fails. Options: 10 bits (0.59 mm, fine), or a continuous action head. Given a quarter of the tasks are fine manipulation, take the continuous head — this also buys lesson 03's multimodality handling, so one decision serves two purposes.

decisionContinuous flow-matching action head over end-effector delta pose, plus a separate categorical gripper head (lesson 01 §4). Per-embodiment normalisation stored with the checkpoint.

Contact. Vision cannot resolve 2 mm at any affordable tokenization (track 1 lesson 03), and force is categorically invisible (lesson 07). But we have a wrist force-torque sensor, and lesson 08's arithmetic says compliance with a 4 mm chamfer takes insertion from erf(0.47) ≈ 0.50 to about 0.88 at 3 mm positioning error.

decisionCompliant (impedance) control during contact phases with commanded stiffness as an action dimension. Add chamfers to every fixture in the cell — a mechanical change that buys more than months of model work. Record joint torque and wrist force on every episode, summarised over each control interval rather than downsampled.

3 · Fix the architecture from the latency budget (lesson 11)

A 3B trunk gives about 300 ms per pass, so fVLM ≈ 3.3 Hz. The controller needs

k ≥ 50 Hz × 0.30 s = 15 steps

and lesson 03's upper bound comes from the disturbance rate. A kitchen with people moving nearby is not a quiet cell — take λ ≈ 1 per second, which puts k* in the tens of steps. The interval [15, k*] is non-empty, so a hierarchy works.

decisionChunk k = 40 (800 ms), re-planned every 10 steps with blended overlap — satisfying the 15-step architectural floor with margin while keeping reaction latency at 200 ms. Proprioception and summarised force go to the fast expert, not the trunk.

4 · The data programme (lessons 04, 05, 06, 07, 09)

Throughput, from lesson 04. Episodes average 45 s of manipulation plus 15 s of automated reset — automation being the cheapest gain available:

3600 / 60 = 60 episodes per operator-hour  ·  4 operators × 6 h/day × 120 days = 2,880 operator-hours ⟹ ≈ 173,000 episodes at ~$30/h ⟹ ≈ $86,000 in collection labour

That is a little over twice DROID's trajectory count, from four people in six months — which is only possible because the tasks are narrow and the reset is automated. Now allocate it, per lesson 05's phase order:

Months 1–2 · breadth demonstrations, 40 tasks × ~1,000 each40,000 episodes
Months 1–6 · scripted collection for reach / transport / place segmentsfree, unbounded
Months 2–6 · on-policy corrections against the deployed checkpoint~60,000 episodes
Months 1–6 · play data for coverage, goals labeled retroactively~25,000 episodes
Held back for evaluation — see §6, and it is not enough~48,000 episodes

Note what is free and what is not. Pooled robot data, human video, and scripted collection cost nothing and supply breadth, semantics, and the easy segments. Every dollar of human time goes to the two things nothing else provides: contact-rich demonstrations and on-policy corrections.

5 · The training schedule (lesson 12)

  1. Inherit a 3B vision-language trunk. No training. Days: 0.
  2. Pretrain the action head on Open X-Embodiment plus our early breadth data, co-training 25% web vision-language. A few thousand GPU-hours, ~$10,000.
  3. Post-train on our body and tasks plus calibrated simulation for contact, still co-training 15% web VL. Hundreds of GPU-hours per iteration.
  4. Correction loop, weekly: re-fit the 300M expert only, on a rolling window of recent corrections (lesson 05's ρj depreciation is why the window rolls and the cadence is weekly). Tens of GPU-hours per iteration — deliberately cheap, because it runs twenty times.
  5. Language probe at every boundary. Minutes. Non-negotiable.

Total compute: on the order of $20,000–40,000. Against $86,000 of collection labour, and — as we are about to see — against an evaluation bill larger than either.

6 · Now price the evaluation, and watch the plan invert

Lesson 13's arithmetic. To detect a 10-point improvement at 70% baseline needs about 330 trials per arm. We have 40 tasks and want to compare each new checkpoint against the previous one:

40 tasks × 330 trials × 2 arms = 26,400 trials per comparison at ~1 minute each ⟹ 440 robot-hours ⟹ ~11 robot-weeks per comparison

We planned twenty weekly correction iterations. Twenty comparisons at eleven weeks each is 220 robot-weeks — over four years — for a six-month programme. The evaluation is 10× more expensive than the training and the collection combined, and it is not close.

binding constraintEvaluation, not training. Every other line item fits comfortably in the budget and the calendar. This one exceeds it by an order of magnitude, and no amount of GPU spend or operator hiring touches it.

So the plan must be rebuilt around lesson 13's five moves, and this is where they stop being good practice and become structural necessity:

  1. Continuous metrics. Completion time and peak contact force instead of a success bit — the same trials resolve far smaller effects. This alone can cut the required n several-fold.
  2. Paired scenes. Both checkpoints from identical initial configurations, removing the dominant between-scene variance.
  3. Aggregate claims only, pre-registered. 20 trials × 40 tasks = 800 trials has real power against a mean effect. Per-task claims are abandoned by decision, in advance, in writing.
  4. Simulation and world-model screening. Twenty candidates down to two in simulation; real trials only for the final pair. And per track 1 lesson 13, screening chooses which two to test, never the winner.
  5. Weekly smoke test, monthly gate. 200 paired trials weekly for regression detection; the full 800-trial aggregate comparison monthly. Six gates in six months, not twenty.

Rebuilt: roughly 10 robot-weeks of evaluation total, which fits. The programme survives because the measurement protocol was designed, not because anything got cheaper.

8 · What both seats have in common

Track 1 costed a world model and found the grounded corpus capped controllability. This track costed a robot programme and found evaluation binding, with every human hour going to contact demonstrations and on-policy corrections. Different seats, and the same conclusion: the constraint is never the model. It is the data — how much of which kind exists, what it costs, and how fast it goes stale.

That question has been implicit in every lesson of both tracks and answered fully in neither. It is the third track's entire subject: what embodied training actually eats, ranked by volume and ranked by value, and why those two rankings are almost exactly inverted.

Takeaway
Fix the irreversible things first: an 8-bit head over ±300 mm gives 2.3 mm against a 2 mm clearance, so go continuous; vision cannot see contact, so record force and put chamfers in the cell. Derive the chunk from latency — k ≥ 50 × 0.30 = 15, ship 40 with re-planning every 10. Collection: four operators over six months buys ~173,000 episodes for ~$86,000, with every human hour spent on contact and corrections while pooled data, human video and scripted segments come free. Compute is $20–40k. Then the surprise: honest per-task evaluation of twenty checkpoints is 220 robot-weeks against a 26-week programme — 10× more expensive than training and collection combined. The programme only closes because the measurement protocol is designed: continuous metrics, paired scenes, pre-registered aggregate claims, simulation screening, weekly smoke plus monthly gate.

Where this points next

Track 3 takes the ledger both seats kept running into. It answers, with arithmetic rather than intuition, what embodied training consumes the most — and shows that the honest answer requires two rankings that disagree, because the corpus that dominates by volume is nearly worthless per token, and the data that decides outcomes exists in quantities four orders of magnitude smaller.
