Where demonstrations come from

Demonstrations are not scraped, downloaded, or generated. A person moves a robot, in real time, once per episode. That single fact — that the data rate is bounded by human hours — is the constraint from which the shape of this entire field follows, and it is worth computing exactly rather than gesturing at.

Where we are
Lesson 03's chunked generative policy needs a great many coherent demonstrations. Lesson 02 said more of them only reduces the coefficient ε. Before deciding how much to buy, we need the price — and the price has a structure that turns out to make one of the alternatives obviously better.

1 · The collection modalities, and what each costs in operator attention

Picture where the data physically comes from. A person sits at a rig for six hours. They move the robot through a task, it takes forty seconds, then they spend twenty more putting the mug and the tray back where they started so they can do it again. That is the machine that produces robot data: a human, in real time, one episode at a time, a third of it spent tidying up. Every number in this lesson follows from that image.

Two of those rows deserve emphasis because they are systematically undervalued.

Scripted collection has unbounded throughput and gives force data, and it is dismissed because "it only covers what you can script." That objection is weaker than it sounds: for the sub-behaviours that are scriptable — reach, transport, place with known poses — scripted data is free, perfectly labeled, and arbitrarily diverse. Spend the human hours only on the parts a script genuinely cannot do, which is usually just the contact-rich segment. This decomposition is the single largest available efficiency gain in demonstration collection and most teams skip it.

Play data — an operator manipulating objects with no task in mind, with goals labeled retroactively by sampling any later state as the goal — yields many goal-conditioned training pairs per hour, because every pair of timesteps in the stream is a valid (start, goal) example. It cannot target a specific task, so it complements rather than replaces task demonstrations. It is a coverage buy, and coverage is what lesson 05 will show you cannot get any other cheap way.

2 · The arithmetic

One operator, one robot. An episode takes τep seconds of manipulation plus τreset seconds to put the scene back — and the reset is not a rounding error, it is often most of the cycle.

episodes per hour = 3600 / (τep + τreset)

At 40 seconds of manipulation and 20 seconds of reset that is 3600/60 = 60 episodes per hour of pure collection. Sustained productive collection runs perhaps 6 hours a day, so one operator delivers roughly 360 episodes a day — and note the second-order fact that follows: only 40/60 = 67% of the collected time is manipulation. A third of your budget buys resets. Automating scene reset is therefore worth up to a 50% throughput gain and is pure engineering with no research risk.

Now scale to a target. Suppose you want 100,000 episodes:

100,000 / 60 ≈ 1,670 operator-hours ≈ 280 operator-days ≈ a bit over one person-year of nothing but collection

at, say, $30 per loaded operator-hour, about $50,000 in labour alone — before robot capital, maintenance, scene authoring, quality control, or the engineering time to run the operation. And DROID, for calibration, is 76,000 trajectories ≈ 350 hours of interaction, gathered over 12 months by 50 collectors with 18 robots across 13 institutions. That is what 76k costs in practice: not one person-year, but a coordinated multi-institution programme.

3 · The three quality traps, each of which silently wastes a fraction of the spend

  1. Near-duplicates. An operator repeating one task 500 times produces 500 highly correlated episodes. As information they are worth far less than 500 — the model sees essentially one trajectory with jitter. Measure it: cluster trajectories and report effective episode count, not raw count. A dataset with 100k episodes and 800 clusters is an 800-episode dataset with good error bars.
  2. Failures with no label. Roughly 5–20% of episodes fail. Discarding them throws away the most informative frames you own — the ones showing what going wrong looks like. Keeping them unlabeled teaches the policy to reproduce failure. So label the outcome, always; even a single success/failure bit per episode makes them usable as negatives, as reward-model data, and as the recovery data lesson 02's escape 2 wanted.
  3. Operator drift. A person gets faster and sloppier over a session, and different people have different styles. This is exactly lesson 03's multimodality — fine, keep it — but it must be recorded. Store an operator ID and a timestamp on every episode. Without them you cannot diagnose "the policy learned operator 3's bad habit," and you cannot hold out an operator to test whether the policy generalises across styles, which is one of the cheapest and most revealing evaluations available.
The metadata rule
Record more than you think you need, at collection time, because you cannot go back. Operator ID, timestamp, scene ID, object instance IDs, outcome label, intervention flags, calibration, and full clock synchronisation across every stream. synthetic_vision/04 derives what a proper provenance and synchronisation contract looks like — adopt it for real collection, where unlike in simulation the information is unrecoverable once the session ends.

Six rows spanning about ten orders of magnitude, sorted almost exactly inversely to how directly each one teaches a robot to act. The bottom row is the most useful data in the table and the smallest by a factor of ten thousand.

That is the field's actual situation, and it forces the rest of this track. If the direct route is rate-limited by human hours, then you must either make each hour worth more (lesson 05: collect at states that matter), reuse other bodies' hours (lesson 06), reuse hours from bodies that are not robots at all (lesson 07), or manufacture hours (lesson 09). Those are not four independent tricks. They are the four available responses to this table.

Takeaway
Demonstration supply is bounded by human hours: 3600/(τep+τreset) episodes per operator-hour, which at 40 s + 20 s is 60 — so 100k episodes is ~1,670 operator-hours, over a person-year, ~$50k of labour alone, and DROID's 76k needed 13 institutions over 12 months. A third of the cycle is reset, so automating it is a ~50% gain with no research risk. Prefer scripted collection for scriptable segments and play data for coverage, spending humans only on contact. Then guard the three quality traps — near-duplicates (report effective, not raw, episode count), unlabeled failures, and unrecorded operator identity — because none can be repaired later.

Where this points next

We now know demonstrations are expensive and rate-limited. Lesson 02 told us they only reduce the coefficient ε, never the exponent. So a fixed budget spent entirely on demonstrations is spending on the wrong term. Lesson 05 splits the budget between more demonstrations and a different kind of data — corrections gathered at the states the policy itself visits — and finds the optimum split, which is usually not where teams put it.

Interview prompts

  • Compute episodes per operator-hour at 40 s manipulation and 20 s reset, and what fraction of time is productive. 3600/60 = 60 episodes per hour, of which only 40/60 = 67% is manipulation — a third of the budget buys resets. (§2)
  • What does 100,000 episodes actually cost? About 1,670 operator-hours, i.e. over a person-year of pure collection and roughly $50k in labour before robots, resets, scene authoring, or QC. (§2)
  • Why is scripted collection undervalued? It has unbounded throughput and yields force data, and although it only covers scriptable segments, decomposing a task lets humans be spent solely on the contact-rich part. (§1)
  • How does play data produce many training pairs per hour? Goals are labeled retroactively, so every pair of timesteps in the stream is a valid (start, goal) example — it buys coverage rather than task-specific competence. (§1)
  • Why report effective rather than raw episode count? Repeated near-identical episodes are highly correlated, so 100k episodes across 800 clusters carries roughly the information of 800 trajectories. (§3)
  • What should you do with failed episodes, and why? Label the outcome and keep them: unlabeled they teach failure, discarded they waste the most informative frames, labeled they serve as negatives, reward-model data and recovery data. (§3)
  • Why record operator ID on every episode? To diagnose learned per-operator habits and to hold out an operator as a cheap, revealing test of cross-style generalisation. (§3)
  • Given the ten-orders-of-magnitude data table, name the four structural responses. Make each hour worth more (on-policy corrections), reuse other robots' hours (cross-embodiment), reuse non-robot hours (human video), and manufacture hours (simulation). (§4)
