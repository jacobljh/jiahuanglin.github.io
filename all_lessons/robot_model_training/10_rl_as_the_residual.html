RL as the residual

Reinforcement learning is the only method here that can exceed the demonstrator, and it is the only one whose sample requirement is measured in millions. Reconciling those two facts does not need a philosophical position — it needs one arithmetic comparison, and the comparison places RL precisely.

Where we are
Lesson 09's simulator supplies the two things imitation never had: a computable reward and a free reset. So reward-driven learning is now affordable — in simulation. The reinforcement_learning track owns the algorithms in full (88 lessons: policy gradients, PPO, offline RL, CQL, IRL). This lesson answers only the placement question: given a fixed budget and an imitation prior, where does RL pay?

1 · What imitation structurally cannot do

Three ceilings, and they are the honest case for RL.

1 · the demonstrator's ceiling
You cannot exceed the data
Cloning targets the demonstrator's actions, so the demonstrator's competence is the ceiling. If a teleoperator inserts connectors at 85%, cloning converges to about 85%.
2 · no notion of better
All demonstrations are equal
A loss on actions has no term for outcome. A slow clumsy success and a fast clean one are equally good targets, so the policy has no gradient toward better.
3 · failures are unusable
Without reward, a failure is noise
Lesson 04's 5–20% failed episodes cannot be used as negatives by a pure imitation objective — you can only discard them.

A reward fixes all three at once, because it introduces an ordering over outcomes. That is genuinely what imitation lacks, and no amount of demonstration data supplies it.

2 · The arithmetic that places it

Now the cost. RL from scratch on a manipulation task needs on the order of 10⁶–10⁸ environment steps. Price that on real hardware at 50 Hz:

10⁷ steps / 50 Hz = 200,000 seconds ≈ 2,300 hours of continuous robot operation

Which is not 2,300 hours. Real robots need resets, they overheat, they break, and someone must supervise a policy that is by construction exploring. Realistically call it a year of a dedicated cell, during which the hardware is repeatedly damaged by a policy whose entire job early in training is to try things that do not work.

Now price the same 10⁷ steps in lesson 09's simulator: 245,760 steps per second means 41 seconds. Seven orders of magnitude, and the conclusion is immediate and unambiguous:

Where RL runs
Reinforcement learning happens in simulation, or in a learned world model, or on a small residual — essentially never as from-scratch training on real hardware. This is not a preference. It is a factor of 10⁷ in wall-clock time and an unbounded factor in hardware cost.

And there is a third venue, which is where these three tracks close their loop. Lesson 09's simulator is a hand-written model of the world. Track 1 trained a learned one. Rolling out a policy inside a learned world model is RL with the sample cost of inference — no hardware, no engine authoring, and the dynamics learned from real data rather than modelled by hand. world_models/08 derives that mechanism and its central hazard: the policy will exploit the model's errors, and the gap scales as δ·√(2 ln K). Which is exactly why track 1's lesson 13 spent a lesson on measuring δ honestly — the world model is the RL environment, so its error budget is the policy's exploitation budget.

3 · The three roles RL actually plays

  1. Fine-tune an imitation prior. The dominant pattern. BC gives a policy that already reaches, grasps, and roughly succeeds — so RL starts from a decent point and only has to climb, which reduces the sample requirement by orders of magnitude because the exploration problem is nearly gone. It also breaks the demonstrator's ceiling, which is the whole point.
  2. Train a residual policy. Keep the BC policy fixed and learn a small additive correction: a = πBC(o) + ΔRL(o). The correction is low-dimensional and bounded, so it is safe to learn on real hardware, and it is the natural home for exactly the contact-phase adjustments lesson 08 cared about.
  3. Train from scratch in simulation for locomotion-like tasks. Where the reward is dense, the dynamics are well-modelled (rigid bodies, no manipulation of deformables), and demonstrations are hard to give, from-scratch RL in sim is genuinely the best method — locomotion is the canonical case, and lesson 09's fidelity table explains why: it lives in the well-modelled rows.

Drag the affordable-steps line from 10⁵ to 10⁸ — the difference between a real robot cell and a simulator — and watch the red curve climb past the amber prior at around 107.5 steps. It never catches the green fine-tune — starting from a prior stays ahead throughout — but that one crossing is where RL stops being a waste of money and starts being worth running, and nothing about the algorithm changed across it.

The shape to internalise: at real-hardware budgets (around 10⁵ steps — a few robot-days) from-scratch RL is below the BC prior, so it is strictly a waste. The fine-tune is above it. Only at simulation-scale budgets does from-scratch catch up. So the decision rule is not about algorithms at all: if your budget is measured in robot-days, RL must start from a prior; if it is measured in simulator-seconds, from-scratch is on the table.

4 · The reward problem, which does not go away

RL needs a reward, and specifying one is where these projects actually fail — not in the optimiser.

  1. Sparse reward is honest and unlearnable. "1 if the connector is seated" is exactly what you want and gives no gradient until the policy succeeds by accident, which it will not. Correct, useless.
  2. Shaped reward is learnable and gameable. Add terms for approaching, aligning, and applying force, and the policy optimises the terms rather than the task — hovering near the socket to farm the alignment bonus. The RL track's reward-shaping lesson works through this failure in detail.
  3. Demonstrations make sparse rewards workable. This is the cleanest resolution and it is why the prior matters twice: a BC policy already succeeds sometimes, so a sparse reward does fire, and the optimiser has something to work with. The prior solves the exploration problem, which is what made sparse reward unlearnable in the first place.
  4. Or learn the reward. Inverse RL infers it from demonstrations, and a learned success classifier is the cheap practical version. Both introduce a model that can be exploited — you have converted a specification problem into a robustness problem, which is progress but not a solution. The IRL lesson covers the machinery.
The thing that is genuinely free, and usually ignored
Lesson 04 told you to label every episode's outcome with a single success bit. That bit is a sparse reward you already own, on data you already collected, at zero additional cost — and it turns your entire demonstration corpus into offline RL data. Given a prior good enough to make it fire, that is the cheapest route to exceeding the demonstrator available, and it requires no new collection whatsoever. If you took lesson 04's advice, you can do this today.
Takeaway
Imitation has three structural ceilings — the demonstrator's competence, no ordering over outcomes, and failures unusable as negatives — and a reward fixes all three. But 10⁷ steps is 2,300 hours of continuous real-robot operation versus 41 seconds in a parallel simulator, so RL runs in simulation, in a learned world model, or as a small bounded residual — essentially never from scratch on hardware. Its three real roles are fine-tuning a prior (dominant, because the prior removes the exploration problem), a residual correction (safe on hardware, natural home for contact adjustments), and from-scratch in sim for well-modelled dynamics like locomotion. At robot-day budgets from-scratch RL sits below the BC prior. And the per-episode success bit you already collected is a free sparse reward that turns your demonstration corpus into offline RL data.

Where this points next

We now have five sources of training signal — demonstrations, corrections, pooled cross-embodiment data, human video, and simulated experience with reward — and they must all land on one model that emits actions at 50 Hz while reasoning about a language instruction. Those two requirements are in direct tension, since a model large enough to understand "put the red one away" cannot run at 50 Hz. Lesson 11 shows how the tension is resolved, and why the resolution makes the chunk length of lesson 03 a hard architectural constraint rather than a tuning choice.
