Fix the distribution, not the loss

A thousand new demonstrations reduce ε. A hundred corrections gathered where the policy actually goes change the exponent. Those are not comparable purchases, and once you can see the difference the budget question has a clear answer — usually a different one than teams give.

1 · The idea, and why it is the only clean escape

The fix is almost insultingly direct, and it inverts what a data-collection session looks like. Instead of recording an expert doing the task well, you let the policy drive — badly — and stand next to it. When it drifts, you take the controls and show it the way out of the mess it just made. What you record is not a good demonstration. It is a rescue, from a situation only this policy would ever have got into, and that is precisely what makes it worth more.

Lesson 02's derivation had one load-bearing step: an error takes the policy somewhere the training data does not describe. Remove that step and the quadratic collapses. And you remove it by the most direct means available — collect data where the policy goes.

The loop is:

  1. Roll out the current policy on the real task. It will drift, hesitate, and do strange things. That is not a failed data-collection session; it is the session.
  2. Have the expert label the correct action at the states the policy reached. Not a fresh demonstration from a clean start — the correct action from here, from the mildly-wrong configuration the policy actually produced.
  3. Add those pairs to the dataset and retrain. Now the training distribution includes the policy's own visitation, so an error at deployment lands somewhere with data.
  4. Repeat. The new policy visits new states; iterate until the distributions stop moving.

This is DAgger, and the theory is the mirror of lesson 02: training on the learner's own induced distribution restores O(εT) from O(εT²). The factor recovered is T/2, which for a 200-step episode is a hundredfold.

Why a correction is worth so much more than a demonstration
A demonstration is a sample from d(π*), and the policy already fits that region well — you are adding data where the model is already right. A correction is a sample from d(πθ) at a point where the policy was wrong. The information content per sample is not comparable: one confirms, the other corrects. This is the same principle as active learning, and here it is not merely more efficient, it changes the asymptotic form of the error.

2 · The operational problem, and the variants that solve it

Plain DAgger asks a human to label the correct action for every state in an off-policy rollout — thousands of frames of "what should the arm have done here?" That is nearly impossible to do accurately, because a human cannot introspect a joint-velocity command for an arbitrary configuration they are not physically in. So the practical variants change how the correction is provided:

HG-DAgger
Human takes over
The operator watches the policy run and grabs control when it goes wrong. Only the intervention segments are labeled, and they are labeled by doing, which humans are good at. The dominant practical form.
sticky autonomy
Shared control
The policy drives; the operator nudges. The difference between the policy's action and the executed action is the correction signal, available continuously without an explicit takeover.
reset-to-state
Demonstrate from here
Log states where the policy failed, drive the robot back to one of them, and record a full demonstration from that state. Slow but exact, and it gives you the recovery data lesson 02's escape 2 asked for.

All three share the property that matters: the human's effort concentrates on the states where the policy is wrong. And they share the same cost structure — an operator supervising a live policy, which is roughly the same hourly rate as demonstrating, but with a very different yield per hour.

The safety constraint that shapes all of this
Running a partly-broken policy on real hardware to collect its mistakes means deliberately operating in the regime where it fails. On a robot with enough authority to damage itself or its surroundings, that requires real limits: force and velocity caps, workspace bounds, a dead-man switch, and compliant control so contact is absorbed rather than transmitted. The RL track's safe-exploration treatment covers the mechanics. The relevant point here is that the safety layer is what makes on-policy collection possible at all, so it is a prerequisite, not a polish item.

3 · So how should the budget split?

Model it. A fixed budget of operator-hours, a share φ spent on corrections and 1−φ on demonstrations. Demonstrations reduce the per-step error along a learning curve; corrections buy coverage of the policy's visitation distribution:

ε(N) = ε0 · (N0/(N0+N))β   ·   cov(M) = 1 − e−M/M0

and the expected failure interpolates between the quadratic and linear regimes according to how much of the visitation distribution is covered:

failure ≈ (1 − cov) · ε(N)·T²/2  +  cov · ε(N)·T

Both terms fall with more demonstrations, but only the first term is the quadratic — so corrections act on the term that dominates. The minimum over φ is an interior point, and the widget locates it.

Two experiments are worth running. Drop the total budget to its minimum: the optimum shifts toward demonstrations, because with too little data the policy is not yet good enough for its own rollouts to be informative — corrections of a policy that fails immediately are just demonstrations with extra steps. Then raise the horizon to 500: the optimum shifts hard toward corrections, because the quadratic term now dominates everything.

The resulting phase order
Breadth first, then corrections. Early on, buy demonstrations — you need a policy competent enough to generate meaningful states. Once it can attempt the task end to end, switch the marginal hour to corrections and keep it there. Teams routinely get this wrong in one specific way: they keep buying demonstrations long past the crossover, because demonstration collection is a comfortable, parallelisable, well-understood operation and correction collection requires a working policy, a safety layer, and a human watching a robot misbehave.

4 · And this is why the data goes stale

One consequence deserves stating plainly because it changes how you plan. Corrections are collected against a specific checkpoint. Retrain, and the policy visits different states — so yesterday's corrections describe a distribution that no longer exists. Their value decays as the policy moves.

Model the decay with a per-generation retention factor ρ — the overlap between consecutive checkpoints' visitation distributions. Corrections collected j generations ago are worth roughly ρj of their original value:

effective value ≈ Σj Mj · ρj

At ρ = 0.7, corrections from three generations back are worth about a third of fresh ones. Which yields a real operational rule: the retraining cadence and the collection cadence must be matched. Collect for six months and retrain once, and most of what you gathered has depreciated. Retrain weekly against a rolling window of recent corrections and you keep the value. Track 3 lesson 09 develops this as the sharpest fact in the entire data ledger — on-policy data is the only asset in this field that has a shelf life.

Takeaway
A demonstration adds data where the policy is already right; a correction adds data where it was wrong — and because it puts the training distribution on top of the visitation distribution, it converts lesson 02's O(εT²) back into O(εT), recovering a factor of T/2. Practically it must be HG-DAgger, sticky autonomy, or reset-to-state, because humans can correct by doing and not by labelling arbitrary frames — and all of it presupposes a safety layer. The budget optimum is interior and moves with horizon and budget, giving the phase order breadth first, then corrections. Finally, corrections depreciate as ρj per retraining generation, so collection and retraining cadence must be matched.

Where this points next

Corrections are the most valuable data per hour available — and they are specific to one policy on one body, which makes them the least shareable. Meanwhile there are a million-plus trajectories of other people's robot data sitting in Open X-Embodiment.
