Evaluation you can afford

A real-robot trial takes a minute, needs a human to reset the scene, and returns one bit. That is the whole measurement instrument, and the binding constraint on robot learning turns out to be not the training but the arithmetic of what twenty bits can resolve — which is much less than the field routinely claims.

What is specific here is that the measurement is physically expensive, so statistical power is the binding constraint rather than a footnote.

1 · Twenty trials, and what they tell you

Standard practice is 10–50 trials per task per method, because a trial is about a minute of robot time plus a human reset. So take 20 trials with 16 successes and ask what that 80% means. The Wilson score interval is the right tool at this sample size:

centre = (p̂ + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n),    half-width = z/(1+z²/n) · √( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n² )

At n=20, p̂=0.80, z=1.96, that gives roughly a 95% interval of 58% to 92% — a width of about 34 points. Your headline "80%" is a claim compatible with the true rate being 58% or 92%. Those are completely different systems.

Now the comparison, which is worse. To detect a difference between two arms you need the minimum detectable effect at reasonable power:

MDE ≈ (zα/2 + zβ) · √( 2p(1−p) / n )

At n=20 and p=0.7: √(2·0.21/20) = 0.145, times (1.96+0.84)=2.80, giving ≈ 0.41.

Forty-one points
With 20 trials per arm you can only reliably detect an improvement of about 41 percentage points. A genuine 10-point improvement — which would be an excellent result — is entirely invisible at this sample size. So a paper or dashboard reporting "our method: 76%, baseline: 68%, n=20" has not measured a difference. It has measured noise, and the sign of that noise is as likely to favour the baseline as the method.

And to detect 10 points, invert the formula:

n ≥ 2p(1−p)·((zα/2+zβ)/Δ)² = 2(0.21)(2.80/0.10)² ≈ 330 trials per arm

Which at a minute per trial plus reset is roughly 11 hours of robot-and-human time per arm per task. For 40 tasks and two arms, that is months of nothing but evaluation. This is the real reason robot papers report small-n results — not carelessness, but that honest measurement costs more than the training did.

Find where the red curve crosses the green line — that is the trial count at which a genuinely good ten-point improvement becomes visible at all. Then drag the baseline success rate toward 90% and watch the crossing move left: measurement gets cheaper as the system gets better, which is the opposite of when you need it.

The last readout is the flip side, and it is the cheapest useful thing on the panel. world_models/16's rule of three: zero failures in n trials bounds the failure rate at about 3/n with 95% confidence. Twenty clean trials means the failure rate is below roughly 15% — nothing stronger. To claim below 1% you need about 300 consecutive successes. For any safety-relevant deployment claim, that arithmetic is the whole conversation.

2 · What to do instead of buying trials

Five moves, in ascending cost, and the first three are nearly free.

  1. Report a distribution, not a bit. Success is one bit per trial; a trial can yield much more. Log time-to-completion, path length, peak contact force, number of retries, and the failure stage. Continuous measures have far more statistical power than a binary, so 20 trials measuring completion time can resolve differences that 20 trials measuring success cannot. This is the single highest-return change and it costs nothing but logging.
  2. Pair the arms. Run both methods from the same initial scene configurations rather than independently randomised ones. A paired comparison removes between-scene variance, which typically dominates, and can cut the required n by a factor of two to four for free.
  3. Rank many, then confirm few. Use simulation or a learned world model to screen twenty candidates down to two, then spend the real trials only on the final comparison. Track 1's warning applies exactly — the screening model has error δ and selecting the best of K inflates by δ√(2 ln K), so screening tells you which two to test, never which one won.
  4. Aggregate across tasks with a pre-registered protocol. Twenty trials on each of forty tasks is 800 trials, which has real power against a mean effect. It cannot support any per-task claim, so decide before you run whether your claim is per-task or aggregate — and then do not make the other one.
  5. Automate the reset. Lesson 04 said a third of collection is reset; here it is worse, because a human is idle between trials. Automating reset can move you from 30 trials a day to 300, which is a tenfold increase in resolvable effect size and is pure engineering.
The protocol trap, which is the same arithmetic again
Track 1 lesson 13's optimiser argument applies to you. If you try eight checkpoints, three prompt phrasings, and two guidance settings, then report the best, you have selected the maximum of 48 noisy estimates and inflated it by roughly δ√(2 ln 48) ≈ 2.8δ — which at n=20 is around 25 percentage points of pure selection artefact. Pre-register the checkpoint, the task list, the trial count, and the metric before the first trial. Every degree of freedom left open afterwards converts noise into apparent progress.

3 · Evaluate what fails, not just whether

A success rate is the least informative thing a trial can produce. Three cheap additions turn evaluation from a scoreboard into a diagnostic that tells you what to buy next.

Failure stage — approach / grasp / transport / insert / releasepoints at the responsible lesson
Failure step index — early or late in the episode (lesson 02)is compounding still binding?
Held-out operator — trained on operators 1–4, tested on 5style generalisation, free
Held-out scene and object instancethe generalisation you claim
Out-of-template instruction — lesson 12's language probecatches co-training collapse
Each row changes which lesson you go back tothat is the point

Concretely: failures concentrated at insertion send you to lesson 08 (instrument force, add a chamfer). Failures concentrated late in the episode send you to lesson 05 (buy corrections). Failures on a held-out operator send you to lesson 03 (the head is averaging styles). A single aggregate success rate sends you nowhere, which is why it is possible to iterate for months on a number that moves without ever knowing what moved it.

Takeaway
Real-robot evaluation is physically expensive, so power binds. At n=20 and p̂=0.8 the 95% Wilson interval is about 58–92%, and the minimum detectable effect is about 41 points — so "76% vs 68%, n=20" has measured nothing. Detecting a genuine 10-point gain needs about 330 trials per arm, roughly 11 hours per task per arm. So instead: log continuous measures rather than a bit (highest return, free), pair the arms on identical initial scenes, screen in simulation but confirm on hardware, pre-register aggregate-versus-per-task claims, and automate the reset. Zero failures in 20 trials bounds the failure rate only at about 15%, not zero. And record failure stage and step index, because those are what tell you which lesson to return to.
