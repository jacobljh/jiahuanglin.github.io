Learning from video of other bodies

Humans have been filmed doing exactly the tasks we want robots to do, for millions of hours, for free. The honest question is not whether that helps — it does — but which capabilities it transfers to, because the answer ranges from "almost completely" down to a hard, scale-independent zero.

Where we are
Lesson 06 pooled twenty-two robot bodies and hit two walls: no force channel, and no escape from the teleoperation rate limit. The corpus that escapes both is video of humans — Ego4D's 3,670 hours, and over a million hours of internet video. It has no action column, which track 1 lesson 06 showed how to manufacture. Here the difficulty is different: the actions belong to the wrong body.

1 · What is available, and the number that gets misquoted

Somewhere there is footage of a person picking up a bowl. You can see the bowl, the hand, the approach, the moment of contact, where on the rim the fingers land, and whether it worked. You cannot see one thing: how hard they squeezed. Hold that asymmetry, because it turns out to be the exact line between what this enormous free corpus can teach a robot and what it can never teach one, at any scale.

The third row is worth pausing on, because it is routinely cited wrongly. Ego-Exo4D's headline is 1,286 video hours, but that counts every synchronised camera; the egocentric hours — the ones with the camera on the actor, which is what makes the dataset special for us — total about 221. When you plan against these corpora, count the hours of the view you actually need, not the hours of video that exist.

Egocentric video earns its own category for a specific reason. When the camera is mounted on the actor, camera motion is body motion. Head and torso movement are directly observable, and the hands are in frame at close range doing the manipulation. That makes egocentric video the closest thing to an action-labeled corpus that nobody had to instrument — the action is partially recoverable from the ego-motion, which is exactly track 1 lesson 06's inverse-dynamics argument applied to a body.

2 · Transfer, capability by capability

This is the substance of the lesson. Ask, for each thing a policy must know, whether human video contains the answer at all.

capabilitytransferwhy
task semantics — what a task is, what its steps arevery highFully visible, and largely language-aligned through narration.
affordance — where on an object to acthighHumans grasp handles, press buttons, pull tabs. Where they touch is where it works.
subgoal sequence — what order things happen inhighVisible, and robust to the body doing it.
object dynamics — how things move when pushedmediumReal physics, genuinely observed. Held back only by resolution and occlusion.
kinematic trajectory — the path to followlow–mediumObservable, but a five-fingered hand's path is not a parallel gripper's. Gated by retargeting.
contact forces — how hard to squeeze, when it slippedzeroNot a property of the image. No resolution, no scale, no architecture recovers it.
The zero is the important row
Every other row improves with more hours. The force row does not, because the information is not in the medium — a camera does not measure newtons, so no amount of video contains them. This is a categorical limit, not a data-scale limit, and it means the frontier capability of manipulation (contact-rich assembly, wiping, deformables) receives nothing from the largest corpus available. Lesson 08 is the direct consequence.

A few thousand hours already gives you most of the available semantics and affordance; the millionth hour is buying trajectory refinement, and no hour is buying force.

3 · The retargeting problem

The trajectory row is gated, so let us be precise about the gate. A human hand has roughly 20+ degrees of freedom, opposable thumb, compliant skin, and tactile sensing across the palm. A parallel gripper has one degree of freedom and two rigid pads. Mapping between them is not a coordinate transform — it is a change of strategy.

Concretely: a human picks up a bowl by spreading fingers under its rim and cradling it. A parallel gripper cannot do that at all; it must pinch the rim, which requires a completely different approach direction and a completely different grip force. So the human trajectory is not a noisy version of the robot trajectory. It is a solution to a different problem.

Which yields the practical rule that makes human video usable: retarget the goal, not the motion.

extract this
Object-centric outcomes
"The bowl ends on the shelf, upright." A statement about objects, valid for any body. Then let the robot's own policy — trained on robot data — find its way there.
not this
Joint-space imitation
"Move the wrist along this arc." A statement about a hand you do not have, encoding a strategy your gripper cannot execute.

This reframing is what makes human video a goal and affordance source rather than a trajectory source, and it is why so many systems use it to pretrain the perception trunk and the goal representation while training the action head exclusively on robot data.

4 · The recipe that works, and the striking ratio inside it

V-JEPA 2 is the cleanest demonstration, and its numbers are the argument.

  1. Pretrain a predictive representation on internet video. Over a million hours, no actions, no rewards, self-supervised. The model learns how the visual world evolves.
  2. Add a small action-conditioned model on top. V-JEPA 2-AC is a 300M-parameter transformer with block-causal attention that autoregressively predicts the next frame's representation given an action and the previous states — trained on roughly 62 hours of DROID video with a 7-dimensional end-effector state.
  3. Plan with it, zero-shot. Image-goal pick-and-place, with no task-specific data, no task-specific training, and no rewards.
1,000,000 hours (free, no actions)  +  62 hours (expensive, actions)  ≈  a usable action-conditioned model

Read the leverage: the action-grounded stage is 0.006% of the data. The free corpus does the representational work; the tiny grounded corpus only has to attach the handle. That is the strategic template for this whole field — put the scale where data is free and the grounding where it is dear — and it is the same arbitrage track 1 lesson 06 priced with the inverse-dynamics exchange rate.

The evaluation trap this creates
A model pretrained on a million hours of internet video has seen an enormous amount of the world, including plausibly your evaluation scenes and objects. "Zero-shot" means no task-specific training; it does not mean the objects are novel. When you report generalisation, hold out object instances and scenes deliberately, and say which — otherwise the number measures corpus overlap rather than generalisation.
Takeaway
Human video transfers in a strict order: task semantics and affordance saturate within a few thousand hours, object dynamics and subgoals follow, kinematic trajectory is gated by retargeting, and contact force transfer is exactly zero at any scale because newtons are not in the medium. So retarget the goal, not the motion — a human's bowl-cradling strategy is a solution to a different problem than a parallel gripper's. Count the hours of the view you need (Ego-Exo4D is 1,286 total but only 221 egocentric). And copy the template: V-JEPA 2 puts >1M free hours under 62 grounded ones — 0.006% — so the scale sits where data is free and the grounding where it is dear.

Where this points next

One row of the transfer table is a hard zero, and it happens to be the row that decides whether an insertion seats, a wipe applies even pressure, or a cloth is grasped rather than dragged. No corpus of video, however large, contains it, and lesson 06 showed the robot pool does not either. Lesson 08 takes contact seriously: why cameras cannot see it, why the fix is partly mechanical rather than statistical, and why compliance can beat precision by a wide margin.
