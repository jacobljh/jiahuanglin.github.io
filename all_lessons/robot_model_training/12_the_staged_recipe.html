The staged recipe

Five data sources spanning six orders of magnitude, a pretrained trunk worth protecting, and a randomly initialised action head. The order in which you combine them is not a matter of taste — one wrong ordering destroys the capability that made the trunk worth using in the first place, silently.

Where we are
Lesson 11 gave the two-rate anatomy. Now we train it. Track 1 lesson 09 derived when to stage versus train jointly — conflict against forgetting, with replay dominating both. That analysis applies unchanged; this lesson instantiates it for the robot stack, where one particular forgetting failure is severe enough to deserve its own treatment.

1 · The stages, and what forces each boundary

Think of it as a funnel with something valuable at the wide end that is easy to lose. You start with a model that already knows what a sponge is, what "red" means, and that "put it away" implies a destination — knowledge that came from the whole web. Then you narrow: all robots, then your robot, then your tasks, then today's mistakes. Each narrowing makes it better at the thing in front of it, and each one can quietly cost you the general knowledge you started with.

  1. Inherit the trunk. Do not train the vision-language model. Take a pretrained one — PaliGemma, Llama-2 with a vision encoder — because the semantic and visual competence you need comes from web-scale image-text data you cannot reproduce with 350 hours of robot video. This stage is a download, and recognising that is the single largest saving in the recipe.
  2. Pretrain the action head on the pooled corpus. All embodiments, all tasks — Open X-Embodiment's 1M+ trajectories, the way OpenVLA used 970k episodes. Purpose: teach the head what actions look like at all, and let the trunk adapt to robot imagery. Forced before specialisation because a randomly initialised head fine-tuned on 300 hours overfits, while one pretrained on a million trajectories has a prior over plausible motion.
  3. Co-train with web vision-language data throughout. Not a stage — a constraint on stages 2 and 4. §2 explains why; skipping it is the failure this lesson is named for.
  4. Post-train on your embodiment and your tasks. Your own hundreds of hours, plus simulation for the contact-rich segments (lesson 09's split). Purpose: specialise the head to your body's units and your task distribution. Forced after pooling because pooling after specialising undoes the specialisation.
  5. Iterate corrections against the deployed policy. Lesson 05's loop, run continuously — and per lesson 11, only the small expert needs re-fitting, which is what makes weekly cadence affordable. Forced last, and never finished, because it is defined relative to whichever checkpoint is currently deployed.

Notice that only stages 2, 4 and 5 are training runs, and they descend in size by roughly an order of magnitude each: a million trajectories, then hundreds of hours, then hundreds of corrections. The recipe is a funnel from general to specific, and each narrowing costs you generality — which is exactly why stage 3 exists.

2 · Why co-training is not optional

Here is the failure, and it is worth stating as a concrete scenario because the numbers make it vivid. You take a 7B vision-language model that can identify objects, read text in images, and follow compositional instructions. You fine-tune it on 970k robot trajectories whose language annotations are 86 task templates — "pick up the sponge," "put the block in the bowl," repeated with variations.

What happens is not subtle. The model becomes excellent at those 86 templates and loses the ability to handle language outside them. Ask for "put the red one away, not the blue one" and it does something reasonable-looking and wrong. The compositional language capability that justified starting from a VLM has been overwritten by a narrow distribution, because nothing in the fine-tuning objective preserved it.

Why this is worse than ordinary forgetting
Two reasons. First, the capability lost is precisely the one you cannot rebuild — you would need web-scale image-text data, which is why you downloaded the trunk. Second, it does not show up in your metrics. Task success on your 86 templates goes up. Every dashboard improves. The loss is only visible on instructions you did not think to test, which is to say, on the generalisation you were buying.

The fix is mechanically simple: keep a fraction of the original web vision-language data in every fine-tuning batch. Some captioning, some visual question answering, some grounding — enough that the gradient never stops asking for the old capability. The cost is that fraction of your throughput, and the benefit is retaining a capability that cannot be repurchased.

Set co-training to 0 and drag the fine-tuning steps out to 100k. The violet bar climbs the whole way and the green bar collapses toward zero — and note that every dashboard you would actually be watching during that run is tracking the violet one. Then raise co-training to 20% and read the two numbers: task success falls from 0.94 to 0.89, and grounding settles at 0.20 instead of 0.02. Note what that means — the co-training share does not slow the forgetting, it sets the floor the forgetting stops at. Whatever fraction you keep is roughly the grounding you keep.

The product row is the one to optimise, because it is the closest proxy for what you actually want: a policy that succeeds and understands what it was asked. Optimising the task column alone drives co-training to zero and produces a very good single-task robot from a very expensive general model.

3 · Mixture per stage

Each stage wants different proportions, for reasons already derived. Track 1 lesson 10's discipline applies: a weight is a request, and dividing by availability tells you how many times you are re-reading the same data.

Two features of that table carry the reasoning. Web vision-language data never goes to zero — that is §2 as a mixture constraint. And corrections take the largest single share in stage 5 despite being the smallest corpus, because lesson 05 showed they act on the exponent while everything else acts on the coefficient. Then check availability: 40% of a stage-5 budget against a few hundred corrections means several epochs over them, so this stage must be short and repeated rather than long and single — which is also what lesson 05's depreciation required.

The language probe is the cheapest high-value item on that list and it is almost never run, because it feels irrelevant to a robotics project. It is the only thing that catches §2's failure before deployment, it takes minutes, and it uses a benchmark you did not have to build.

Takeaway
The recipe is a funnel: inherit the trunk (a download, not a training run), pretrain the action head on the pooled cross-embodiment corpus so it has a prior over plausible motion, post-train on your body and tasks plus sim for contact, then iterate corrections forever against the deployed checkpoint — re-fitting only the small expert, which is what makes weekly cadence affordable. Running through all of it: co-train with web vision-language data, because fine-tuning on a few dozen task templates overwrites compositional language understanding, that capability cannot be rebuilt without web-scale data, and the loss raises every metric you are watching. Never let the web share reach zero, keep corrections the largest stage-5 share, and run a language probe at every boundary.

Where this points next

The system is trained. Now the question that decides whether any of this was worth it: is the new checkpoint better than the old one? On a real robot that means running trials, and trials cost minutes each and require a human. Lesson 13 computes what a realistic trial budget can actually resolve, and the answer invalidates a large fraction of the comparisons people make.

Interview prompts

  • Why is the first stage a download rather than a training run? The semantic and visual competence required comes from web-scale image-text data that cannot be reproduced from a few hundred hours of robot video. (§1)
  • Why pretrain the action head on pooled data before specialising? A randomly initialised head fine-tuned on a few hundred hours overfits, whereas one pretrained on a million trajectories carries a prior over plausible motion. (§1)
  • Describe the co-training failure concretely. Fine-tuning a general VLM on a corpus with a few dozen language templates makes it excellent on those templates and unable to handle compositional instructions outside them. (§2)
  • Why is this forgetting worse than ordinary forgetting? The lost capability requires web-scale data to rebuild, and the loss raises every metric being tracked — task success on the templates improves — so it is invisible until deployment. (§2)
  • Why does the web-data share never reach zero in any stage? Because the gradient must never stop asking for the general capability; a zero share is the failure mode, not a limiting case. (§3)
  • Why do corrections take the largest stage-5 share despite the smallest corpus? They act on the quadratic exponent while the other sources act only on the coefficient ε. (§3)
  • Why must the correction stage be short and repeated rather than long and single? A large share over a few hundred corrections is several epochs, and corrections depreciate per retraining generation, so cadence must be frequent. (§3)
  • Name the cheapest high-value evaluation at each boundary, and why it is skipped. A language probe — captioning and VQA on held-out web data — which takes minutes and is the only thing catching co-training collapse, but feels irrelevant to a robotics project. (§4)
