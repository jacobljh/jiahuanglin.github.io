The action is the hard part

Perception gets the attention and the action space decides the project. It fixes the control rate you must hit, which datasets are reusable at all, how errors accumulate, and — through the least-examined choice in the stack, discretisation — whether the millimetres your task turns on can even be expressed as a command.

Where we are
Lesson 00 established that a policy authors its own test set, and that the object we train emits a chunk of future actions. Before training anything we have to say what one action is. This is the mirror of track 1 lesson 03: there the tokenizer's patch size set a resolution floor on what could be perceived; here the action encoding sets a floor on what can be commanded.

1 · Four action spaces, and what each commits you to

Stand at the arm and ask what you are actually going to send it. "Move two centimetres left" is one answer. "Set joint four to 1.31 radians" is another. "Apply 0.4 newton-metres to joint four" is a third. All three can produce the same motion, and choosing between them decides how fast your loop must run, whether anyone else's data is usable to you, and whether your policy controls the elbow at all.

The choice is not about elegance. Each option fixes a control rate, because each is a different distance from the physics.

The rate column has a clean explanation. Torque is the actual physical input, so the loop must run faster than the mechanical dynamics it is stabilising. Position commands delegate stabilisation to a servo already running at kHz, so your policy only has to update the setpoint. Each step up the abstraction ladder buys you a slower loop by handing the fast part to something else.

The last column decides your data strategy, and it is why the field converged where it did. End-effector delta pose is expressed in the task's frame, not the robot's, so "move 2 cm left and close" means the same thing on a Franka, a UR5, and a mobile manipulator. That makes cross-body pooling possible at all — lesson 06's foundation, and the reason Open X-Embodiment could pool 22 embodiments into one dataset.

The price of the convenient choice
Task-space actions hide the null space. A 7-DOF arm has infinitely many joint configurations achieving one end-effector pose, and the inverse-kinematics solver picks one — so your policy does not control the elbow. Fine until the elbow is about to hit the shelf. Whole-body and bimanual tasks are exactly where this bites, which is why bimanual systems tend to command joint positions and accept the loss of portability. There is no free abstraction here; there is a trade you should make on purpose.

2 · Units, frames, and normalisation: the boring bugs that cost weeks

Before any modelling, three conventions have to be nailed down, because every one of them is a silent failure.

  1. Absolute or relative? An absolute pose target is robust to accumulated drift but ties the policy to a fixed workspace origin — move the table and the policy is wrong everywhere. A delta is translation-invariant, so it generalises across placements, but errors integrate: a small constant bias becomes a large drift, which is the same compounding as lesson 00. Most systems use deltas and accept the bias risk. Check for the bias explicitly; it looks like a policy that slowly slides.
  2. Which frame? Base frame, end-effector frame, or camera frame — and the answer must be identical in training and deployment. A policy trained on base-frame deltas and deployed with tool-frame deltas will produce confident, smooth, entirely wrong motion. This bug is common, produces no error message, and looks like a model that "didn't learn."
  3. Normalised how? Action dimensions have wildly different scales: translation in centimetres, rotation in radians, gripper in {0,1}. Unnormalised, an L2 loss is dominated by whichever dimension happens to be numerically largest, and the gripper — one bit that decides task success — contributes almost nothing. Normalise per dimension using dataset statistics, and store those statistics with the checkpoint: a policy is only valid with the normaliser it was trained with, and mismatched normalisers are a leading cause of "the released model doesn't reproduce."

3 · Discretisation: the resolution floor

Now the choice that determines whether your task is even expressible. Text-token VLAs like RT-2 reuse the language head by binning each action dimension — typically into 256 bins across a fixed range. That is elegant, and it imposes an arithmetic limit:

resolution = 2 · range / 2bits

Put in real numbers. A translation range of ±100 mm at 8 bits (256 bins):

200 mm / 256 = 0.78 mm per bin

Fine for a 2 mm clearance. Now widen the range to ±500 mm, because you also want the arm to make large reaching motions with the same head:

1000 mm / 256 = 3.9 mm per bin — about 2× the clearance you need

The policy physically cannot command the motion that completes the task. It is not imprecise, it is unable, in exactly the sense of track 1's blur floor. And note the trap: the failure was introduced by broadening the range for an unrelated reason, so it appears as "insertion regressed when we added the reaching tasks" — a symptom that points nowhere near its cause.

Invert the formula for the bits you actually need:

bits ≥ log₂( 2 · range / clearance )   ⟹   for ±500 mm and 2 mm: log₂(500) ≈ 9 bits

Read the fourth metric too, because it is the constraint people meet second. One bin per control step at 50 Hz is the slowest motion you can command — a coarse grid cannot move slowly. At 3.9 mm and 50 Hz the minimum non-zero speed is about 0.2 m/s, which is far too fast for the final approach to a connector. A coarse action grid therefore breaks fine manipulation twice: it cannot place precisely, and it cannot creep.

Which is why continuous heads exist
π₀'s flow-matching action expert outputs continuous values, sidestepping the grid entirely — and it is a separate 300M-parameter module with its own weights rather than a reuse of the language head. That is the trade in full: discretisation gets you a free head and a resolution ceiling; a continuous head costs parameters and machinery and removes the ceiling. Lesson 03 gives the other reason to prefer a continuous generative head, which turns out to matter even more.

4 · The gripper is not a dimension, it is a mode

One special case worth calling out because it breaks the tidy story above. Continuous action dimensions are smooth; the gripper is a discrete decision with enormous, irreversible consequence. Regressing it as a continuous value produces a policy that hovers at 0.5 — half-closed, holding nothing, dropping the part — for exactly the reason lesson 03 will generalise: the average of "open" and "closed" is a state that accomplishes neither.

Treat it as what it is: a separate binary or categorical output with its own loss, weighted so that it is not drowned by the six continuous dimensions. This is a two-line fix and it routinely moves task success by tens of points, because gripper timing is the task in most manipulation.

Takeaway
The action space fixes your control rate (torque needs kHz because it is the physical input; position delegates stabilisation to a servo), and only end-effector delta pose is body-agnostic enough for cross-embodiment pooling — at the cost of surrendering the null space, which is why bimanual systems often go back to joint commands. Nail down absolute-versus-delta, the frame, and per-dimension normalisation stored with the checkpoint, since all three fail silently. Then compute the resolution floor: 2·range/2bits, which at ±500 mm and 8 bits is 3.9 mm against a 2 mm clearance — unable, not imprecise, and it also sets a minimum commandable speed. And give the gripper its own categorical head.

Where this points next

We can now say exactly what our model outputs. So take the obvious approach: collect demonstrations, and train the map from observation to action with supervised learning. It is the right first move and it has a specific, provable flaw — the one lesson 00 previewed. Lesson 02 derives the flaw properly, gets the O(εT²) scaling, and shows what it takes to recover a linear bound.
