Imitation, and the lie inside it

Behaviour cloning is supervised learning on (observation, action) pairs. Every tool applies, every intuition transfers, and one assumption is false — the one that says test inputs are drawn independently of the model. Correcting it turns a linear error bound into a quadratic one, and that single exponent is the reason robot learning is hard.

Where we are
Lesson 01 defined the output. Now train the map. Behaviour cloning is the right first move and this lesson is not an argument against it — it is an argument for knowing exactly what it costs, because the cost has a closed form and the form tells you which fix to buy.

1 · The objective, and the assumption hiding in the expectation

Watch a cloned policy run and the shape of the failure is always the same. The first second is uncanny — it looks exactly like the demonstrator. Then it is slightly off-centre. Being slightly off-centre is a situation the demonstrator never got into, so there is no data about it, so the next action is a guess, which puts it further off-centre. It does not break; it wanders, and it wanders faster the longer it runs. That escalation is not a bug in the network. It is a term in the objective, and we can write it down.

Behaviour cloning minimises the discrepancy between the policy's action and the expert's, over states drawn from the expert's trajectories:

minθ Es∼dπ*[ ℓ( πθ(s), π*(s) ) ]

Look at the subscript. The expectation is over dπ* — where the expert goes. That is unavoidable, because the expert generated the data and there is no other distribution available at training time. But at deployment the states arrive from dπθ — where your policy goes. So we minimise a loss under one distribution and pay a cost under another, and nothing in the training procedure measures the mismatch.

This is exactly the shape of track 1 lesson 04's exposure bias, and it is worth noting the parallel because the two are the same phenomenon in the two seats: train on real prefixes, deploy on self-made ones. What differs is the consequence, and the consequence here is worse.

2 · Derive the quadratic

Let ε be the probability that the policy's action differs from the expert's in a consequential way, measured under the expert's own distribution — the quantity training actually minimises. Follow one episode of length T.

At step 1 the policy is on the expert's distribution. It errs with probability ε. If it errs, it enters a state the expert never visited, and there the policy has no guarantee at all — it was never trained there, so assume the worst: it stays off-distribution and accrues cost for the rest of the episode.

Count the total cost by summing over the step at which the first departure occurs. A first departure at step t costs roughly (T − t) — the remaining steps — and occurs with probability about ε. So

E[cost] ≈ Σt=1T ε · (T − t) = ε · T(T−1)/2 ≈ ε T² / 2

Compare the bound you would have written if errors were independent samples, as in supervised learning: ε per step over T steps gives ε T. The gap is a factor of T/2, and it is not a loose constant. It is the formal statement of lesson 00's asymmetry: each error is charged not once but for every step that follows it.

What the numbers do
Take ε = 0.01 and T = 400 — two seconds of 200 Hz control, or eight seconds at 50 Hz. The linear intuition says expected cost 0.01 × 400 = 4 bad steps out of 400: a 1% problem. The correct bound says 0.01 × 400²/2 = 800, which saturates: failure is essentially certain. A validation accuracy of 99% on held-out demonstration frames is entirely compatible with a policy that never completes the task.

Read the horizon readout first, because it is the actionable form of the whole derivation. Setting εT²/2 = 1 and εT = 1 gives the longest episode each approach can finish: TBC = √(2/ε) against Ton-policy = 1/ε. At ε = 2% that is 10 steps versus 50 — cloning buys you a fifth of a second at 50 Hz, and training on your own distribution buys a second. Neither is a four-second task, which is why lessons 03 and 05 both exist.

Now do the experiment that reorders priorities, and note that the log axes make it visual. Halving ε — which is what "collect more data" and "train a bigger model" buy you — slides the red line down by 0.3 and leaves its slope at 2. Setting k = 20 slides it down by 1.3, again at slope 2. But switching to on-policy training changes the slope to 1, and a slope change beats any offset once the horizon is long enough. Coefficients shift the line; only structure bends it — which is why the two big empirical wins in modern imitation learning, action chunking and on-policy correction, are structural rather than a matter of scale.

3 · The three escapes, and which one each technique is

Read the derivation backwards to see what could break it. It needed three things: errors leave the training distribution, off-distribution behaviour is unbounded, and departures accumulate over T steps. Attack any one.

escape 1
Train on your own states
If the training distribution is the visitation distribution, an error no longer moves you somewhere untrained. This restores O(εT) and is what DAgger does — lesson 05. The strongest fix and the most operationally awkward.
escape 2
Make recovery trained behaviour
Off-distribution cost is unbounded only because nothing taught recovery. Deliberately include perturbed starts and corrections, and the "worst case" becomes a case with data. Cheap, underused, partially effective.
escape 3
Shorten the effective horizon
Reduce the number of independent decision points. Chunking does this by a factor of k; so does a hierarchy where a high-level policy emits few decisions. Lessons 03 and 11.

What is not on that list is instructive: nothing about model capacity, architecture, or dataset size. Those reduce ε, which is the coefficient. The quadratic is structural, so it survives any amount of scale — which is precisely why a field that could otherwise have waited for bigger models had to invent something else.

4 · What this means for how you read your own metrics

Three concrete consequences, all of them corrections to habits imported from supervised learning.

  1. Held-out action MSE is nearly uninformative. It estimates ε under dπ*, which is the wrong distribution, and the mapping from there to task success runs through T². Two checkpoints with the same validation loss can differ completely in success rate. Rank checkpoints by rollout success and nothing else.
  2. Report the horizon with every success number. "84% success" without an episode length is not a claim, because success falls superlinearly in T. A method that wins at T = 50 can lose badly at T = 400, and comparisons across papers with different horizons are not comparisons.
  3. Watch where in the episode failures occur. The derivation predicts failures concentrate in the later part of an episode, since departures accumulate. A histogram of failure step is a cheap and very direct test of whether compounding is your binding problem — if failures are uniform in time, something else is wrong and this lesson's fixes will not help you.
Takeaway
Behaviour cloning minimises loss under the expert's distribution dπ* and is evaluated under its own dπθ. Charging each departure for every remaining step gives Σ ε(T−t) ≈ εT²/2 against the supervised intuition's εT — a factor of T/2. At ε=1%, T=400 that is the difference between a 1% problem and certain failure, which is why 99% validation accuracy is compatible with a policy that never finishes. Only three things escape it: train on your own states (linear again), make recovery trained behaviour, or reduce decision points. Scale reduces ε, the coefficient — never the exponent.

Where this points next

The derivation assumed a single correct action per state, with ε the chance of missing it. That assumption is also false, and in a way that hurts before compounding even starts: two demonstrators route around the mug on opposite sides, both correct, and a regression head answers with their average, which drives straight into it. Lesson 03 shows that the fix for this — predict a distribution, and predict a whole chunk at once — is also the fix for the exponent, which is why one design change carried so much of the field's recent progress.
