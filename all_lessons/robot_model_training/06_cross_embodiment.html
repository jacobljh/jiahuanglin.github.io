One policy, many bodies

Your lab has three hundred hours. The community has a million trajectories across twenty-two different robots. Whether that helps you is a question with a real answer, and the answer has two halves that pull in opposite directions — which is why the design choice is where to split the model, not whether to pool.

Where we are
Lesson 05 produced the most valuable data per hour — corrections — and the least shareable, since they describe one policy on one body. Now we go the other way and ask what the most shareable data can buy. Open X-Embodiment exists precisely to answer this: 1M+ trajectories, 22 embodiments, pooled from 60 datasets across 34 labs.

1 · Why anything transfers at all

The naive expectation is that it should not. A Franka's joint limits, link lengths, dynamics, and gripper geometry differ from a UR5's. Nothing about one arm's commands is valid for the other. So decompose what a manipulation policy actually has to know:

What a mug looks like, and that it has a handlefully shared
That "put the red block on the plate" refers to those objectsfully shared
That grasping precedes lifting, which precedes transportingfully shared
Where on an object a stable grasp existsmostly shared — depends on gripper
Approach direction and standoff distancepartly shared
Joint commands realising that approachnot shared at all
Share of the parameters that carry the shared partthe vision-language trunk — nearly all of them

That last line is the whole argument. In a 7B model with a small action head, the overwhelming majority of parameters are doing perception and semantics — which is body-independent. The body-specific part is a thin final mapping. So pooling trains the large shared component on twenty-two robots' worth of data and only the thin head suffers from the mismatch.

And lesson 01 already supplied the mechanism that makes even the head partly shareable: end-effector delta pose is expressed in the task frame. "Move 2 cm toward the object and close" means the same thing on every arm that can reach. Open X-Embodiment normalises to such a space, which is precisely what makes the pooling possible; RT-1-X and RT-2-X are the demonstrations that it pays.

2 · What interferes

Now the opposite force, and it is not the one people expect. The problem is not that different robots look different — vision handles that. It is that the same observation maps to different correct actions on different bodies.

A gripper with 8 cm of stroke and one with 3 cm require different approach standoffs for the same mug. A robot with a 200 ms actuation delay needs to command earlier than one with 20 ms. A 5-DOF arm cannot achieve poses a 7-DOF arm can. So for a shared action head, these are contradictory labels on identical inputs — and a single head must average them, which is lesson 03's failure re-appearing across bodies instead of across operators.

Model the interference as growing with the number of pooled embodiments, saturating as the head is progressively forced toward a generic compromise:

interference(n) = c·(n−1) / (1 + c·(n−1))

while the benefit grows with the useful pooled hours, where a shared-structure fraction σ governs how much of another body's data counts for yours:

useful(n) = h + σ·(n·h − h)   ·   benefit = 1 − e−useful/h0

A saturating benefit times a saturating penalty gives a peak — a number of embodiments past which a shared head gets worse. That is a testable prediction, and it points at the fix.

Switch the selector between the two architectures with the conflict held at 0.35 and watch where the amber curve turns over. At 0.35 it saturates at five embodiments. Drop the conflict to 0.05 and the turnover moves out to seventeen — it still happens, because contradictory labels still accumulate, but you can pool three times as many bodies before paying for it. The per-embodiment head is not universally better; it is insurance, and the conflict slider is the premium.

The design conclusion is direct: share everything that is body-independent and condition everything that is not. Concretely — one vision-language trunk trained on all embodiments, plus either a small per-embodiment output head or, more cheaply, an embodiment identity token in the input so a single head can specialise its behaviour on demand. The token approach is elegant because it costs almost nothing and lets the model learn how much to specialise per embodiment rather than being told.

3 · The four practical decisions

  1. Normalise per embodiment, not globally. Action statistics differ by body, so a global normaliser produces a distribution that is wrong for everyone. Compute mean and scale per embodiment and store them alongside the embodiment ID. This is the single most common cross-embodiment bug and it is invisible — the model trains fine and behaves oddly.
  2. Weight by relevance, not by size. A naive pool is dominated by whichever dataset happens to be largest, which is rarely the one most like your setup. Up-weight embodiments whose gripper, control mode, and camera placement resemble yours. Lesson 10 of track 1's warning applies unchanged: a weight is a request, and availability decides.
  3. Pad and mask, do not project. Different bodies have different action dimensionalities. Padding to a maximum width with a validity mask keeps each dimension's meaning intact; projecting everything into a common latent action space mixes semantics across dimensions and loses the ability to command a specific joint. Padding is uglier and correct.
  4. Fine-tune on the target body last. Pooling gives a trunk with excellent perception and semantics; a final pass on your own hundreds of hours specialises the head. This is lesson 12's staging, and the order matters — pooling after specialising undoes the specialisation.
The measurement that stops the debate
Whether pooling helps you is empirical and cheap to settle: train on your body alone, then on the pool plus your body, and compare on your own held-out tasks. Do it early, because the answer varies with how unusual your setup is, and a team can spend months on an integration whose value was measurable in a week. And check per-task, not in aggregate — pooling reliably helps semantic and coarse-motion tasks while doing nothing for fine contact, so an aggregate number averages a real gain with a real zero.

4 · What pooling cannot fix

Two limits worth naming, because they set up the next lessons.

Pooling does not add contact information. Twenty-two robots' worth of vision and end-effector poses is still twenty-two robots' worth of data with no force channel. If the decisive quantity is a contact force — and lesson 08 argues it usually is — the pool contains none of it, no matter how large it grows. Scaling a corpus does not add a column it never had.

Pooling is still robots. All 1M+ trajectories were collected the expensive way, by the process lesson 04 priced. Pooling shares the cost across institutions, which is a genuine and large win, but it does not change the fundamental rate limit. To escape that you have to use data from bodies nobody instrumented — which is the largest corpus in existence and the subject of lesson 07.

Takeaway
Pooling works because almost all the parameters of a robot foundation model do body-independent perception and semantics, and because end-effector delta pose is task-frame, so the thin body-specific part is the only thing that mismatches. What interferes is not appearance but contradictory labels on identical observations — different strokes, delays and kinematics require different correct actions — so a single shared head peaks at some pool size and then declines. Fix it by sharing the trunk and conditioning the head, via a per-embodiment head or an embodiment ID token; normalise per embodiment, weight by relevance, pad-and-mask rather than project, and fine-tune on your body last. Pooling adds neither a force channel nor a higher collection rate.

Where this points next

Every hour discussed so far was collected on an instrumented robot. There are roughly three thousand six hundred and seventy hours of Ego4D, and over a million hours of internet video, showing bodies doing exactly the tasks we care about — with no action column at all. Lesson 07 asks what can be extracted from that, and gives an honest per-capability answer, including the capability whose transfer is exactly zero.

Interview prompts

  • Why does cross-embodiment pooling work despite incompatible commands? Nearly all parameters serve body-independent perception and semantics, so only a thin final mapping is body-specific — and task-frame end-effector actions make even that partly shareable. (§1)
  • What is the real source of interference, and why is it not appearance? Different bodies require different correct actions for identical observations — different gripper strokes, actuation delays and reachability — so a shared head receives contradictory labels and averages them. (§2)
  • Why does a shared action head have a peak pool size? Benefit from useful pooled hours saturates while interference grows with the number of embodiments, so their product declines past a finite n. (§2)
  • Give the architectural fix and a cheap version of it. Share the trunk and condition the head: a per-embodiment output head, or more cheaply an embodiment identity token in the input so the model learns how much to specialise. (§2)
  • Why normalise action statistics per embodiment rather than globally? Bodies have different action distributions, so a global normaliser is wrong for every one of them — and the model still trains cleanly, making the bug invisible. (§3)
  • Why pad-and-mask rather than project to a common action space? Padding preserves each dimension's meaning and the ability to command a specific joint; a learned projection mixes semantics across dimensions. (§3)
  • How would you settle whether pooling helps your setup, and what must you avoid? Train on your body alone versus pool-plus-your-body on your held-out tasks — reported per task, since pooling helps semantics and coarse motion but not fine contact, so an aggregate hides a real zero. (§3)
  • Name two things pooling cannot fix. It adds no force or contact channel, since none of the pooled data has one; and it does not raise the collection rate, since every trajectory was still gathered by teleoperation. (§4)
