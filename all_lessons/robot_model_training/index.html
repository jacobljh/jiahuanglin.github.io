Training a Robot Model

A policy is the only model that authors its own test set. That one asymmetry — against every classifier, language model and world model in this repository — generates a different discipline with its own central theorem, its own scarcest resource, and its own reason that scale alone does not fix it.

The seed this track grows from
A policy's state distribution dπ depends on π, so an error is not an independent sample — it is the next decision's input, drawn from a region the demonstrator never visited. Supervised errors average; policy errors compound. Everything in this track is downstream of that sentence.

The theorem the whole track turns on

With per-step error ε measured under the expert's own distribution, charging each departure for every remaining step gives

E[cost] ≈ Σt ε·(T−t) = ε·T(T−1)/2 ≈ ε T² / 2

against the supervised intuition's εT — a factor of T/2. At ε = 1% and T = 400 the linear reading says "a 1% problem" and the correct one says failure is essentially certain. A 99% validation accuracy on held-out demonstration frames is fully compatible with a policy that never completes the task.

Only three things escape it: train on your own state distribution, make recovery trained behaviour, or reduce the number of decision points. More data and bigger models reduce ε — the coefficient, never the exponent. That is why this field had to invent action chunking and on-policy correction rather than wait for scale.

And the data is scarce twice over
Actions must be in your body's units, so another robot's data is not directly reusable — and the states that matter are the ones your current policy visits, so corrections depreciate as the policy changes. For scale: Open X-Embodiment pools 1M+ trajectories across 22 embodiments from 60 datasets and 34 labs; DROID is 76k trajectories ≈ 350 hours, twelve months of work by 50 people at 13 institutions. Converted to tokens that is roughly 5,700× less than a language model's corpus.

The fifteen lessons

00
The actor's seat
Why dπ depending on π changes everything: at ε=1%, a 200-step episode ends off-distribution 87% of the time. What a robot foundation model is, and the two ways its data is scarce.
01
The action is the hard part
Four action spaces and the control rate each commits you to. Why only end-effector delta pose pools across bodies. The discretisation floor: ±500 mm at 8 bits is 3.9 mm against a 2 mm clearance.
02
Imitation, and the lie inside it
Derive O(εT²) from charging each departure for every remaining step. The three escapes, and why neither capacity nor data is among them.
03
Averaging two good plans
The conditional mean of two valid routes is a collision. Generative heads, and why chunking fixes both the modes and the exponent — plus the reactivity cost that gives k an interior optimum.
04
Where demonstrations come from
60 episodes per operator-hour; 100k episodes is a person-year and ~$50k of labour. A third of the cycle is reset. Why scripted collection and play data are undervalued, and three quality traps you cannot repair later.
05
Fix the distribution, not the loss
A demonstration confirms; a correction corrects — and puts the training distribution on the visitation distribution, restoring O(εT). HG-DAgger, the budget optimum, and why corrections depreciate as ρj.
06
One policy, many bodies
Pooling works because nearly all parameters are body-independent. Interference is contradictory labels on identical inputs, so a shared head peaks and declines — share the trunk, condition the head.
07
Learning from video of other bodies
Transfer ranked by capability, with force at exactly zero because newtons are not in the medium. Retarget goals, not motion. V-JEPA 2's ratio: 1M free hours under 62 grounded ones.
08
What cameras cannot see
Occlusion, scale, and the categorical fact that force has no appearance. Then compute why compliance beats precision — 0.50 to 0.88 with no change to the policy — and why chamfers outrank model work.
09
Simulation as a data engine
170 robot-days per wall-clock hour, plus the counterfactual replay hardware can never offer. But fidelity is inversely ordered against usefulness — so calibrate first, because identification makes the optimal width narrower and better.
10
RL as the residual
10⁷ steps is 2,300 real robot-hours or 41 simulator-seconds. Its three real roles, why from-scratch RL sits below the BC prior at robot-day budgets, and the free sparse reward you already collected.
11
Anatomy of a robot foundation model
A 10–25× latency gap forces two systems. Derive k ≥ fctl·τVLM, discover the same structure lesson 06 wanted, and note the staleness the design accepts on purpose.
12
The staged recipe
Inherit, pretrain, post-train, correct — and co-train throughout, because fine-tuning on a few dozen task templates erases compositional language while raising every metric you track.
13
Evaluation you can afford
At n=20 the Wilson interval is 58–92% and the minimum detectable effect is 41 points, so "76% vs 68%" measures noise. Detecting 10 points needs 330 trials per arm. Five moves that recover the power.
14
Capstone: spec a training programme
40 tasks, 4 operators, 6 months, with numbers throughout — and the discovery that honest evaluation costs 220 robot-weeks against a 26-week programme, 10× the training and collection combined.

How to read this track

  1. Rank checkpoints by rollout success, never by action MSE. Validation loss estimates ε under the wrong distribution, and the path to task success runs through T².
  2. Report the horizon with every success number. Success falls superlinearly in T, so a rate without a horizon is not a claim.
  3. Ask whether a fix touches the coefficient or the exponent. More data and bigger models touch ε. Corrections, chunking and hierarchy touch the structure.
  4. Instrument the body before collecting from it. Force cannot be retrofitted onto data already gathered, and it is the quantity that decides contact tasks.
  5. Price the evaluation before the training. It is routinely the binding constraint, and it is the one no budget increase fixes.
