The actor's seat

Every other model in this repository is trained on one distribution and tested on another that somebody else chose. A policy chooses its own. That single asymmetry generates a different discipline, a different central theorem, and a different scarcest resource — and it is the whole reason robot learning does not look like supervised learning with a robot attached.

Where we are
Track 1 built the forward map p(o′|o,a) — a simulator that answers counterfactuals accurately, obediently and fast. It has never moved anything. Now we take the other seat: the inverse map p(a|o,g). Same tuple, opposite direction, and one property changes that changes everything.

1 · The asymmetry, stated exactly

Take an image classifier. Training draws from D, test draws from D, and nothing the model does changes D. The model's errors are independent samples from a fixed distribution, so they average. That is the assumption underneath every learning-curve, every confidence interval, every "more data helps."

Now a policy. It acts at time 1, which determines the state at time 2, at which it acts again. So the distribution of states it is tested on is

dπ(s) = (1/T) Σt=1T P(st = s | π)

and the π in that expression is the thing being trained. The test distribution is a function of the model. Which means a mistake is not a sample from a fixed error distribution — it is an input to the next decision, drawn from a region the demonstrator never visited and the model was therefore never trained on.

The consequence in one sentence
Supervised errors average; policy errors compound. Everything in this track is downstream of that.

Make it quantitative right now, because the number is startling. Suppose the policy makes a mistake serious enough to leave the demonstrated region with probability ε per step. The probability it is still on familiar ground at step t is

P(on-manifold at t) = (1 − ε)t

At a very good ε = 0.01 — one mistake in a hundred decisions — a 200-step episode ends on familiar ground with probability 0.99²⁰⁰ ≈ 0.13. Eighty-seven percent of the time, the robot finishes the task in territory its training data never described. And 200 steps at 50 Hz is four seconds.

Set ε to 3% and the half-life is about 23 steps — under half a second at 50 Hz. That is the actual situation for a freshly cloned policy, and it explains an observation every robot-learning team makes independently: the policy looks excellent for the first second and then goes somewhere strange. It is not a bug in the network. It is the geometry of the problem.

2 · What a robot foundation model actually is

Strip away branding. The object being trained in 2025–26 is a map

(image(s), language instruction, proprioceptive state)  ↦  a chunk of future actions

and the standard construction is a pretrained vision-language model with an action head bolted on. Three concrete examples, which we will return to throughout:

RT-2 — a VLA that emits actions as text tokens, reusing the language head unchangeddiscretised actions
OpenVLA — 7B, Llama-2 with fused DINOv2 and SigLIP vision, trained on 970k episodes from Open X-Embodimentopen weights
π₀ — PaliGemma 3B plus a separate 300M flow-matching "action expert"; chunks of 50 waypoints at 50 Hz; 10k+ hours of robot datacontinuous actions

Notice the three design axes those choices span, because each becomes a lesson: how the action is represented (text tokens versus a continuous head — lesson 01), how many actions come out at once (chunking — lesson 03), and how the slow reasoning pass and the fast control loop are decoupled (lesson 11).

3 · Why the data is scarce in a different way than track 1's

Track 1's constraint was that the action column is expensive. Here it is worse in two specific ways.

1 · your body's units
Actions are not portable
A world model can learn physics from any video. A policy must emit commands this controller accepts. Data from a different arm has an action column in the wrong units — lesson 06's whole subject.
2 · your policy's states
Data goes stale
§1 says the states that matter are the ones your current policy visits. Change the policy and yesterday's corrections describe a distribution that no longer exists. Lesson 05, and track 3 lesson 09.

Put the scale next to it. The largest open real-robot dataset, Open X-Embodiment, pools 1M+ trajectories across 22 embodiments from 60 datasets and 34 labs — a heroic community effort. DROID, one of its highest-quality components, is 76k trajectories ≈ 350 hours, collected over 12 months by 50 people across 13 institutions.

Three hundred and fifty hours. A language model reads on the order of 15 trillion tokens. Convert DROID at track 1's rate and it is about 2.6 billion — roughly 5,700× less. This is not a field waiting for a better architecture; it is a field operating four orders of magnitude below the data regime where the current recipes were discovered, and every technique ahead is a response to that.

Takeaway
A policy's test distribution dπ depends on π, so its errors are inputs to later decisions rather than independent samples: supervised errors average, policy errors compound. With a per-step departure rate ε, only (1−ε)t of an episode is on trained ground — at a strong ε=1%, a 200-step episode ends off-distribution 87% of the time. The object being trained is (images, language, proprioception) ↦ an action chunk, and its data is scarce twice over: actions are in your body's units, and they describe a policy that keeps changing.

Where this points next

We are producing actions, so we had better be precise about what one is. It turns out to be the least glamorous and most consequential choice in the stack: the action space decides what control rate you need, which datasets are reusable at all, and — through discretisation — whether the millimetres your task depends on can be commanded. Lesson 01 computes that resolution floor, and finds the same shape of argument that track 1's blur floor had.

Interview prompts

  • State the structural difference between training a classifier and training a policy. The classifier's test distribution is fixed and independent of the model; a policy's state distribution d^π depends on π, so it authors its own test set. (§1)
  • Why do policy errors compound rather than average? An error changes the next state, so it becomes an input drawn from a region the demonstrator never visited and the model was never trained on. (§1)
  • At ε = 1% per step, what fraction of a 200-step episode ends on trained ground? (1−0.01)²⁰⁰ ≈ 0.13, so 87% of episodes finish in untrained territory — and 200 steps is four seconds at 50 Hz. (§1)
  • Describe the input-output signature of a modern robot foundation model. Images, a language instruction and proprioceptive state map to a chunk of future actions, typically via a pretrained vision-language model with an action head. (§2)
  • Name the two ways robot data is scarcer than world-model data. Actions must be in your own body's units so other robots' data is not directly reusable, and the states that matter are those your current policy visits, so data goes stale as the policy changes. (§3)
  • Put DROID's scale next to a language corpus and interpret it. 350 hours ≈ 2.6 billion tokens against roughly 15 trillion for text — about 5,700× less, so the field operates four orders of magnitude below the regime its recipes came from. (§3)
  • How does this track's derivation differ in direction from the RL track's? The RL track derives robot control downward from the MDP and reward; this track starts from obtainable data, proves cloning fails, and introduces RL only as a residual. (§4)
