Anatomy of a robot foundation model

A model large enough to know what "put the red one away" means takes hundreds of milliseconds to think. A controller that keeps an arm stable needs an answer every twenty. Those requirements are incompatible, and the way the field resolved them turns lesson 03's chunk length from a tuning knob into a hard constraint.

Where we are
Five sources of training signal now have to land on one artifact. Lesson 01 defined its output, lesson 03 gave it chunks, lesson 06 told us to share the trunk and condition the head. This lesson assembles the whole thing and derives the one constraint that ties the pieces together.

1 · The incompatibility, in numbers

Write both requirements down and the conflict is arithmetic, not architectural taste.

Understanding "put the red one away" — a vision-language model, billions of parameters200–500 ms per pass
Keeping a 7-DOF arm stable and smooth through contact20–50 ms per command
Ratio~10–25×

One model cannot satisfy both: shrink it to hit 50 Hz and it no longer understands the instruction; keep it large and the arm receives a command every third of a second, which for a moving arm in contact is not control at all.

The resolution is to notice that the two requirements are about different things. Semantics change slowly — "put the red one away" is the goal for the next several seconds. Actions change fast. So run them at different rates, and let the slow system emit enough actions to cover its own thinking time. That is frequency decoupling, and it is why every current robot foundation model is two systems rather than one.

2 · The constraint that follows immediately

If the reasoning pass takes τVLM and the controller consumes actions at fctl, then between one pass and the next the controller needs

k ≥ fctl · τVLM

actions in hand, or the arm runs out and stalls. Put in π₀'s numbers as a sanity check: a chunk of 50 waypoints at 50 Hz covers exactly 1 second, which comfortably brackets a 200–500 ms reasoning pass with margin to spare. The design is not arbitrary — the chunk length is the reasoning latency times the control rate.

Which reframes lesson 03
Lesson 03 derived an optimal chunk length k* from the disturbance rate — how often the world surprises you. Now we have a second, independent lower bound from the architecture. The chunk you ship must satisfy both: fctl · τVLM  ≤  k  ≤  k*(λ)

and if that interval is empty — if your world changes faster than your model can think — no chunk length works and the design must change. That is a real and common situation, and recognising it as an empty interval rather than a tuning failure is what points you at the right fix.

The three fixes, when the interval is empty: shrink τVLM (a smaller trunk, quantisation, caching), or add a third even-faster layer that handles disturbances locally without consulting the VLM (a compliant controller or a reactive safety layer — which is what lesson 08's compliance really is, architecturally), or reduce λ by fixturing the workcell. All three are available; the third is cheapest.

3 · The two-system anatomy, part by part

system 2 · slow
Vision-language trunk
A pretrained VLM — π₀ uses PaliGemma 3B, OpenVLA a 7B Llama-2 with fused DINOv2 and SigLIP vision. Consumes images plus the instruction, emits a conditioning signal. Runs at 2–5 Hz. Almost all the parameters, and the body-independent part (lesson 06).
system 1 · fast
Action expert
Small and specialised — π₀'s is a separate 300M-parameter transformer with its own weights and full bidirectional attention over the action tokens, taking proprioceptive state plus the trunk's conditioning and emitting the chunk via flow matching. Runs at 50 Hz. The body-specific part.

Two design details in that description are worth extracting because they are easy to get wrong.

Proprioception goes to the fast system, not the slow one. Joint state changes at 50 Hz, so feeding it into a 2 Hz trunk throws away nearly all of it. It belongs where it is consumed at rate — which is also where lesson 08's summarised force channel belongs, for the same reason.

The action expert has its own weights, and attends bidirectionally over the chunk. Bidirectionality is what makes the chunk internally coherent: waypoint 30 is generated with knowledge of waypoint 50, so the trajectory is smooth rather than a sequence of independent guesses. A causal head cannot do that, and it shows up as jerky motion.

Which explains why the two representational choices differ across systems. RT-2 emits actions as text tokens, reusing the language head at no extra parameter cost — accepting lesson 01's resolution ceiling and a low control rate. π₀ pays for a separate continuous expert and gets 50 Hz precision. That is the same trade lesson 01 identified, now visible as an architectural fork.

4 · What the hierarchy buys beyond latency

Three things, and the second is a genuine surprise.

  1. Independent scaling. The trunk scales with semantic difficulty — more objects, more language, more scenes. The expert scales with control difficulty — more DOF, higher precision. They are separate budgets, so you are not forced to buy language capability to get dexterity, or vice versa.
  2. The right place to attach each body. Lesson 06 wanted a shared trunk and a conditioned head. This hierarchy is that structure: one trunk pooled across 22 embodiments, one small expert per body. The architecture that solves the latency problem also solves the cross-embodiment problem, and they were derived independently.
  3. Independent update cadences. The trunk changes rarely — it is expensive to train and improves with pooled data from everywhere. The expert can be re-fit weekly on fresh corrections. That matches lesson 05's finding exactly: corrections depreciate as ρj per generation, so the component that consumes them must be the cheap one to retrain. A monolithic model cannot do this; you would have to retrain everything to absorb a week of corrections.
The failure mode of a two-rate system
The trunk's conditioning goes stale within a chunk. It was computed 300 ms ago, from images 300 ms old, and the arm has moved since. So the last waypoints of a chunk are being generated from a description of a world that has changed — which is lesson 03's missed-disturbance term, now with a named cause. The mitigations are the ones lesson 03 gave: overlap chunks and re-plan before exhausting them, and blend across the overlap. Just do not expect a smart architecture to remove the staleness; it is inherent to running the trunk slowly, and the design accepts it in exchange for being able to think at all.
Takeaway
Understanding an instruction takes 200–500 ms; stabilising an arm needs a command every 20–50 ms — a 10–25× gap that no single model spans. Hence frequency decoupling: a large slow vision-language trunk (2–5 Hz, nearly all the parameters, body-independent) plus a small fast action expert (50 Hz, body-specific, bidirectional over the chunk so the trajectory is coherent). The chunk must satisfy fctl·τVLM ≤ k ≤ k*(λ) — π₀'s 50 waypoints at 50 Hz is exactly 1 second bridging a ~300 ms pass — and if that interval is empty the design must change, not the knob. Send proprioception and summarised force to the fast system. The hierarchy also gives independent scaling, the natural attachment point for each body, and independent retraining cadences that match how fast corrections depreciate.

Where this points next

We have the shape. Now the training order, which is not obvious: a randomly initialised action expert on a pretrained trunk, five data sources with wildly different sizes, and a known hazard — fine-tuning hard on robot data destroys the language grounding that made the trunk worth using. Lesson 12 gives the staged recipe and shows why co-training is not optional.
