What cameras cannot see

Vision-only policies are excellent at reaching and terrible at insertion, and the reason is not perception quality. It is that the quantities deciding a contact task are not optical, and the fix is only half statistical — the other half is mechanical, and it is the half that pays better.

Where we are
Lesson 07's transfer table had one hard zero: contact force. Lesson 06 showed the pooled robot corpus has no force column either. So the capability that decides the manipulation frontier receives nothing from either of the two largest data sources. This lesson is what to do about that.

1 · Why the camera is the wrong instrument

Three separate reasons, and it is worth keeping them apart because they have different fixes.

1 · occlusion
The contact is behind the gripper
At the moment of grasp, the fingers are between the camera and the contact patch. The most decision-critical instant is the one with the least visual information — a scheduling problem no resolution solves.
2 · scale
Millimetres and micrometres
Track 1 lesson 03 computed it: a patch-16 tokenizer over a 400 mm field of view has a 25 mm blur floor. Deformation at contact is sub-millimetre.
3 · the quantity is not optical
Force has no appearance
A gripper holding a paper cup at 2 N and at 20 N look identical right up until the cup crushes. There is no imaging solution because force is not a visual property.

The third is the categorical one, and it means the answer must be a different sensor: joint torque, a wrist force-torque sensor, or tactile skin on the fingertips. These are the only channels that carry the quantity at all.

And they are nearly free to add to your own data
Here is the asymmetry worth exploiting. You cannot retrofit force onto Ego4D or Open X-Embodiment — the information is gone. But joint torque estimates are already available on most arms, at kHz, at zero marginal cost, and a wrist force-torque sensor is a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, once. So force is simultaneously the most expensive data in the field to obtain at scale and among the cheapest to add to the data you collect from tomorrow. Teams that record it from day one have something no corpus can give them; teams that do not cannot go back.

2 · Compute why compliance beats precision

Now the mechanical half, which is the better-paying half. Take the insertion: a peg with clearance c, and a policy whose positioning error is Gaussian with standard deviation σ.

Stiff position control. The controller drives to the commanded pose regardless of contact. Insertion succeeds only if the error was already within the clearance:

Pstiff = P(|e| < c) = erf( c / (σ√2) )

At c = 2 mm and σ = 3 mm this is erf(0.47) ≈ 0.50. A coin flip. And when it misses, the stiff controller keeps pushing, generating force F = k·x that scales with the misalignment — so the failure mode is not "it did not go in," it is "it jammed or broke the part."

Compliant control. Now let the controller behave like a spring: command a target, but allow deviation under contact force. The peg touches the chamfer at the socket's mouth, the contact force pushes it laterally, and it slides into alignment. The effective capture region is no longer the clearance — it is the clearance plus however much of the chamfer the compliance lets you exploit:

capture = c + chamfer · 1/(1 + k/k0)   ⟹   Pcompliant = erf( capture / (σ√2) )

With a 4 mm chamfer and a soft gain of 8 N/mm, the compliance factor is 1/(1+8/20) = 0.71, so capture becomes 2 + 4(0.71) ≈ 4.9 mm and P = erf(4.9/(3√2)) ≈ 0.89. Success went from a coin flip to nearly nine in ten, and the policy did not change at all. The mechanics solved a problem the statistics could not.

Set the chamfer to 0 and the two curves collapse onto each other. That is the honest boundary: compliance exploits geometry that guides. With a guiding feature it converts positioning error into a self-correcting motion; without one it buys only safety. Which turns into a design instruction that is not about learning at all — put chamfers, funnels, and fixtures in the workcell. Two millimetres of chamfer is worth more than a great deal of model improvement, and it is a one-time mechanical change.

3 · What this changes about the policy you train

Compliance is not just a controller setting; it changes the learning problem in three ways.

  1. The action space grows. The policy can now command stiffness alongside position — stiff during free-space transport, soft during contact. That is a genuinely useful extra action dimension, and it is one a human demonstrator sets unconsciously, so it must be either recorded (from the leader arm's measured force) or scripted per phase.
  2. The required precision falls. Lesson 02's ε is defined relative to a tolerance, and compliance widens the tolerance. So mechanical compliance directly reduces the ε in εT²/2 — a hardware change acting on the coefficient of the central theorem, which is a much cheaper way to buy it than data.
  3. Force becomes an observation, and a good one. Contact state is nearly unobservable visually and trivially observable in the force signal: a step change in wrist force is contact, a drop is a slip, a plateau is a seated part. These are crisp, low-dimensional, high-signal features — and they are what let a policy know it has succeeded, which vision often cannot tell it.
The sampling-rate mismatch you must handle explicitly
Force is meaningful at 1 kHz; vision arrives at 30 Hz; the policy runs at 50 Hz. Naively downsampling force to the policy rate destroys the signal — a 30 ms slip transient vanishes into a 20 ms average. Instead summarise the fast channel over the control interval: max, mean, variance, and time-since-last-transition. Those summaries preserve the events at the policy's rate. world_models/10 derives the general principle — match each channel's rate to the decision's rate, and hand the slow loop a summary of what it skips — and this is its most consequential instance.

4 · The consequence for data collection

Put the three lessons together and a clear priority order falls out for anyone collecting manipulation data today.

Record joint torque on every episodefree, already available
Add a wrist force-torque sensorone-off, low cost, high value
Add chamfers and fixtures to the workcellone-off mechanical, buys ε directly
Record the demonstrator's implied stiffness where the rig allowscheap if the leader measures force
Add tactile fingertipsmoderate cost; decisive for slip and deformables
Collect more vision-only demonstrations of contact taskslowest value per hour
Because the last row cannot contain the deciding quantity at allno scale fixes a missing column

That bottom row is where a lot of effort goes, and this lesson's argument is that it is misallocated. Collecting more vision-only demonstrations of a contact task is buying more of the column that does not decide the outcome. Instrumenting the body first, and only then collecting, makes every subsequent hour worth more.

Takeaway
Cameras fail at contact for three separate reasons — the gripper occludes the contact patch at the decisive instant, the scale is sub-millimetre against a 25 mm blur floor, and force has no appearance at all. The last is categorical, so the fix is a different sensor: joint torque (free, already on the arm), a wrist force-torque sensor (one-off, cheap), tactile skin. Then the mechanical half, which pays better: compliance converts positioning error into a self-correcting slide along a chamfer, taking a 2 mm clearance at 3 mm error from erf(0.47)≈0.50 to about 0.88 with no change to the policy — so put chamfers in the workcell. Compliance also widens the tolerance that defines ε, buying lesson 02's coefficient with hardware. Summarise the kHz force channel into the control interval rather than downsampling it.

Where this points next

Force and contact data is the most valuable and the most expensive kind, obtainable only by having a real body do the real task. Which makes an alternative extremely attractive: a simulator computes contact forces exactly, for free, in parallel, with perfect labels and free resets. Lesson 09 takes that offer seriously — and finds that the parameters governing contact are precisely the ones simulators get wrong, which turns the whole thing into a question about calibration before randomisation.
