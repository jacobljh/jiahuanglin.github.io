Averaging two good plans

Two demonstrators route around the mug on opposite sides. Both succeed. A regression policy trained on both learns to go straight through it. The fix — predict a distribution, and predict a whole chunk at once — happens to also divide lesson 02's quadratic by the chunk length, which is why one change carried so much of the field's recent progress.

Where we are
Lesson 02 assumed one correct action per state, with ε the chance of missing it. Human data violates that before compounding even begins. And the same mechanism appears in track 1 lesson 07 for futures — the conditional mean of two valid outcomes is an invalid one. Here it is the mean of two valid plans, and the consequence is a collision.

1 · The average of two safe paths is not safe

First get straight that none of this is a recording defect. Ask two people to load a dishwasher and they will not agree on whether the pan or the lid goes in first. Ask one person on two different days and they still will not. Hand anyone a rotationally symmetric connector and there are four equally correct ways to grip it. Any dataset large enough to be useful therefore contains several right answers to the same question — and that is a property of tasks, not of sloppy collection. The trouble starts when you ask a model to produce "the" answer anyway.

Formalise the situation. At state s, the demonstration data contains actions from two modes: go left with probability ½, go right with probability ½. A squared-error policy converges to the conditional mean:

πMSE(s) = E[a | s] = ½·aleft + ½·aright ≈ straight ahead

Straight ahead is where the mug is. This is not a small error to be polished away with more capacity — the mean of a bimodal distribution lands in the region of lowest density, so the model is aiming, with perfect fidelity to its objective, at the action the data most strongly indicates nobody took.

Why "collect cleaner demonstrations" is the wrong instinct
The natural response is to standardise: one operator, one route, every time. It works, and it costs you the thing you were buying. Multimodality in demonstrations is not noise, it is coverage — evidence about which of several routes remain available when the first is blocked. Averaging it away yields a brittle policy with one strategy; standardising collection yields a policy with one strategy and no evidence that others exist. The right move is to keep the modes in the data and change the head so it can represent them.

And note where multimodality comes from, because it is unavoidable rather than a data-collection defect: different operators, the same operator on different days, genuinely equivalent orderings of subtasks ("pick up the lid or move the pan first"), and symmetry (a rotationally symmetric part has several equally correct grasps). Any dataset large enough to be useful is multimodal.

2 · Two fixes, and they compose

Fix one: a generative head. Replace the point estimate with a distribution over actions and sample. Diffusion Policy is the canonical instance — a denoising head over the action sequence, which represents arbitrary shapes without committing to a mode count — and π₀ uses flow matching for the same reason. Then π(a|s) puts mass on left and right, and a sample is a real plan rather than a compromise between plans.

Fix two: chunk the output. Instead of one action, emit the next k. ACT does this with k ≈ 100 at 50 Hz — two seconds of committed motion — and this is where the two fixes stop being independent, because chunking solves the mode problem a second way.

Here is the argument, and it is the nicest idea in this lesson. Multimodality bites hardest when the policy must re-choose a mode at every step, because at step 1 it might commit left, at step 2 right, and the resulting sequence belongs to neither route — mode switching mid-trajectory is itself a failure mode, distinct from averaging. Predicting the whole chunk collapses k mode decisions into one. The commitment is made once and then executed.

And it acts on lesson 02's exponent. If a chunk is one decision, the number of independent decision points falls from T to T/k, and the accumulated cost becomes

ε · (T/k) · T = ε T² / k

A twentyfold reduction at k=20, for free, from a change made for a different reason. That coincidence — the fix for multimodality is also the fix for compounding — is why chunking became standard so fast.

3 · But chunking has a cost, and therefore an optimum

Committing to two seconds of motion means two seconds of not reacting. If the part slips at 200 ms into the chunk, the policy keeps executing a plan for a world that no longer exists.

Model it. Disturbances arrive at rate λ per second; a chunk of k steps at control rate f lasts k/f seconds, so

P(unhandled disturbance) = 1 − e−λk/f

Two terms in k pointing opposite ways. Compounding failure falls as 1/k; missed-disturbance failure rises toward 1. Success is the product, so there is an interior optimum:

success(k) ≈ (1 − εT²/2k) · e−λk/f

The widget makes the design rule readable directly: k* is set by how often the world surprises you. A quiet, rigid, well-fixtured cell tolerates long chunks — commit and execute. A cloth-folding task where the material moves continuously wants short chunks and frequent re-planning. Reading a published k and adopting it is therefore a mistake; k is a property of your environment, not of the method.

The mainstream compromise: overlap and blend
You do not have to choose. Emit a chunk of k steps but re-plan every k/4 steps, blending the overlapping predictions — temporal ensembling. This gets the commitment benefit for the mode decision while keeping the reaction latency at k/4, and it smooths the discontinuity where one chunk ends and the next begins, which is otherwise a visible jerk in the motion and a real source of dropped objects.

The last row is the diagnostic that pays. Failures at the chunk's start mean the mode decision was wrong — a head problem. Failures in the middle mean the world changed and you could not react — a k problem. Failures at the boundary mean discontinuity — a blending problem. Three different fixes, and the timestamp tells you which, which is far faster than guessing.

Takeaway
Demonstrations are multimodal by construction (multiple operators, equivalent orderings, symmetric grasps), and the conditional mean of two valid routes lands in the lowest-density region — a collision, not a compromise. So keep the modes in the data and change the head: a generative policy (diffusion or flow) samples a real plan, and chunking collapses k mode decisions into one — which also divides lesson 02's quadratic by k. Chunking costs reactivity, P(unhandled) = 1−e−λk/f, so k* is set by your disturbance rate, not by a published number. Overlap and blend to get both.

Where this points next

Both fixes need demonstrations, and a chunked generative policy needs more of them than a per-step regressor did — it is learning a distribution over sequences rather than a point per state. So the question can no longer be deferred: where do demonstrations come from, how fast, and at what price? Lesson 04 does the throughput arithmetic, and the answer explains why the whole field is shaped the way it is.

Interview prompts

  • Why is the average of two valid demonstration modes worse than either? The conditional mean of a bimodal distribution lands in the lowest-density region between the modes — for two routes around an obstacle, that is straight into it. (§1)
  • Why is standardising demonstration collection the wrong fix? Multimodality is coverage, not noise: it is evidence about which alternative routes exist, so removing it yields a brittle single-strategy policy with no record of alternatives. (§1)
  • Give the two distinct ways chunking helps with multimodality and compounding. It collapses k per-step mode decisions into one commitment, preventing mid-trajectory mode switching; and it reduces decision points from T to T/k, dividing the quadratic term by k. (§2)
  • Write the cost of chunking and explain the resulting optimum. P(unhandled disturbance) = 1−exp(−λk/f) rises with k while compounding failure falls as 1/k, so their product peaks at a finite k*. (§3)
  • Why is copying a published chunk length a mistake? k* is determined by the disturbance rate of your environment relative to your control rate, so it is a property of the task, not the method. (§3)
  • What does overlapping chunks with blending buy? Commitment for the mode decision plus reaction latency of only the re-plan interval, and it removes the boundary discontinuity that otherwise jerks the motion. (§3)
  • A chunked policy fails consistently in the middle of chunks. What is wrong? k is too long for the disturbance rate — the policy is executing a plan for a world that has changed; shorten k or re-plan sooner. (§4)
  • Why does a chunked generative policy need more demonstrations than a per-step regressor? It fits a distribution over action sequences rather than a single point per state, which is a much larger object to estimate. (§4)
