The curriculum

Four objectives, one budget, and they disagree. Staging is the standard answer and it is usually right — but "usually" is not a principle. There is a crossing point between two competing costs, and on one side of it joint training genuinely wins.

Where we are
Lessons 02–08 each added a requirement: represent the scene, predict one step, survive long rollouts, obey the action, remember. Each has its own loss term. Adding them up with hand-tuned weights is what most projects do first, and it is why most projects have a model that is mediocre at all five.

1 · Why the objectives fight, concretely

Imagine four people sharing one steering wheel. One wants every detail of the scene preserved. One wants the state small enough to roll forward cheaply. One wants the model maximally reactive to its input. One wants it deliberately damped so errors die out. None of them is wrong, and pulling at once does not average — it cancels. That cancellation has a size, and so does the alternative of letting them steer in turn.

Not vaguely — name the pairs, because the conflicts are specific and each one has a direction.

pairthe disagreement
reconstruction vs. dynamicsReconstruction wants every high-variance detail preserved (lesson 02: the background). Dynamics wants a compressed state that is easy to roll forward. One asks the encoder to keep everything, the other to throw most of it away.
one-step vs. long-horizonOne-step accuracy is maximised by fitting δ as tightly as possible. Long-horizon stability is bought by contraction — deliberately damping responses to input variation. A contractive map is less reactive, hence slightly worse one-step (lesson 04).
fidelity vs. controllabilityUnder lesson 05's arithmetic, the likelihood-maximising move is to lean on history and ignore the action. Controllability requires the opposite.
sharpness vs. calibrationLesson 07: a head can concentrate mass to look decisive, or spread it to be honest. Not both.

Now the mechanism by which "they fight" costs you something measurable. Two loss terms whose gradients point in opposing directions partially cancel in the sum. With m objectives and average pairwise conflict c ∈ [0,1], the useful component of the combined gradient shrinks roughly like

efficiencyjoint ≈ 1 / (1 + c · (m − 1))

With four objectives and c = 0.45, that is 1/(1+1.35) ≈ 0.43. You are spending more than half your compute on gradient components that cancel. Staging removes the cancellation entirely — one objective at a time, full efficiency.

2 · But staging has its own bill

Train stage 2 and stage 1's capability decays. This is ordinary catastrophic forgetting: nothing in stage 2's objective preserves stage 1's solution, so the parameters drift toward whatever stage 2 prefers. If each subsequent stage retains a fraction (1−λ) of the previous stage's capability, then after m stages the first stage's contribution is

efficiencystaged ≈ (1 − λ)m−1

With λ = 0.20 and four stages, 0.8³ = 0.512. Better than 0.43, but not by much — and if λ were 0.35 the answer flips. Set the two expressions equal and the crossing point falls out:

c* = ( (1−λ)−(m−1) − 1 ) / (m − 1)

Below c*, train jointly. Above it, stage. And note the third option that dominates both, because it attacks λ directly: stage with replay. Keep a small fraction of each earlier stage's data and loss in every later stage. It costs a little of the current stage's throughput and drives λ toward zero, which is why in practice the answer is almost always "stage, with replay" rather than a genuine choice between the two extremes.

Do the experiment in two moves. First set conflict to 0 and forgetting to 0.4: joint training wins outright, because there is nothing to gain from separating objectives that agree. Then set conflict to 0.8 and forgetting to 0.1 and staging wins by a wide margin. Now return to the defaults (0.45 and 0.2, where staging leads 0.51 to 0.43) and push replay to 40%: staging goes to 0.67 while joint training does not move at all, because replay is a lever only staging has. It does not reverse the verdict at zero conflict — nothing does — but wherever conflict is non-trivial it widens staging's margin for a few percent of throughput, which is why the real answer is almost never either pure strategy.

3 · The five stages, and what forces each boundary

Given that we stage, the ordering is not arbitrary. Each boundary is forced by a dependency: a stage cannot start until the thing it consumes is stable.

  1. Tokenizer / encoder. Reconstruction and commitment losses only. Freeze when codebook usage and perplexity plateau (lesson 03 §4). Forced first because every later stage's inputs and targets are defined in this representation — change it later and all downstream learning is invalidated.
  2. Short-horizon dynamics. One-step or few-step prediction in the frozen representation, teacher-forced or diffusion-forced. Forced second because long-horizon training on a model that cannot do one step is just an expensive way to do one step.
  3. Long-horizon / rollout. Extend the effective horizon, drive J down (lesson 04). Forced third because contraction is a refinement of an existing map, not a replacement for it.
  4. Action-conditioned post-training. A smaller, high-quality, action-diverse, controllability-focused set (lesson 05 §5, lesson 11). Forced fourth because controllability is measured on rollouts, so the rollouts must first be worth measuring.
  5. Distillation to the deployment budget. Compress sampling steps to fit the tick (lesson 12). Forced last because you can only distil a teacher that exists.
A freeze is a commitment, so make it explicit
Freezing the tokenizer at stage 1 makes stages 2–5 cheap and stable, and it permanently locks in lesson 03's ceiling. That is the right trade provided you checked the ceiling first. The pathology to avoid is discovering at stage 4 that contact is unrepresentable and unfreezing — which invalidates everything learned in stages 2 and 3. Run the blur-floor arithmetic before stage 1, not after stage 4.

4 · The trap: staged training, staged evaluation

This is the mistake that survives longest because it feels like good hygiene. You train in stages, so you evaluate each stage's objective as you go: reconstruction after stage 1, one-step error after stage 2, horizon curves after stage 3. Every number looks good. The final model is bad.

The reason is that the interactions live between stages and no per-stage metric can see them. Stage 3's contraction slightly degraded stage 2's one-step accuracy — fine in isolation, but if it degraded it specifically in the contact regime, stage 4's controllability has nothing to work with. Stage 4's post-training on a narrow controllable set may have quietly broken the diversity that stage 2 learned.

The rule
Train in stages, evaluate jointly, every stage. Run the full lesson-01 acceptance suite — controllability, consistency, calibration, in the latency budget — at every stage boundary, including the boundaries where the property being tested is not yet supposed to work. The value is in the trend: a metric that was rising and starts falling at a boundary has located an interaction, and locating it at the boundary is a hundred times cheaper than locating it at the end.

Stratification is the part people skip and the part that pays. An aggregate one-step error hides the only regime that matters: free-space motion is easy and abundant, contact is hard and rare, and an average dominated by the former will look excellent while the latter regresses. Report the two separately, always. It is the same argument as lesson 02's — decision-relevance and volume are anti-correlated — applied to metrics instead of bits.

Takeaway
Joint training loses roughly 1/(1+c(m−1)) of its gradient to cancellation between objectives that genuinely disagree; staged training loses roughly (1−λ)m−1 to forgetting. Equate them for the crossing point c* = ((1−λ)−(m−1)−1)/(m−1) — and note that stage with replay dominates both by attacking λ directly. The five stages are ordered by dependency (representation → one-step → horizon → control → latency), each freeze is a permanent commitment to be checked beforehand, and the discipline that saves the run is: train in stages, evaluate jointly at every boundary, stratified by regime.

Where this points next

Each of those five stages wants different data. Stage 2 wants breadth so the dynamics generalise. Stage 4 wants a small, action-diverse, high-quality set. And the sources have wildly different availability — a hundred hours of grounded interaction against a million hours of video. Lesson 10 makes mixture the explicit design object, and shows that the binding constraint is not what you want but what exists.
