The rare case

Lesson 5 ended on a frequency. The case that leaves the shuttle least time to stop, a pedestrian who steps out from behind the van closer than 12 m, is drawn in one frame in 123, so 1,600 training frames hold 13 of them, and the detector misses 58% of them where it misses 9% of the pedestrians who stand in the open at the same distances. A program, unlike the street, can choose how often to draw a case. This lesson spends renders on the rare case on purpose and measures what the detector makes of the extra examples: they change what the loss believes, a weight restores the street at a price in effective sample size, and the share of renders and the weight of a case turn out to be two different decisions. It cannot say what a miss costs, or what counts as a pedestrian.

The thesis, here
A generator may spend its renders where it likes, but a learner takes the frequencies it is shown for the truth. Drawing a case on purpose is therefore two decisions that are easy to confuse: how many examples of it to draw, which sets the variance, and how much each example counts, which sets the detector. The weight that restores the street is the density ratio p/q, and what it costs is the effective sample size.
Linear position
Forced by:
New idea: drawing a case on purpose changes the distribution the learner sees, and a weight, target over proposal, converts it to any distribution we choose for a price in effective sample size. The share of renders decides how noisy the detector is and the weight decides which detector it is: with the street's weights it is the natural one, 59% miss on the rare case, and as drawn at a tenth of the renders it is the detector of a street where the case is 14 times as likely to be the answer, 51%.
Forces next:
The plan
Seven moves. (1) Measure the rare case, and see that more natural data does not repair it. (2) Compare the ways of getting more examples and find the one only a program has. (3) Derive what a mixture does to what the learner believes, and the weight that undoes it. (4) Price the weight: the effective sample size. (5) Measure everything on the street. (6) Choose the share and the weight. (7) Ask which pedestrians count in the rare slice.

1 · The case the exam barely sees

Call case R a pedestrian who steps out from behind the van at a depth under 12 m. The program Lesson 5 left, the exact-stage program bbba (the street's scene, light and camera, the program's own label rule), draws its scenes from a law it can run, so it can count its own frequencies without a single label. In 106 scene draws case R came up 8,150 times, 0.815% of the frames, one in 123; an exact integral of the scene law gives the same figure. A training set of 1,600 frames holds 13 of them. Not every one shows enough of the pedestrian to count: 80% pass the exam's rule (at least 15% of the silhouette and 6 px² visible), which makes the exam's own count one frame in 153, and the real test set of 3,000 frames holds 19 of them, too few to grade a slice.

So the lab grades the slice another way. It draws the street directly in case R, a stratified sample of 3,000 frames in which 2,400 pedestrians count, and a second sample of open pedestrians closer than 12 m, for comparison. A project cannot buy these frames; the lab can, and says so. The detector is the one the exact-stage program trains on 1,600 frames (three seeds), at the exam's threshold.

The exam is a frame average and prices a case by how often the street draws it; for a case that matters for another reason it is the wrong instrument, so from here on the lesson grades slices.

2 · Where the renders can go

To get more examples of R a project has three roads, and they differ in how many frames must be produced for each example.

OptionFrames rendered per example of RWhat it costsWhat goes wrong

(b) Draw naturally, keep every R frame and a fraction of the rest123 rendered, one kept160 examples among 1,600 kept frames need 19,600 renderedthe same training set as (c) for 12 times the renders
(c) Draw R on purpose1, plus 17.2 scene drawsa conditional scene samplerthe training set is no longer the street (§3)

Option (c) is the program's privilege: a fleet collects what it meets, a program is asked for what it wants. The sampler is a rejection sampler on the scene law. The scene program draws the van first and draws everything else without asking whether a pedestrian will step out; forcing the step-out (a pedestrian present, hidden behind the van) therefore gives exactly the street's law of the scene given a step-out, and keeping only the draws closer than 12 m gives its law given R. A draw is kept with probability 5.8%, which costs 17.2 scene draws, microseconds each, per kept frame and one render. The check is a comparison of 8,000 scenes from the sampler with the R scenes found among 106 natural draws: on the pedestrian's depth and position and the van's depth and length no distribution differs by more than 0.02 (Kolmogorov-Smirnov), inside what chance allows. The program can now ask for any share q of its frames to be case R, and the rest of this lesson is about what it should ask for, and what the answer does to the detector.

3 · What a mixture does to the statistics

Let p be the street's frequency of R and q the share the generator draws: with probability q a frame from the street's law given R, otherwise a frame from its law given anything else, both exactly as the street has them. The detector minimises an average of a loss ℓ over its frames, which is an expectation under the generator's law; the street's loss is the expectation under its own. Both are sums over the two cases, with μR and μo the mean loss of each, and the link is one line:

Eq[ w·ℓ ] = q·(p/q)·μR + (1 − q)·((1 − p)/(1 − q))·μo = p·μR + (1 − p)·μo = Estreet[ ℓ ]

Weighting each frame by the density ratio, wR = p/q for a frame of R and wo = (1 − p)/(1 − q) for any other, makes the generator's average the street's, in expectation. That is the importance-sampling identity: the plain weighted mean is unbiased, while the self-normalised form that divides by the weights' sum is only consistent (Owen, Monte Carlo theory, methods and examples, chapter 9). It needs the generator to draw whatever the street draws: no weight repairs a case the sampler gives probability zero, which is Lesson 5's point again. At q = 25% the weights are 0.033 for R and 1.32 for everything else, and they add up to the number of frames.

Without the weights the learner's world is the mixture. A classifier fits P(pedestrian | picture) under the frames it sees, and by Bayes' rule its odds are the street's odds times the ratio of the training prior odds to the street's. A mixture changes the prior in two ways that behave differently.

(i) A shift of the label. The share of frames that hold a pedestrian changes. The odds of every picture are multiplied by the same constant, so for a logistic model that is right the intercept moves by the log of the ratio of prior odds and the slopes stay (Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker, 2002; King and Zeng, 2001), the ranking of frames does not move, and the exam, which fixes its threshold at 10% false alarms on pedestrian-free frames, absorbs the change. The assumption is that frames are chosen by their label alone; this detector also chooses its hard negatives by their score, so the absorption is approximate, and §5 measures how far.

(ii) A shift inside the positive class. More of the pedestrians are R. Of the pedestrian frames in a natural set, 1.6% are R; at q = 25% it is 40%, and the odds that a pedestrian frame is R are multiplied by c(q) = q(1 − p) / (p(1 − q)) = 40.6, which a count over a plan of 1,600 frames reproduces (41). Only pictures that look like R are multiplied, and a sliver of pedestrian beside a van is not far from a van's edge. No threshold undoes that: the loss now says R is 40.6 times as important as the street thinks, and the detector spends its capacity accordingly. The weights are what undoes it, because they put the street's odds back.

4 · What the weights cost: effective sample size

The weights repair the expectation, not the variance. A weighted mean Σ wi fi / Σ wi of independent values of equal spread σ has variance σ² Σ wi² / (Σ wi)², and an unweighted mean of neff values has σ² / neff; the two agree when

neff = (Σ wi)² / Σ wi²

This is Kish's effective sample size (Kish, 1965, used for importance sampling after Kong, 1992): a diagnostic that reads the weights alone and is the exact variance factor only when every frame's loss has the same spread, which §6 relaxes. With qN frames of R at weight p/q and the rest at (1 − p)/(1 − q), the sums are closed forms:

neff / N = 1 / ( p²/q + (1 − p)²/(1 − q) ) ≈ 1 − q

Over- and under-sampling are the same trade: fixing the shares fixes the weights and the effective sample size, and the only difference is how many frames were rendered to get there (option (b) of §2).

As drawn, the examples help the slice and the loss pays for it. The rare slice falls by 7.6 points at 10% and by 9.4 at 25%, and then stops: at 50% it is 48.0%. About 48% is what this detector can do on R. The cost has no such floor. The exam rises by 1.5, 4.7 and 11.4 points, the open pedestrians at the same distances go from 9.0% to 18.7%, and the score that lets 10% of the empty street's frames alarm moves from 0.06 to 0.29 and 0.55. This is shift (ii) of §3: the loss says R is 40.6 times as important, so the detector trades the common cases for it, and past a share of about 10% the trade only loses.

Weighted, the detector is the natural one at a smaller size. The rare slice is back at 59.2%, 60.9% and 62.0%, no better than natural and a little worse. The exam is the natural program's at the effective sample size: the weighted designs score 48.4%, 49.7% and 49.3%, and the natural program trained on exactly 1,463, 1,220 and 813 frames scores 48.5%, 50.0% and 49.5%. The effective sample size is an exchange rate that the lab can check, and what the weights buy is the street's exam, not the slice: 400 frames of R that weigh as 13 teach the detector what 13 teach it. The brute-force control agrees from the other side: the natural program at 11,200 frames holds 73 frames of R, as many as 5% as drawn (80), and misses 57.1% on the slice where the 5% design misses 52.9%. The examples were never what the detector lacked. The weight on them was.

The weight chooses the detector, the share chooses the variance. Draw 50% of the frames in R and weight them to restore a world in which R is a quarter of the frames (weights 0.5 and 1.5): the rare slice is 48.6% and the exam 52.5%; draw 10% and weight the same way (2.5 and 0.83): 48.0% and 52.0%. The detector as drawn at 25% scores 48.7% and 51.5%. They are one detector; the two off-target designs have an effective sample size of 1,280 frames where drawing the target itself keeps all 1,600. A mixture as drawn is a weighting in disguise: it asks for a street in which R has the odds c(q), and the weights t/q ask for the same street from any share.

Scores keep their scale only under the weights. The exam re-sets its threshold, so it cannot see that the unweighted detector's scores no longer mean what they meant: at a fixed cut the alarm rate on empty street frames goes from 10.7% to 15.0% and 21.2%, and stays at 11.9% and 12.5% when weighted. Anything that reads a score as a probability, a braking rule for one, inherits the wrong prior (Lesson 11). A pure shift of the label, the share of frames that hold a pedestrian moved from 50% to 90% or 10%, moves that same threshold by +0.7 and −1.4 and the exam by +2.3 and −2.1 points, and the rare slice by 0.2 and 1.5: shift (i) is mostly absorbed by the threshold and cannot buy the case.

Road not taken · oversample and say nothing
It is the usual practice, and the first look rewards it: the rare slice, the number the team watches, falls by 7.6 to 9.4 points. What breaks is everything else. The exam rises 4.7 points at a quarter of the renders and 11.4 at a half ("balance the classes"), the open pedestrians at the same distances miss 9.7 points more, the scores drift off the street's scale, and the gain stops at 48%. The road returns, as a choice: an unweighted mixture is the target in which R is 13.6, 40.6 or 121 times as important, and §6 says how to make that choice on purpose instead of by the share of the renders.

6 · Choosing the share and the weight

The lab has separated two decisions that the textbook, and the unweighted mixture, merge. The target is the street as the job weighs it: ordinary pedestrians count 1 and a miss in R counts c, a cost that this lesson cannot price (Lesson 11 does) and that the exam sets to 1. Its share of R is t = cp/(1 − p + cp). The share is how the renders are spent to reach it. For a given target, the variance of the weighted loss Σs ts μ̂s, with ns frames in stratum s and σs the spread of a frame's loss there, is

Var = Σs ts² σs² / ns, with Σs ns = N minimised at ns ∝ ts σs

(set the derivative of the variance plus a multiplier times Σ ns to zero: ts² σs² / ns² is the same for every stratum). This is the classical optimal allocation of stratified sampling, and it says: spend renders in proportion to the weight a stratum has in the target times how much its losses vary. The lab measures the spreads from the natural detector: a frame of R holds a pedestrian that counts and is missed with probability 0.46, so σR = 0.50; for any other frame it is 0.19 and σo = 0.39. At the exam's c = 1 the best share is the street's own, 1.0%, and any oversampling only costs: that is the case of §5. At c = 10 the best share is 9.4%, which is where the 10% design breaks even with the natural one. At c = 100 it is half the renders, but the measured winner is the 10% design: the detector cannot get below 48% on R, so the extra weight a larger share buys is paid for and not used. A target with c above 10 is worth a tenth of the renders; the lab says no more than that, and the price of c itself is the next lesson but four.

7 · Which pedestrians count in the rare slice

Every miss rate of this lesson is over the pedestrians that count, and the rare slice is where counting is least obvious: a pedestrian who steps out is a sliver first. The last panel of the widget cuts the detector's miss rate by how much of the silhouette shows. For the first 15% of it the detector misses nearly everything (100% below 5%, 98% between 10% and 15%), and the miss rate falls as he steps into view to 22% for the last fifth. A rule that decides who counts is a cut on this curve, and the number it reports is the average of the curve above the cut.

The program counts 13% of the R frames that have a sliver under 6 px² as pedestrians, and trains on them; the exam does not count them, so the same detector scores 4.6 points worse under the program's rule than under the exam's, and 15.1 points better under a rule that asks for half of him. The slice that §6 told us to spend renders on has a number that moves by 19.7 points with the definition of who counts, more than any share of the renders moves it (7.6 at 10%). Training frames and exam agree about what is in a frame and how often; they do not yet agree about what counts, and Lesson 7 treats that as a question of its own.

What this lesson did not do
It did not say what a miss in R costs, so the target of §6 stays a parameter: Lesson 11 prices a close step-out by the time left to stop. The weights used the street's frequency p, which the program counted exactly; a project reads p off its logs, and with 10,000 logged frames and a case of this frequency it sees 82 of them, so p and every weight carry an error of 11% (Lesson 5's counts, Lesson 12's proof). The finding that more examples of R did not help belongs to this learner, 111 numbers on fixed filters; a learner with more room could turn them into skill, and the lesson does not measure it. One rare case was drawn on purpose; a real project has dozens, and the shares compete for the same renders. The label rule was left as the program's: Lesson 7.

Common mistakes / failure modes

"A rare case needs more data"
Seven times the renders moved the rare slice by 1 point. The detector was short of weight on the case, not of frames (§1, §5).
"Oversampling and weighting do the same thing"
As drawn, 25% in R lowers the rare slice by 9.4 points and raises the exam by 4.7; weighted, the slice stays at 60.9% and the exam at 49.7%. The share sets the variance, the weights set the detector (§5).
"Weights are free, they just restore the truth"
They cost effective sample size: 1,220 of 1,600 at 25%, 813 at 50%, which is what the natural program scores with that many frames (§4, §5).
"A threshold repairs a prior shift"
Only a shift of the label, and only when frames are chosen by label alone. A shift inside the positive class moves the odds of some pictures only, and the exam rose 4.7 points at 25% after re-setting its threshold (§3).
"Balance the classes: draw 50%"
The slice stops improving at about 48%, near a tenth of the renders; at 50% the exam is 11.4 points worse (§5, §6).
"The exam will tell me if the rare case improved"
The rare case is 1.5% of the exam's pedestrians: a perfect detector of it would move the exam by 0.9 points (§1).
"Any weight repairs any case"
Only a case the sampler draws with positive probability; a weight multiplies, it does not create (§3).

Checkpoint exercise

Try it
A generator draws 20% of its 2,000 frames from a case that is 1% of the street's frames. What are the two weights, what is the effective sample size, how much do the case's frames weigh in all, and by what factor does a learner trained without weights overrate the case's odds? Answer: the weights are 0.01/0.2 = 0.05 for the case and 0.99/0.8 = 1.2375 for the rest, and they add to 2,000. The effective sample size is 2,000² / (400 · 0.05² + 1,600 · 1.2375²) = 1,632, which is 0.816 of N, near 1 − q = 0.8. The 400 frames of the case weigh 20, the 20 frames the street would have given. Without weights the odds that a pedestrian frame is the case are multiplied by 0.2 · 0.99 / (0.01 · 0.8) = 24.75.

Where this points next

The program now draws the street's cases, draws a rare one on purpose when it must, and says with its weights what its frames stand for. The detector, the exam and the weights agree about what is in a frame and how often it happens. They do not agree about what counts: every number in the rare slice is a miss rate over the pedestrians that count, and counted by the program's rule, the exam's, or one that asks for half of him, the same detector misses 62.7%, 58.1% or 43.0% of them. A pedestrian with two pixels showing is a pedestrian to the program and not to the exam, which asks for a visible fraction. What does a label mean, and where can the program's definition differ from the exam's?

Takeaway
A program can draw a rare case on purpose, at the price of a training set that is no longer the street. The loss then says the case is common, and the detector pays for it elsewhere: at a tenth of the renders the rare slice improves by 7.6 points and the exam gets 1.5 points worse, and the gain stops near 48% while the cost does not. The weights p/q restore the street exactly, in expectation, and then the detector is the natural one trained on as many frames as the effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw², roughly N(1 − q). The share of the renders decides the variance and the weight decides the detector; a weight other than the street's is a statement of what a miss costs, and it should be made as one. The best share for a given target is proportional to the target's weight on a case times the spread of its losses. And the number the rare slice reports depends on which pedestrians count.

Interview prompts

  • A class is 1% of production traffic and you train on a set where it is 25%. What changes in the model, and what do you do about it? (§3 — its odds for that class are overrated by the ratio of prior odds, 40.6 here; weight by p/q to restore the street, or re-set the threshold if only the label prior moved.)
  • Define the effective sample size of a weighted sample and say what it assumes. (§4 — (Σw)²/Σw², the size of an unweighted sample with the same variance of the mean; it reads the weights alone, so it is exact only when every frame's loss has the same spread.)
  • Oversampling improved your slice metric and hurt the overall metric. Is that a bug? (§5 — no: an unweighted mixture trains for a world where the case is c times as important; the trade is real and has a floor on the gain.)
  • What decides the trained model, the share of renders or the weights? (§5 — the weights; three shares weighted to the same target gave one detector, and the share only changed the effective sample size.)
  • Why can a program draw a rare case on purpose when a data campaign cannot? (§2 — it conditions its own scene law, here by rejection on a forced step-out, at one render per kept frame.)
  • How would you choose the share of renders for a rare case? (§6 — fix the target weight first, then spend in proportion to target weight times spread; at the exam's cost that is the natural frequency.)
  • Why can no weight repair a case the generator never draws? (§3 — a weight multiplies a probability that is zero; the importance-sampling identity needs the proposal to cover the target.)
  • A benchmark changes its visibility rule and your slice metric moves 15 points. Is the model worse? (§7 — no: a rule is a cut on the miss-versus-visible-fraction curve and the reported number is the average above the cut.)
