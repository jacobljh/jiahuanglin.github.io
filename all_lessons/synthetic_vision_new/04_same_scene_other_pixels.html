Same scene, other pixels

Lesson 3 gave the program the street's camera and took the miss rate from 80% to 66%, but every frame it draws is still lit by one sun behind the shuttle at one brightness. The street shows the same scenes at dusk, in glare and under cloud, and the label does not care: a pedestrian is where the geometry puts him, whatever the light. This lesson asks which of the light's ten parameters the detector has to be shown varying, and how far. One number carries the price in this lab, the brightness, because the camera turns light into noise. Its range can be read from the sky in unlabelled frames; widening it past that range costs frames and then breaks the program's own labels; and the repair lands within two points of the exact one. It cannot see the sun's bearing or the clothes, and it leaves the scene untouched.

The thesis, here
A nuisance is a factor the label does not depend on, and a learner becomes invariant to it only by being shown it vary. How far to show it is the range the world has, read from pixels whose content is known, and no wider: beyond that the extra frames are paid for in data, and beyond a point the pedestrian is no longer in the pixels at all. Which nuisances are worth showing is itself something to measure, on the program's own free labels.
Linear position
Forced by: Flat frames of a grey card gave the camera's full well and read noise, an edge gave its blur, and logs gave its exposure control; added to the program, they take the miss rate from 80% to 66%, and the program's pictures now carry the grain and softness of the real camera. A calibrated camera still sees only the light the program gives it: one sun behind the camera, white vans, bright shirts, a blue sky. The real street shows the same scenes at dusk, in glare and under cloud. What should be allowed to vary between two pictures of the same scene, and what must not?
New idea: a nuisance is a factor the label ignores, and invariance to it is bought by showing the learner the range the world has, no wider. Of the light's ten parameters only the brightness moves this exam; its range, read from the sky of unlabelled frames, takes the miss rate from 65.8% to 0.0%, and a range three times wider costs 0.0 points at 800 frames.
Forces next: Randomizing the light over the range measured from probe pixels of sky and road in unlabelled frames takes the miss rate from 66% to 62%, and randomizing wider than that costs data without buying accuracy. With the pixels right, what the program draws starts to matter in a way it did not: a scene repair that was worth a little over 5 points while the pictures were wrong is worth 15 now. And the scene is where the program is silent about whole kinds of case: every pedestrian it drew stood in the open and within 16 m. Which real cases does the scene never draw, and how do we count them?
The plan
Six moves. (1) Draw one scene under many lights and see that the label does not move. (2) Ask the detector which lights it can tell apart. (3) Find where a nuisance stops being one: the pedestrian leaves the pixels. (4) Read the street's range off the sky and the road of unlabelled frames. (5) Sweep the width of that range: where the program's own error says stop, what invariance costs, which groups of parameters carry the price. (6) Price the program built from evidence against the exact one, and see what it leaves.

1 · One scene, many lights

The program of Lesson 3 has the street's camera and the light of SIM0: a sun 55° high behind the camera, one brightness, a white van, bright shirts, a blue sky. One number shows how far that is from the street, and it is read at the camera. Auto-exposure raises its gain when the scene is dark, up to a cap of 8. Over 1,000 logged street frames the gain runs from 1.9 to 8 and sits at the cap in 26% of them; over the program's frames it runs from 3.1 to 3.9 and is never at the cap. The detector has never met a frame taken with the exposure control at its limit.

What must not vary is what the label is made of. The label is a function of the scene and the pinhole: which pixels the pedestrian covers, how much of him shows, whether he counts. Light appears nowhere in it. Draw 8 scenes, each under 8 looks drawn from the street's range of brightness: the masks of one scene are identical to the last bit (the largest difference is 0), and so are the labels and the areas, while the pictures are not. So all ten are nuisances in the precise sense of the word: factors the label does not depend on. The theory of augmentation says the same from the other side: averaging over transformations is safe exactly when they leave the data distribution, and so the label relation, invariant, and a transformation that changes the label breaks the premise (Chen, Dobriban and Lee, 2020).

A learner cannot be told which factors are nuisances. It fits whatever separates its training pictures, and if the light never changes, "what this pedestrian looks like in this light" separates them as well as "what a pedestrian is". It can only be shown, by pictures of one scene in other light, that the factor moves while the label does not. Every factor shown costs frames, so the first question is which of the ten the detector needs.

Why brightness and not the sun? The detector reads bars and steps in both polarities, and a pedestrian against a road is an edge whichever of them is lit. A sun in another direction moves the light and shade about; the edge is still there. Brightness changes something the filters cannot read around, the noise. A pixel of radiance L collects ev·L electrons and its relative noise is √(ev·L + r2) / (ev·L), with ev = 600 electrons per unit radiance and read noise r = 3 electrons (Lesson 3): at L = 0.30 that is 0.0%, and at 0.045, the same road at brightness 0.15, it is 0.0%, 0.0 times as much. Auto-exposure lifts the picture back to the same average level and the noise with it. The light reaches the picture by two doors, pattern, which this detector ignores, and noise, which it does not. That is a statement about this learner. A project runs the same test on its own, a handful of trainings on free labels, and it tells which nuisances to show.

3 · Where a nuisance stops being one

Why not show every nuisance as widely as it goes? Because at some brightness the pedestrian is no longer in the picture. Make that precise. The signal of a pedestrian is the picture with him minus the same picture without him, as the camera records it (blur included), with value si at pixel i and channel. The camera adds independent noise of variance σi2 = (ev·Li + r2) / ev2 there, with Li the radiance behind the pixel. The signal's length in units of noise is the contrast-to-noise ratio:

CNR = √( Σi si2 / σi2 )

It is the detectability d′ of an observer who knows the signal exactly, and no detector does better: a matched filter run on 3 scenes of the lab's own noisy frames measures a d′ within 0% of the CNR. A frame has K = 576 cells that could alarm, and one empty frame in ten may alarm, so each cell may alarm with probability 1 − 0.91/K; for Gaussian noise that is a threshold of z = 3.56 noise sd, which a simulation of the largest of 576 normals confirms. The ideal observer finds a pedestrian of CNR c with probability Φ(c − z): half of them at c = 3.56, nearly all at 8 (0%).

The detector is not ideal; it does not know the pedestrian's clothes or where he stands. Grade the detector of the street's range of brightness on its own frames and bin its pedestrians by CNR:

4 · The street's range, from pixels whose content is known

The street's range of brightness is not in any label. It is in the pixels whose content is known without one, in frames a fleet already logs (cost: nothing beyond the logging). Three bands qualify. The top row of the picture is sky, because the far wall ends below it; its radiance is the brightness times the sky's colour. The bottom rows are road, whose radiance is the brightness times the ground albedo times the road's light, ambient + (1 − ambient) · sin(elevation). Rows 3 to 6 are the far wall. Read each band through the calibrated camera, DNγ / (κ · gain) with the logged gain, which turns the picture back into scene radiance (SV.evidence.linear), and take the median of the band, so that a tree, a van or a pole over part of it does not move it.

The sky tells two things. Its colour, the ratios of its channels with the level divided out, names which of three skies the frame shows (blue, grey or warm; the table of their colours comes from the program, which is the calibration standard of its own probes), correctly in 0% of the frames it classifies (0% of them), at shares 0, 0 and 0% for blue, grey and warm. Its level divided by the level the program's probe reports for that sky at brightness 1 is the frame's brightness; against the hidden truth (a lab privilege) it is 0.95 of it (middle 80% from 0.90 to 0.99), a few per cent low because the blur leaks the darker wall into the top row. If the brightness is uniform on [lo, hi] its p-quantile is lo + p(hi − lo), so the 5% and 95% quantiles give the whole range and a few outliers move neither:

The road tells less. Dividing its level by the brightness leaves G = ground albedo × road light, a product of three things the road cannot separate. Take a road of albedo 0.30 under a 55° sun and ambient light 0.45, and one of albedo 0.45 under a 30° sun and ambient light 0.20: their road light is the same, 0.270 and 0.270, the probe reads the road alike (ratio 1.00), and the wall behind them differs by a factor 1.0. The probes constrain these parameters jointly and identify only the brightness and the sky. The sun's bearing has no probe at all, and neither have the clothes: a pedestrian is in 2% of the frames. The principle is to match the distribution of what you can observe, and only through a cause you can attribute. That is the idea of adjusting a simulator's parameter distribution until simulated observations match real ones (Chebotar et al., 2019, who matched trajectories from the real robot); here the observation is a handful of pixel statistics from unlabelled frames.

Road not taken · randomise as widely as it goes
Tobin et al. (2017) took this road. They randomised lights, textures, distractors, the camera and the noise widely, trained on rendered images alone (no real image of the task), and localised objects in real webcam images to 1.5 cm on average; they found the result poor with fewer than a thousand distinct textures at ten thousand images, and still improving up to about fifty thousand. A wide range is how one covers a world one has not measured, and it pays when frames are plentiful and the learner has the capacity to be invariant to all of it. Here, with 1,600 frames, the street's look three times wider costs 0.0 points of exam against the street's width and raises the program's own miss rate to 0.0%, and at 200 frames it costs 0.0 points (§5). Once the probes have measured the range, width beyond it is data spent on frames no street produces.

What this lesson did not do
It repaired the light for one learner and one exam: the matrix of §2 says that this detector's filters read around the sun's direction and not around the noise, and a network with other filters would give another matrix (the test, not the verdict, is what carries over). The range came from a sky and a road; the sun's bearing, the clothes and the stripes have no probe in unlabelled frames, and the estimate assumes a uniform spread, which another distribution would change. It left the scene alone: which pedestrians the program never draws, and how many, is Lesson 5, and how often the rare ones should be drawn is Lesson 6. It did not touch the camera (Lesson 3) or what counts as a pedestrian (Lesson 7), and it never trained on a real label: what a few real labels would add is Lesson 10.

Common mistakes / failure modes

"If the label does not depend on a factor, randomise it"
Randomise what the learner is not already invariant to. The three bright looks of the matrix are one look to this detector (at most 0.0% apart), so showing it the sun's direction buys nothing, and each extra range of the whole look adds frames without accuracy (§2, §5).
"Wider is safer"
Not once the nuisances are widened together: the whole look at three times the street's width has an exam of 0.0% against 0.0% at width 0.5, and its own miss rate leaves 0.0% for 0.0% (§5).
"A pedestrian in the label is a pedestrian in the pixels"
Below a contrast-to-noise ratio of 0 this detector finds fewer than half of the pedestrians it is given; the label is exact and the evidence for it is not there (§3).
"Match every probe and the program matches the street"

"The meter says the program is not the street yet, so keep widening"

"Read the range from the extremes of a few frames"

"Train on the best light to be safe"

Takeaway
A nuisance is a factor the label does not depend on, and all ten parameters of the light are nuisances: re-drawing a scene under another light leaves its mask, label and area bit for bit the same. A learner becomes invariant to a nuisance only by being shown it vary, and which nuisances are worth showing is measured, on free labels, by training under one look and grading under another: for this detector the sun's direction does not matter and the brightness does, because the camera turns brightness into noise. A nuisance may be shown only as widely as the pedestrian stays in the pixels: the contrast-to-noise ratio of the signal against the camera's noise law says when he leaves, the detector's own hit rate puts the line at 0, and the program can count its pedestrians below it with no street. The range comes from pixels whose content is known: the sky gives the brightness (0.00 to 0.00 from 1,000 logs, close to the street's 0.12 to 1.00) and the road only a product of three causes, which must not be matched through one of them. The evidence-built program misses 0.0%, within 0.0 points of the exact-stage look, and the repair is mostly a matter of level, not width. What the repaired pixels leave is the scene.

  • What is a nuisance factor, and how does a learner come to depend on one? (§1 — a factor the label does not depend on; the learner fits whatever separates its training pictures, so if the factor never changes it is as good a feature as the object.)
  • How do you decide which nuisances to randomise? (§2 — train under one setting and grade under another on free labels; randomise those whose off-diagonal miss rate is large and leave the rest.)
  • Why can randomising a nuisance more widely make the synthetic data worse? (§3 — when widths multiply, some frames hold a pedestrian whose contrast-to-noise ratio is below what the detector can use: label noise, and a heavier false-alarm tail that raises the threshold for every frame.)
  • How would you set the range of a lighting parameter without labelled real data? (§4 — read pixels whose content is known (sky, road) through the calibrated camera, divide out what the camera cancels, and take a robust range of the resulting statistic.)
  • What can a sky-and-road probe not tell you? (§4 — the road's level is a product of ground albedo, ambient fraction and sun elevation, so it constrains them jointly; the sun's bearing and the clothes have no probe.)
  • Why is the program's own error a useful stopping rule for widening, and why is the unlabelled alarm rate not? (§5 — own error rises when frames leave the pixels; the alarm rate keeps falling as the program looks more like the street, whether or not the labels improve.)
  • When does a wide, unmeasured randomisation make sense? (§5, road not taken — when frames are plentiful and the learner has the capacity to be invariant to all of it, as in Tobin et al. (2017); with 1,600 frames a measured range is cheaper.)
