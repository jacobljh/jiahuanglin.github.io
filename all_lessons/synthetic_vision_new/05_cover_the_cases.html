Cover the cases

With the light repaired, what the program draws begins to matter: the scene repair that bought 5.5 points while the pictures were wrong buys 14.8 now. But a scene can be silent about whole situations, and a detector is right only where its data has pedestrians. This lesson turns "which cases does the scene never draw?" into a count. A few hundred outlined frames and the ground plane turn each pedestrian's foot row into a distance and his outline's neighbours into a hiding test; the same ruler on the program's own frames gives the other side of the table. The program draws 42% of the street's pedestrians. The count says which kinds of case are missing, not how often the rare ones occur.

The thesis, here
A model can be right only where its training data has mass. A case is a cell of the situations the exam asks about; the street's pedestrians counted per cell, with a ruler built from a small labelled sample and the ground plane, beside the program's own count, show which cells the program never fills. The labelled sample sees the pedestrians, unlabelled frames see the background, and the exam needs both.
Linear position
Forced by: Randomizing the light over the range measured from probe pixels of sky and road in unlabelled frames takes the miss rate from 66% to 62%, and randomizing wider than that costs data without buying accuracy. With the pixels right, what the program draws starts to matter in a way it did not: a scene repair that was worth a little over 5 points while the pictures were wrong is worth 15 now. And the scene is where the program is silent about whole kinds of case: every pedestrian it drew stood in the open and within 16 m. Which real cases does the scene never draw, and how do we count them?
New idea: a case is a cell of the situations the exam asks about, and a detector is right only in cells its data fills: count the street's pedestrians per cell with a ruler built from outlines and the ground plane, and the program's own per cell. The program fills cells holding 42% of the street's pedestrians; a few hundred labelled frames read this, and no count says how often a cell is drawn.
Forces next: Counting cases on a small labelled sample, with each pedestrian's distance read off the foot row of its mask, shows that the scene program draws only 41% of the street's pedestrians: 54% of them stand beyond the 16 m it ever drew and 17% step out from behind a van it never lets hide anyone. Repairing the scene takes the miss rate from 62% to 47%, with the distance range and the step-out worth 5 and 3 points alone and 10 together. But a case is a continuum, not a box: the step-outs closest to the shuttle, which leave the least time to stop, occur in about one frame in 140, so a training set of 1,600 frames holds about eleven of them. How should a generator spend its renders on cases that rarely happen, and what does that do to the statistics the model learns?
The plan
Seven moves. (1) See a detector fail where its data has no pedestrians. (2) Build a ruler for cases from outlines and the ground plane. (3) Count the street's cases and the program's, and read off the coverage. (4) Ask how many labelled frames the count needs. (5) Price the components of the scene stage, and see which half the exam sees and which half a label-free meter sees. (6) Repair the scene from the evidence and price it against the exact stage. (7) Find the case that a count of kinds cannot see.

1 · A detector is right where its data has pedestrians

The entry baton compares two prices of one repair: the street's scene bought 5.5 points while the camera and light were wrong and buys 14.8 now (with the street's camera and light, abba misses 61.6%; with the street's scene as well, bbba, 46.8%). It also records one thing about the program's scene: every pedestrian it drew stood in the open and within 16 m. The data also decides something no later stage can undo. Lesson 2 said that a case the scene never draws cannot be supplied downstream; the table cells now show it. Repairing the camera and the light took the open pedestrians' miss rate from 78.9% (aaaa) to 56.2% (abba) and left the step-outs' at 87.0% and 87.4%.

Call a case a cell of the situations the exam asks about. Benchmarks cut their exams this way already: the Caltech benchmark (Dollár et al., 2012) reports pedestrians separately by pixel height, near at 80 px or more, medium from 30 to 80 px and far at 30 px or less, and KITTI (Geiger et al., 2012) reports easy, moderate and hard objects, defined by box height, occlusion and truncation. Here a cell is a distance band and whether the pedestrian is hidden. Which cells has the street filled that the program never does, and what share of the street's pedestrians stands in them? That share is the coverage: the share of the street's pedestrians whose cell the program draws at all. To count it, every pedestrian needs a coordinate, and a project does not have the lab's.

2 · A ruler for cases, from outlines and the ground plane

What a project holds. A labelled sample: in each of n frames a person outlines every pedestrian the written rule counts (at least 15% of his silhouette showing) and every vehicle, and nothing more: no distance, no depth, no flag for hidden. The cost is the number of outlines, 1.3 a frame on average (a van in 80% of the frames, a counted pedestrian in 43%), so 100 frames are about 130 outlines; Lesson 10 prices a labelled frame against everything else. An outline is binary: a pixel belongs to the pedestrian when at least half of it does. The program's frames have the same outlines for nothing.

Distance from the foot row. The camera is hc = 1.4 m above the road, its focal length is f = 83.1 px and the horizon lies on row V0 = 9, rows counting down. A road point at depth z (metres along the axis) lies hc/z below the horizon in slope, so it falls on row

v = V0 + f·hc / z    ⇔    z = f·hc / (v − V0)

with f·hc = 116.3 px·m. The lowest row of a pedestrian's outline, the foot row, is where his feet meet the road: a foot on row 16.75 is 15.0 m away. An outline says only which row the foot is in, a half-row uncertainty Δv = ½. Differentiating the law, |dz| = z²·dv/(f·hc), so

Δz / z = z·Δv / (f·hc)   =   3.4% at 8 m,  6.9% at 16 m,  10.3% at 24 m

The ruler loses resolution in proportion to distance. A brute-force check places a pedestrian at every 0.01 m, lets the renderer draw him and reads the foot row off his outline (the lab's rule: a pixel is outlined when at least half of it is pedestrian, which here puts the foot more than a quarter row into it), and the rows read as follows. Another outlining convention shifts every reading by up to half a row, 2.5 m at 24 m: one reason Lesson 7 treats conventions as part of the label.

Against the true position of the pedestrian's axis a reading is off by 0.3, 0.5, 0.9 and 1.4 m (standard deviation) in the four bands of §3, and 0.2 m short at close range, because the foot row sees the front of the legs. The same ruler on the program's own frames, where the truth is free, measures that offset; §6 uses it.

Hidden or not. Only a van can hide a pedestrian in this street. One who steps out from behind it shows a strip of himself beside the van's silhouette, and the van is nearer. Both facts are in the outlines: his touches the van's, and the van's reaches lower in the picture than his feet, since nearer things stand lower. That test needs no distance. On the exam pedestrians of the test set it finds 228 of the 228 who step out and calls 0 of the others. How much of him shows comes from area, not width: at 16 m a whole pedestrian is 2.6 px wide, an outline is 2, 3 or 4 columns, and width says almost nothing, while area adds up the pixels. The outline's area against the area a whole pedestrian has at that distance, A0·(f/z)², with A0 = 0.75 m² the median of area·(z/f)² over the open pedestrians of the same sample, reads 0.95 for open pedestrians and 0.75 for step-outs, and follows the true visible fraction of the step-outs with correlation 0.83.

Road not taken · read the distance off the pedestrian's height
A pedestrian is about 1.7 m tall, so his height in pixels, h, gives z = 1.7f/h with no camera height and no horizon. It attracts because it needs less, and it needs a height, which varies: here from 1.5 to 1.9 m, 7% (standard deviation) at every distance, to which the outline's two ends add half a pixel each on a figure 14 px tall at 10 m and 6 px at 24 m. Against the truth the foot row's relative error is 3% in the nearest band and 6% in the farthest; the height's is 9% and 13%, with a height constant that has to be calibrated against the foot row anyway.

3 · Counting: the street's cases and the program's

A case needs two coordinates. The first is the distance band, with its edges where the foot row changes: 12.6, 16.05 and 22.2 m. The 16 m edge falls on a row boundary, so the program's own limit is read exactly: its pedestrians, between 8 and 16 m, read in rows 16 and below and never in the bands beyond. The second is whether he steps out. Four bands and two classes make eight cells. Run the ruler over a pool of 40,000 street frames, 17,260 counted pedestrians, which no project has labelled but the lab can draw, and over 12,000 of the program's frames:

That is the ruler's resolution, not a bias to correct. No project can count 40,000 frames. What do a few hundred give?

4 · How many labelled frames does the count need?

A cell holding a share s of the street's pedestrians appears in a frame with probability p = s·0.43, a frame holding 0.43 counted pedestrians on average. It is absent from n independent frames with probability (1 − p)n ≈ e−np. To see it at least once with 95% confidence, e−np = 0.05, so np = ln 20 = 3.0: about 3/p frames, the rule of three. Frames of one drive are correlated, so this is a best case. The commonest cell (16 to 22 m, open: 31.5% of the pedestrians, 13.6% of the frames) needs 21 frames. The rarest of the eight (the nearest band, stepping out: 2.2% of the pedestrians, one frame in 105) needs 314; 100 labelled frames lack it with probability 38%, 400 with 2%.

The coverage is a proportion of the m = 0.43n pedestrians a sample holds, so its standard deviation is √(c(1−c)/m). Drawing n frames from the pool over and over gives the same:

5 · The two halves of the scene

The street's scene differs from the program's in five settings: the distance range of the open pedestrians (8 to 24 m against 8 to 16), the share who step out (28% against none), the pedestrian's size, the van (anywhere from 5 to 18 m, sometimes absent) and the clutter of poles and trees (twice as much, a tenth of the poles red). Adding the street's value of one setting at a time to abba prices them inside the stage, as Lesson 2 priced the stages, and settles what §1 left open: whether a group improves because its own data arrives. Read the groups first: the distance range moves the far open pedestrians and not the step-outs, the step-outs move the step-outs and not the far ones. A group improves when its own data arrives, which §1 could not show. The van and the pedestrian's size, left out of the table, move the exam by 0.0 and 0.4 points. Then the last column. The distance range, the step-outs and the size leave the meter at 25%; the clutter and the van take it to 15% and 14%. An unlabelled frame holds a pedestrian one time in fifty, so the program's pedestrians cannot show in it. The exam sees the pedestrian cases, the meter sees the background, and a repair guided by one is blind to half the stage.

The settings compound, for the third time in the series. Distance and step-outs are worth 5.0 and 2.7 points alone and 9.9 together, 2.2 more than their sum. The clutter is worth 1.4 on the program and 6.9 on the program whose pedestrians are right (51.7% to 44.8%), and the van, worth nothing alone, adds 0.2 once the clutter is there. A pedestrian in more places gives the detector more places to mistake a pole for him, and the clutter is where it learns not to. A repair judged where it was cheapest to try, on the program as it stood, would have been dropped.

How far should the background be pushed? With the pedestrian cases repaired, clutter at 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 poles and trees a frame (the street has 1.0; one seed each) gives alarm rates of 25.4, 18.7, 16.3, 13.2 and 8.8% and miss rates of 54.1, 49.7, 48.6, 47.2 and 45.8%. The meter keeps falling past the street's own value and crosses its nominal 10% near twice the street's clutter, because the van, the red poles and the size are also wrong and it charges their gap to the one knob it was given. It says that the background is incomplete and by how much, not in which setting: a repair with no stopping rule, which §6 leaves alone.

6 · Repair from the evidence, and price it

The labelled sample sets two settings. The distance range is the nearest and the farthest open pedestrian the ruler reads, each corrected by the ruler's offset, −0.09 m, measured on the program's own frames run with a deliberately wide range (8 to 26 m), where the truth is free. The step-out probability needs care. The sample shows the share of step-outs among the pedestrians counted, ŝ = 17.3%, and the program must show the same share through the same ruler. Counting favours the open pedestrian (a strip has to reach 6 px²), so the odds of a counted step-out are a fraction κ of the odds of drawing one, and κ is measured on the program's own frames: 0.56. Matching odds(ŝ) = κ·e/(1 − e) gives e = odds(ŝ)/(odds(ŝ) + κ) = 0.27, within a point of the street's 0.28, which the sample never saw. The van, the clutter and the size stay the program's. Each training seed gets its own labelled sample, so the evidence's variation is in the spread.

7 · A kind is not a frequency

The repaired program draws every cell, and drawing a cell is the least a program owes it. A cell is a box several metres deep, and the case the shuttle has least time for sits at the near end of one. Take the pedestrian who steps out from behind a van with his axis nearer than 12 m. The street is a program, so the lab can count this without rendering a frame: of 1,000,000 scene draws, 8,136 hold one, a frame in 123. A training set of 1,600 frames should hold 13.0 of them, and the three sets of the exact-stage program hold 11, 13 and 16. The ruler's nearest-band cell is the same case seen through the exam's counting rule and the ruler's row: one frame in 105.

To grade a detector on the case itself the lab draws it on purpose: scenes of the street's program with a step-out nearer than 12 m, kept by rejection at the scene-draw level (a scene costs microseconds, one in 17 is kept, and only the kept ones are rendered). A project cannot do this. It gives 1,200 frames, 970 with a pedestrian the exam counts. The detector trained with the street's scene misses 56.6% of them and 9.8% of the pedestrians who stand in the open nearer than 12 m (1,200 frames drawn the same way); the program-trained detector misses 77.0% and 18.5%. The program has been told about the case and the detector has seen about thirteen of it: the cell is covered and the case is not learned. Waiting does not help. To hold 100 of them a program that draws as the street does needs 12,300 frames, and almost all of those frames show pedestrians the detector already finds.

What this lesson did not do
It counted kinds, not frequencies: a cell is drawn or it is not, and how often is the next lesson's question. The lab's program already contains the mechanics of stepping out, at probability 0; a project whose program lacks the mechanism needs a person to write it, and no ruler designs it. The hiding test is exact here because a van is the only occluder, and the sample is labelled at the street's own frequencies. Where step-outs fall follows the van, which the sample does not set. The clutter belongs to the meter, which cannot size it (§5); what an outline counts as the pedestrian is a convention of the label (Lesson 7); and what a labelled frame costs against what it buys is Lesson 10's.

Common mistakes / failure modes

"A detector that works at 10 m will work at 20 m"
It answers a case from the examples near it. The program-trained detector misses 80.8% of the pedestrians beyond the 16 m it drew and 32.1% within (§1).
"A missing case can be fixed downstream"
Repairing camera and light took the open pedestrians' miss from 78.9% to 56.2% and left the step-outs at 87.4% (§1).
"A label-free meter will tell me the scene is incomplete"
It is blind to the pedestrian: the distance range and the step-outs leave the alarm rate at 26.0% (§5).
"A foot row is as exact at 24 m as at 8 m"
A half row is 3.4% of the distance at 8 m and 10.3% at 24 m (§2).
"A few labelled frames are enough to know the cases"
For common cells, yes: 21 frames. The nearest step-out cell needs 314 (§4).
"Repair each case and add the gains"
5.0 + 2.7 is not 9.9, and the clutter is worth 1.4 alone and 6.9 after (§5).

Checkpoint exercise

Try it
In a labelled frame the pedestrian's outline ends on row 20 and touches the van's, whose outline reaches row 23. (a) How far away is he, within what interval, and is he stepping out? (b) A cell holds 3% of the street's pedestrians. How many labelled frames make the sample hold at least one of them with 95% confidence? Answer: (a) the foot is on row 20, so v = 20.75 and z = 116.3/(20.75 − 9) = 9.9 m, really between 9.5 and 10.3 m. The van's outline reaches lower than his feet, so the van is nearer, and it touches him: he steps out. (b) A frame holds 0.43 counted pedestrians, so p = 0.03 × 0.43 = 0.0129 and n = ln 20/(−ln(1−p)) = 232 frames; 3/p gives 233.

Where this points next

The ruler turns "which cases does the scene never draw?" into a table of eight cells and a coverage of 42%; a few hundred labelled frames read it to 3.5 points (400 frames), and a program whose pedestrians are set from it misses as often as one set from the street's own values. That tells a program which cells to fill, not how often. The case the shuttle has least time for, a step-out nearer than 12 m, occurs in one frame in 123; a training set of 1,600 frames holds about 13 of them; and the detector trained on it misses 57% of them, against 10% of the pedestrians who stand as near in the open. A program that draws as the street does needs 12,300 frames to hold a hundred of them, and nothing in the street lets it ask for fewer. The program can ask: it can draw the case on purpose. How should a generator spend its renders on cases that rarely happen, and what does that do to the statistics the model learns?

Takeaway
A detector is right only where its data has pedestrians: the program-trained detector misses 32% of the pedestrians within the 16 m the program drew and 81% beyond it, and no repair of the camera or the light moved the step-outs, which the program never drew. A case is a cell of the situations the exam asks about, and a project can count the street's cells from a few hundred outlined frames and the ground plane: the foot row gives the distance, to 3.4% at 8 m and 10.3% at 24 m; a nearer vehicle whose outline touches his says he is stepping out; and the same ruler on the program's own frames gives its side of the table. The program fills cells that hold 42% of the street's pedestrians. A cell seen once in p frames needs about 3/p labelled frames: 21 for the commonest cell, 314 for the rarest of the eight. The exam sees the pedestrian cases and the label-free meter sees the background, and the settings compound (5.0 and 2.7 alone, 9.9 together). A cell drawn is not a case learned: the nearest step-outs are one frame in 123, about 13 in a training set, and 57% of them are missed.

Interview prompts

  • How would you find out which situations a synthetic data generator never produces? (§2, §3 — outline a few hundred real frames, give every pedestrian a coordinate from the outlines and the geometry, run the same ruler on the generator's own frames, and compare the share of each cell.)
  • How far away is a pedestrian in a monocular frame, and how accurate is the reading? (§2 — z = f·hc/(v − V0) from the foot row on the ground plane; a half-row error grows linearly with distance.)
  • Why can't a better camera model recover a case the scene never draws? (§1 — repairs downstream change the pixels of the cases that exist; the step-out miss stayed put while the open pedestrians' fell by 22.7 points.)
  • How many labelled samples make you 95% sure of seeing a case of probability p? (§4 — (1 − p)n ≈ e−np = 0.05 gives n ≈ 3/p, a best case, since frames of one drive are correlated.)
  • Why can a label-free domain-gap meter miss the most important gap? (§5 — pedestrians are rare in unlabelled frames, so the alarm rate saw the clutter and the van and stayed put when the distance range and the step-outs were repaired.)
  • Two repairs each help a little and help more together. Why? (§5 — the detector learns in the program's distribution, so a repair pays most where the others have made it matter: the clutter, worth 1.4 alone, is worth 6.9 once the pedestrians are right.)
  • The generator draws every kind of case and the model still fails on one. What do you check? (§7 — how many examples of it the training set holds: the nearest step-outs are one frame in 123, about 13 in 1,600 frames.)
