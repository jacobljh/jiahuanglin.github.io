Real robot data: the expensive kernel

Every technique in these three tracks is an attempt to need less of this. It is worth knowing exactly how much of it exists, because the number is small enough that seeing it written down changes what you consider a reasonable plan.

Where we are
Video is inexhaustible and has no action column. Egocentric video is a thousand times smaller and has a partial one. Simulation is unlimited within an authored support and contested exactly where it matters. What is left is a real body doing the real task — the dearest row in lesson 02's ledger, and the only one with no substitutes.

1 · The inventory

Here is a fact worth sitting with. If you gathered every recording ever publicly released of a real robot manipulating a real object — every lab, every institution, every collaboration, all of it — you would have a few thousand hours. A language model reads more text than that in its first minute of training. This is the whole world's supply of the one thing that cannot be substituted, and it fits on a small number of disks.

Take the whole public corpus at, generously, ten thousand hours. Convert at lesson 01's rate:

10⁴ h × 7.37 × 10⁶ tok/h ≈ 7.4 × 10¹⁰ tokens

and set it beside a modern language corpus of about 1.5 × 10¹³ tokens:

1.5 × 10¹³ / 7.4 × 10¹⁰ ≈ 200× less, for the entire field's pooled output

Do it for DROID alone — 350 hours, roughly 2.6 × 10⁹ tokens — and the ratio is about 5,700×. And against web video's ~7 × 10¹⁵ tokens, all public robot data is about one hundred-thousandth of one percent.

the number to internaliseThe entire world's publicly available real robot interaction data is smaller than a language model's training corpus by two orders of magnitude, and smaller than the video corpus by five. Robot learning is not operating in a data-scarce corner of a data-rich field. It is operating five orders of magnitude away from the regime in which its own architectures were discovered.

Notice the bottom bar. V-JEPA 2-AC's grounding stage is around 62 hours — a few hundred trajectories — and it produced zero-shot image-goal pick-and-place. So the ledger contains both a discouraging fact and an encouraging one: the corpus is tiny, and tiny grounding corpora demonstrably work when they sit on top of a large free one. Those are the two halves of the same observation, and which one you emphasise determines whether you conclude "wait for more data" or "get the architecture right."

2 · Why this corpus grows so slowly, and will continue to

Track 2 lesson 04 gave the mechanism; here it is as a growth rate. One operator produces about 60 episodes an hour. So the corpus grows in proportion to people doing nothing but teleoperating robots, and there is no mechanism by which it accumulates otherwise. Contrast the two other rows:

web video
Grows for free, forever
Accumulates as a by-product of billions of people using cameras. Nobody funds it, and it grows anyway.
simulation
Grows with engineers
Bounded by authoring hours, but each authored scenario yields unlimited experience — so it grows superlinearly in effort.
real robot data
Grows strictly linearly in human hours
One hour of teleoperation is one hour of data. There is no leverage, no amortisation, no by-product. It is the only row with no multiplier.

That is the structural reason to expect the gap in lesson 00's inversion to widen rather than close. And it is why the community's response was pooling — Open X-Embodiment exists because sharing was the only available multiplier when the collection rate itself cannot be multiplied. Sixty datasets from thirty-four labs is a coordination achievement substituting for a technological one.

3 · Reading the corpus honestly, in three corrections

The headline numbers overstate what is usable, for three separate reasons that all reduce the effective size.

  1. It is not your body. Of OXE's 1M+ trajectories, the fraction on your exact robot with your gripper and your camera placement is small. Track 2 lesson 06 showed the trunk transfers and the action head partly does not, so the effective size for control on your body is far below the headline. Weight by similarity, not by count.
  2. It is not diverse. Trajectory counts include heavy repetition — the same task in the same scene many times over. Report effective count after clustering; a dataset with 100k trajectories over 800 distinct scene-task combinations behaves like an 800-item dataset with good error bars. Lesson 11 develops this.
  3. It has almost no force. Most of the pooled corpus is RGB plus end-effector pose. Torque, wrist force, and tactile are rare or absent, so for lesson 02's contact capability the effective size of the entire public corpus is close to zero hours — which is lesson 08's subject.
And one thing that is better than it looks
DROID's design is worth singling out: 564 scenes across 52 buildings, three synchronised RGB streams with calibration and depth, and language instructions, collected by 50 people. That scene and operator diversity is exactly what makes it disproportionately useful relative to its 350 hours — diversity, not duration, is what a robot corpus is short of. A thousand hours in one cell is worth less than three hundred hours across five hundred scenes, which is a useful thing to know before planning your own collection.
Takeaway
All public real robot interaction data is on the order of 10³–10⁴ hours — Open X-Embodiment's 1M+ trajectories pooled from 60 datasets across 34 labs, DROID's 76k trajectories ≈ 350 hours from 13 institutions over a year. Converted to tokens that is about 200× less than a language corpus for the whole field's output, and 5,700× less for DROID alone; against web video it is a hundred-thousandth of a percent. The reason it grows so slowly is structural: it is the only source with no multiplier — one human hour yields one hour of data — which is why pooling was the community's response. Correct the headline three ways (not your body, not diverse, almost no force), and note the encouraging half: V-JEPA 2-AC grounded on ~62 hours. Diversity, not duration, is what these corpora are short of.

Where this points next

Across five lessons one column keeps being the difference between an abundant source and a useful one: the action. Video lacks it, egocentric video has a fragment, simulation has it perfectly within a bounded support, and real robot data has it at 10⁴ hours total. So the action label is the field's actual currency. Lesson 07 prices the two ways to obtain a grounded hour — buy it directly, or buy a few and pseudo-label an ocean — and computes where the crossover is.
