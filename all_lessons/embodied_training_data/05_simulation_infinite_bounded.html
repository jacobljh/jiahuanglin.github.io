Simulation: infinite, bounded

Simulation is the only row of the ledger with an action column, a force column, and unlimited supply. It is also the only row whose price is quoted in the wrong currency: the GPU hours are nearly free and the bill arrives as engineering time, which is what actually limits how much usable data you get.

Where we are
Four sources in, and none has an action column in your units. Simulation has one, plus forces, plus rewards and resets. Track 2 lesson 09 derived the throughput and the fidelity gaps from the training side; synthetic_vision owns simulation as a designed data-generating process. This lesson does the part neither does: price it correctly.

1 · The compute is genuinely free

Two rooms. In the first, one robot does a task once, then a person resets the mug. In the second, four thousand copies of the robot do it simultaneously, at sixty times a second, resetting themselves, reporting every contact force exactly, and never wearing out. The second room costs about a thousandth of a cent per robot-hour. Everything about that comparison is real, and it is also the wrong comparison — but we should do it properly before saying why.

Track 2 lesson 09's arithmetic, restated as a price. At 4,096 parallel environments stepping at 60 Hz:

4,096 × 60 = 245,760 steps/s  ⟹  4,096 robot-hours per wall-clock hour  ≈  170 robot-days per hour

At a cloud GPU rate around $2.50/hour, that is

$2.50 / 4,096 robot-hours ≈ $0.0006 per robot-hour

against teleoperation's ~$45. Roughly 75,000×. So on the compute axis simulation is not merely cheaper, it is free to a rounding error, and any discussion that stops here concludes "simulate everything."

2 · The bill arrives as authoring

But you cannot train on 4,096 copies of one scene. What a policy needs from simulation is coverage — many scenarios, objects, layouts, and initial conditions — and each distinct scenario has to be authored by a person: assets modelled or sourced, physical properties assigned, the scene laid out, the task's success condition scripted, randomisation ranges chosen, and the whole thing debugged until the physics does not explode.

Call that τa engineering-hours per scenario. Then the honest cost of simulated data is

cost = compute (negligible) + Nscenarios × τa × engineer rate (dominant)

Put in numbers. Two hundred scenarios at 8 engineering-hours each, at $60/hour loaded:

200 × 8 × $60 = $96,000

which is comparable to track 2's entire teleoperation programme. And unlike the compute term, this one does not amortise over hours of generated data — generating a thousand times more experience from the same 200 scenarios costs nothing extra and adds no coverage. It amortises over scenarios, which is the axis that matters.

The unit that makes simulation legible
Stop quoting simulation in dollars per robot-hour, where it wins by 75,000×, and quote it in dollars per unit of coverage — per distinct scenario, object, or initial condition. In that unit it is expensive, it competes directly with real collection, and the comparison becomes an actual decision instead of a foregone conclusion. This reframing is the whole content of the lesson.

Push scenarios to 2,000 and authoring dominates compute by orders of magnitude — and 2,000 scenarios is not a lot of coverage for a general manipulation policy. This is why simulation-heavy programmes end up with large graphics and scene-generation teams: the constraint migrated from GPUs to people, and nobody re-costed it.

3 · What is genuinely unlimited, and what is not

Hours of experience within the authored scenariosunlimited, free
Initial conditions and randomisation samples within a scenariounlimited, free
Perfect labels — poses, contacts, segmentation, forcesunlimited, free
Rewards and resetsunlimited, free
Counterfactual replay — same scene, one parameter changedunlimited, and impossible elsewhere
Distinct scenarios, objects, layouts, task definitionsbounded by engineering
Fidelity on friction, contact and deformablesbounded by physics models

Five free rows, two bounded ones — and the two bounded rows are the two that decide whether simulated data transfers. Which yields a clean statement of what simulation actually is:

what simulation isA machine that turns engineering hours into unlimited perfectly-labelled experience within a bounded support, whose accuracy is high for kinematics and free-space motion and low for exactly the contact quantities that decide manipulation tasks.

That framing prices the two ways to spend on it. Adding hours within existing scenarios: free, and adds nothing after saturation. Adding scenarios: expensive, and is the only thing that grows the support. So the marginal value of a simulation dollar is entirely about coverage, and the standard failure is generating ten million more episodes of the same two hundred scenarios and reporting it as a scaling result.

4 · Where generative video models fit

One recent shift belongs here because it changes the authoring term rather than the compute term. A learned video world model can produce plausible visual variation without anyone authoring an asset — a photoreal restyling of a crude simulated scene, or an entirely generated scenario. NVIDIA's Cosmos line frames this as Predict, Transfer and Reason, with Transfer taking a 3D simulation to photoreal appearance.

Read against §2, the significance is precise: this attacks the authoring cost, which is the binding one. If a generative model produces the visual diversity that would otherwise require hundreds of hand-authored assets, the dominant term in the cost equation falls, and simulation gets cheaper in the unit that matters rather than in the unit where it already won.

But mind which column it fills
A generative model trained on video inherits video's structural zeros. It can add appearance diversity convincingly; it does not add friction coefficients, actuator dynamics, or contact forces — those come from the physics engine either way, and lesson 02's asterisk still applies. So generative augmentation lowers the price of visual coverage and leaves the contact-fidelity bound exactly where it was. It is a large win on one axis and none on the other, and conflating the two is how a photoreal simulation ends up producing policies that still cannot insert a connector.
Takeaway
On compute, simulation is free to a rounding error: 4,096 environments at 60 Hz is 170 robot-days per wall-clock hour, about $0.0006 per robot-hour against teleoperation's $45 — some 75,000×. But that is the wrong unit. Coverage requires authored scenarios, and 200 scenarios at 8 engineering-hours each is $96,000, comparable to a whole teleoperation programme — and it amortises over scenarios, not over generated hours. So quote simulation in dollars per unit of coverage, where it competes directly with real collection. It is a machine that converts engineering hours into unlimited perfectly-labelled experience within a bounded support, accurate for free-space motion and weak on contact. Generative video augmentation attacks the authoring term — the binding one — but adds no physics.

Where this points next

Simulation's support is bounded and its contact fidelity is contested, so some hours have to be real, on a real body, in the real world. That is the most expensive row in the ledger and the one everything else is trying to avoid. Lesson 06 states exactly how much of it exists — publicly, in total — and the number is small enough to reframe the whole field.
