Two rankings that disagree

"What does this training consume the most?" is a question with two correct answers. By volume it is video, by an enormous margin. By value per dollar it is action-grounded, contact-rich, on-policy data. The two rankings are almost exactly inverted, and that inversion is not a curiosity — it is the problem the entire field is organised around.

Where we are
Both previous tracks costed a complete programme and both landed on the same wall. Track 1 found the grounded corpus capping controllability; track 2 found every human hour going to contact demonstrations and corrections, with evaluation costing 10× the training. Neither track could resolve its own bottleneck, because the bottleneck is the ledger. This track is the ledger.

1 · The answer by volume

Put the two things side by side physically. On one side, every video ever uploaded: a billion hours of hands, kitchens, streets, workshops. On the other, a shelf holding every recording ever made of a robot actually doing something, with the commands that did it — a few hundred hours. Both are "training data." One of them is going to consume essentially all your compute, and the other is going to decide whether the robot works.

Take the standard sampling plan and run the arithmetic once, because it settles the volume question immediately. A 256×256 frame cut into 16×16 patches:

(256/16)² = 256 tokens per frame  ·  at 8 fps: 2,048 tokens/s  ·  per hour: 7.37 × 10⁶ tokens

Now a minute of speech is about 150 words, call it 200 tokens, so an hour of narration is roughly 12,000 tokens. Video is

7.37 × 10⁶ / 1.2 × 10⁴ ≈ 600× denser per wall-clock hour than language

and there are on the order of 10⁹ hours of it. Multiply: about 7 × 10¹⁵ tokens of web video against roughly 1.5 × 10¹³ tokens of web text. Video exceeds the entire text corpus of the internet by two to three orders of magnitude.

answer 1 · by volumeVideo, overwhelmingly. Anything that trains on video consumes more tokens than anything that trains on text, and it is not a close comparison. If the question is which corpus the FLOPs are spent on, the answer is video and the discussion is over.

2 · The answer by value

Now ask a different question: for one more dollar, which data buys the most capability? Both prior tracks answered this without stating it as a ranking.

Web video — coverage and semantics, no action column, no forces~$0.02/h
Egocentric human video — plus partial ego-motion, still no forces~$0.5/h
Simulation — perfect actions and labels, approximate physics~$0.02/h
Pooled robot data (OXE) — real actions on other bodies~$8/h amortised
Your own teleoperation — real actions in your units~$45/h
Force and tactile — the only source of the deciding quantity~$55/h
On-policy corrections — the only thing that fixes the exponent~$60/h, and perishable

Roughly three thousand times separates the cheapest row from the dearest. And the ordering of that column is almost exactly the ordering of usefulness for the capabilities that actually gate deployment — control and contact. The cheap rows buy semantics, which saturates early. The expensive rows buy the things nothing else supplies.

answer 2 · by valueAction-grounded, contact-rich, on-policy data — with one partial exception that matters. Among finite, real corpora these rows win outright: they are four orders of magnitude smaller than the video corpus and they carry the columns the observation rows structurally lack. Simulation is the exception: it has an action column and unbounded supply at web-video prices, which is why the field leans on it so hard — and the price of that cheapness is a bounded authored support and a contact model computed by the least reliable part of the engine. Lesson 02 puts a number on it and lesson 05 spends a whole lesson on the bounds.

Note that at control the best-value row comes back marked unbounded — that is simulation, and the readout flags it because an unbounded source is not comparable to a finite corpus on either axis. The best finite entry beside it is the honest per-dollar winner among things that actually exist in fixed quantity.

Set the capability to semantics and the rankings largely agree — web video is both the biggest and among the best value, which is exactly why vision-language pretraining worked so well and arrived first. Set it to control or contact and they invert completely. That is the whole diagnostic: the capabilities where the two rankings agree are the ones the field has already solved, and the capabilities where they disagree are the frontier.

3 · Why the inversion exists, and why it will not resolve itself

It is not an accident of what has been collected so far. It is structural, and the structure has three layers.

layer 1
Observation is passive
A camera records without anyone intending to create a dataset. So observation accumulates as a by-product of civilisation, at zero marginal cost, forever.
layer 2
Action requires an instrumented actor
Recording what was done means someone deliberately logging commands. Nothing accumulates by accident, so the corpus size is bounded by intent — and by human hours.
layer 3
Force requires a body doing the task
Newtons need a sensor in contact. That means a real body performing the real task, which wears out. No passive accumulation is even conceivable.

So the price ordering follows from what each column physically requires to come into existence. That is why the gap does not close as the field matures: cameras get cheaper and more numerous, which grows the cheap column; the expensive column still needs a person or a robot, one hour at a time. The spread widens.

Takeaway
"What does embodied training consume the most?" has two correct and opposed answers. By volume: video — 7.37 M tokens per hour, ~600× denser than speech, times ~10⁹ hours, which is two to three orders of magnitude beyond all web text. By value per dollar: action-grounded, contact-rich, on-policy data — roughly 3,000× more expensive per hour and four orders of magnitude scarcer. The inversion is structural, because observation accumulates passively, action needs an instrumented actor, and force needs a body doing the real task — so the spread widens as cameras proliferate. Hence the one trade every method here implements: put the scale where data is free and the grounding where it is dear.

Where this points next

Both rankings above were asserted with a unit already chosen. That is the first thing to examine, because "the biggest corpus" means something different in bytes, in tokens, in hours, and in decision-relevant bits — and the four orderings are not the same. Lesson 01 computes all four for the same hour of video, and the spread between the largest and the smallest is about eight orders of magnitude.
