On-policy data is perishable

Every other row in this ledger is an asset: you buy it, you own it, and it is worth the same next year. One row is not. Corrections are simultaneously the most valuable data per hour in the whole table and the only data here that expires — and the rate at which it expires dictates your entire engineering cadence.

Where we are
Lesson 08's force data is a capital asset that accumulates forever. This lesson's asset does the opposite. Track 2 lesson 05 established why corrections are worth more than demonstrations — they act on the error exponent rather than its coefficient. Here we price the shelf life and derive what it forces.

1 · Why it expires, mechanically

A correction is a label at a state your current policy visited. That is the entire source of its value: it puts training data exactly where the policy is wrong, which is what converts O(εT²) into O(εT).

Now retrain. The new policy is different, so it visits a different set of states. Some overlap with the old set — the policy did not change completely — but some do not, and corrections for states the new policy never enters are training data for a distribution that no longer exists. They are not wrong; they are simply about somewhere else.

Let ρ be the overlap between consecutive checkpoints' visitation distributions — the fraction of a correction's value that survives one retraining generation. Then a correction collected j generations ago is worth

value(j) = ρj   ⟹   half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(ρ) generations

At ρ = 0.7 the half-life is about 2 generations; at ρ = 0.85 it is about 4.3. Either way it is small. A correction from ten generations back is worth 3% of a fresh one at ρ=0.7, which is to say worthless.

The uncomfortable corollary
Corrections collected during a six-month campaign and used in a single retraining run at the end have mostly depreciated before they were ever used. The team paid full price — track 2's ~$60 per hour, the most expensive row in the ledger — and consumed a fraction of the value. This is a common and entirely invisible waste: the data is all there, in the bucket, correctly labeled, and most of its value evaporated while it waited.

2 · Derive the cadence

Two forces oppose each other, so there is an optimum. Retrain often and each correction is fresh; retrain rarely and you amortise the fixed cost of a retraining cycle over more data.

Collect M corrections per week, retrain every T weeks, and let per-week retention be ρ so a correction aged t weeks is worth ρt. The total surviving value accumulated in one cycle is the integral over the cycle:

V(T) = M ∫0T ρt dt = M · (1 − ρT) / ln(1/ρ)

Note what that expression says on its own: as T grows, V saturates at M/ln(1/ρ). Collecting for longer stops adding value entirely, because the early corrections rot as fast as the late ones arrive.

Now include the overhead. Each retraining cycle costs τov weeks of engineering, validation and redeployment, during which nothing is absorbed. The throughput of useful corrections is

throughput(T) = V(T) / (T + τov) = M(1 − ρT) / ( (T + τov) · ln(1/ρ) )

which vanishes at both ends — at small T because the numerator goes to zero linearly while the overhead dominates the denominator, and at large T because the numerator saturates while the denominator grows. So there is an interior optimum T*, and it is a computable number rather than a matter of team preference.

Push the retention ρ up toward 0.98 and the optimum slides right — a slow-moving policy lets you batch. Then push the retrain overhead up instead, and the optimum also slides right, for the opposite reason: not because the data lasts longer but because each cycle costs more to run. Two controls, same direction of movement, entirely different meanings — which is exactly why this number has to be computed rather than guessed at.

At realistic settings the optimum lands at a small number of weeks. Which produces the operational rule that follows from the arithmetic rather than from taste: the retraining cadence and the collection cadence must be matched, and both must be fast. A quarterly release cycle for a policy that collects corrections daily throws away most of what it collects, and that is a scheduling decision, not a research one.

Which is why the architecture matters here
Retraining fast requires retraining cheap, and this is where track 2 lesson 11's two-rate design pays a dividend nobody designed it for. The vision-language trunk is expensive and changes slowly; the action expert is small and is what absorbs corrections. Re-fitting a 300M expert weekly is routine; re-fitting a 7B monolith weekly is not. So the architecture that solved the latency problem is also what makes the optimal cadence affordable — three independent derivations landing on the same structure.

3 · What raises ρ, and therefore extends the shelf life

Retention is not a constant of nature — it is a property of how much your policy changes per generation, so it is partly under your control.

Smaller learning-rate steps and smaller updates per generationraises ρ directly
Fine-tuning only the action expert, not the trunkraises ρ — less changes
Mixing prior-generation data into each update (replay)raises ρ by anchoring visitation
Corrections at states that are robust — near a task's invariant bottleneckhigh ρ intrinsically
Corrections at states peculiar to one checkpoint's quirkslow ρ — expire almost immediately
And the one that lowers it hardesta large policy change between generations

The fourth and fifth rows are worth acting on because they are a collection-time choice, not a training-time one. Corrections at a task's structural difficulty — the alignment before insertion, the regrasp when the object is at an awkward angle — describe a situation every future checkpoint will also encounter, so they retain value across many generations. Corrections at an idiosyncratic failure of this particular checkpoint — a hesitation that this policy has and the next one will not — expire immediately. Both cost the same to collect and they differ enormously in shelf life, so prefer collecting at states that are hard for the task rather than merely wrong for this model.

4 · How to account for it

  1. Timestamp and checkpoint-stamp every correction. You cannot compute depreciation without knowing which policy generated the state. This is one field and it is frequently absent.
  2. Report an effective correction count, not a cumulative one. "We have collected 40,000 corrections" is a meaningless number; Σ Mjρj is the real holding. The two can differ by an order of magnitude.
  3. Measure ρ rather than assuming it. Compare the state-visitation distributions of consecutive checkpoints on a fixed set of initial conditions — even a coarse estimate turns the cadence question from an argument into a calculation.
  4. Use a rolling window, and let it be short. Weight recent corrections up, or simply drop everything older than a few half-lives. Old corrections are not neutral ballast; they actively pull the policy toward a distribution it no longer has.
  5. Budget corrections as an ongoing subscription, not a purchase. This is the mental shift the lesson is for. Force is capital, demonstrations are inventory, corrections are rent — and you pay it for as long as the policy is deployed.
Takeaway
A correction is valuable precisely because it sits where the current policy goes, so retraining moves the policy and strands it: value decays as ρj per generation, with a half-life of only 2–4 generations at realistic retention. Cycle value V(T)=M(1−ρT)/ln(1/ρ) saturates — collecting longer stops adding anything — and throughput V(T)/(T+τov) has an interior optimum, so cadence is computable. Hence: match collection and retraining cadence, and keep both fast; a quarterly release against daily collection wastes most of the most expensive row in the ledger. The two-rate architecture is what makes a fast cadence affordable. Raise ρ by changing less per generation and by collecting at states hard for the task rather than quirks of this checkpoint. And account for corrections as rent, not capital.

Where this points next

Every row is now characterised: its volume, its per-capability yield, its price, its availability ceiling, its saturation point, and — for one row — its shelf life. That is enough to state the allocation problem properly rather than by intuition.   • Why does collecting corrections for longer eventually add nothing? V(T) = M(1−ρ^T)/ln(1/ρ) saturates at M/ln(1/ρ): early corrections rot as fast as late ones arrive. (§2)
  • Show why an optimal retraining interval exists. Throughput V(T)/(T+τ_ov) vanishes at small T because overhead dominates and at large T because the numerator saturates, so it peaks in between. (§2)
  • Why does the two-rate architecture matter for this lesson? A fast cadence requires cheap retraining, and re-fitting a small action expert weekly is routine while re-fitting a large monolith is not — the latency architecture also makes the optimal cadence affordable. (§2)
  • Which corrections have long shelf lives, and which expire immediately? Those at a task's structural difficulty, which every future checkpoint will encounter, retain value; those at one checkpoint's idiosyncratic quirks expire at once — and both cost the same to collect. (§3)
  • Why is a cumulative correction count meaningless? The real holding is Σ M_j ρ^j, which can be an order of magnitude below the cumulative total — which is why every correction needs a checkpoint stamp. (§4)
  • Classify the three kinds of data asset in this ledger. Force is capital (buy once, accumulates free), demonstrations are inventory (buy and hold), and corrections are rent (paid continuously for as long as the policy is deployed). (§4)
