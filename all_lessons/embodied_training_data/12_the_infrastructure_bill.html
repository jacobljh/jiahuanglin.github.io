The infrastructure bill

Language data is small and dense; embodied data is enormous and sparse. That difference moves the bottleneck somewhere nobody budgets for: not the accelerators, not the network, but the CPU cores turning compressed frames back into pixels fast enough to keep the accelerators fed.

1 · The bytes, computed

Language data is deceptively tidy: a book is a megabyte, and a whole library fits on a laptop. Embodied data is not like that. One robot, three cameras, one hour — and you have written three hundred gigabytes. Do that for a thousand hours and the pile is the size of a language model's entire training corpus, while containing a tiny fraction of its actual training signal. That mismatch is where the surprises in this lesson come from.

Take a modest real setup: three cameras at 640×480 RGB, 30 fps. Per frame:

640 × 480 × 3 = 921,600 B ≈ 0.92 MB × 30 fps × 3 cameras = 82.9 MB/s  ⟹  298 GB per hour, raw

At roughly 20:1 JPEG that is about 15 GB/hour stored. So a thousand hours of collection is 15 TB — manageable. But now add depth (another camera-equivalent or two), a wrist camera, and the force stream at 1 kHz, and it grows to tens of terabytes for a modest programme.

Compare with language. Fifteen trillion text tokens is a few tens of terabytes — the same order as a thousand hours of three-camera robot video. Which is the observation that makes this lesson necessary: embodied data has language-corpus bytes with a tiny fraction of the tokens, so every byte-denominated cost — storage, egress, and above all decode — is enormous relative to the compute those tokens buy.

Text: bytes per token~4
Video at 256²/patch 16: stored bytes per token~800
Ratio — the source of every infrastructure surprise here~200×

2 · Where it actually breaks: decode

Storage is cheap and network is usually fine. The bottleneck is that every stored frame is compressed, and every training step needs it decompressed.

A CPU core decodes on the order of 200 JPEGs per second at 640×480. A training step consuming a batch of 256 clips at 8 frames each needs 2,048 frames per step; at a modest 4 steps per second that is 8,192 frames per second, requiring

8,192 / 200 ≈ 41 CPU cores, running flat out, purely to decode

and that is for one training node at a modest batch size and resolution. Double the resolution and it quadruples. Which produces the characteristic and widely-experienced symptom:

the symptomExpensive accelerators at 30–50% utilisation, a profiler showing them waiting on data, and a team concluding they need more accelerators. They need more CPU cores, or a different storage format. Embodied training runs are usually input-bound, and the diagnosis is routinely wrong because everyone's mental model comes from language training, which is compute-bound.

Drag the decode cores down to 8 and read the utilisation figure: the accelerators are being fed a fraction of what they can consume, and no amount of additional accelerator would change that number. Then put the cores back to 32 and raise the resolution to 1080: utilisation falls to 44% and the readout tells you it now wants 73 cores, because each core decodes a large frame more slowly and there are more bytes in it. Both are the same lesson from opposite directions — in this pipeline the constraint is almost never the arithmetic.

3 · The five fixes, in order of return

  1. Pre-decode into the training representation, once. If lesson 01's tokenizer is frozen (and after track 1's stage 1 it is), tokenise the whole corpus once and store tokens. Tokens are ~800× smaller than stored frames per unit of training signal, need no decoding, and load at memory speed. This is the single largest win available and it is under-used because it feels like premature optimisation.
  2. Store at the resolution you train at. Collecting at 1080p and training at 256² means every frame is decoded at full resolution and then thrown away. Keep the high-resolution originals in cold storage for the future and materialise a training-resolution copy. Cheap, and it multiplies decode throughput directly.
  3. Use chunked, sequential-friendly formats. Trajectory data is read as contiguous time windows, so a format storing episodes as contiguous shards — the RLDS-style layout Open X-Embodiment uses, or a WebDataset-style tar shard — turns random reads into streams. Random access to individual frames across millions of small files is the worst case for every storage system.
  4. Decode on the accelerator. Modern accelerators have hardware video decoders sitting idle. Moving decode there removes the CPU bottleneck entirely, at the cost of pipeline complexity.
  5. Buy CPU cores. Last because it is the least elegant, but if the profiler says input-bound then cores are the direct fix and they are far cheaper than the accelerators they unblock.
The one thing you must not compress away
Everything above is about throwing away redundancy in the observation column. Do not apply the same instinct to the others. The force stream at 1 kHz is a rounding error in bytes — a few megabytes an hour against 15 gigabytes — and lesson 08 showed its transients are the decisive signal and are destroyed by resampling. Likewise timestamps, calibration and provenance: all tiny, all irreplaceable. Compress the abundant column hard and store the scarce columns at full fidelity forever. The bytes are all in the cheap column, which means the expensive columns are effectively free to keep.

4 · The cost, in perspective

Storage, 2,000 hours at 15 GB/h, cloud object storage for a yearorder $10³
Egress, if you move it between regions or cloudsorder $10³ per move
Decode compute across a training campaignorder $10³–10⁴
Accelerator time wasted at 40% utilisationorder $10⁴ — the real cost
Collection labour, for comparison (track 2 capstone)order $10⁵

Read the fourth row against the third. The direct infrastructure cost is modest; the cost of the accelerator time it wastes is an order of magnitude larger. So the argument for fixing the pipeline is not the storage bill — it is that an under-fed training cluster is the most expensive idle asset in the programme. A week of engineering on the data loader routinely returns more than a hardware upgrade, and it is the sort of work that gets deferred because it is not modelling.

Takeaway
Three cameras at 640×480/30 fps is 298 GB/hour raw, ~15 GB/hour stored — so a thousand hours is comparable in bytes to a 15-trillion-token text corpus while carrying a tiny fraction of the tokens (~800 stored bytes per token against text's ~4, a 200× ratio). Hence every byte-denominated cost dominates, and the binding one is decode: a modest batch needs ~8,200 frames/s, or ~41 CPU cores flat out, so accelerators sit at 30–50% and the diagnosis is usually wrong because language training is compute-bound and this is not. Fix it by pre-tokenising once, storing at training resolution, using chunked sequential formats, decoding on-device, then buying cores. And compress only the abundant column — force, timestamps, calibration and provenance are byte-trivial and irreplaceable, so keep them at full fidelity forever.
