What the Training Eats

The two tracks before this one each costed a complete training programme and each landed on the same wall: not the architecture, not the compute — the data. This track is that ledger, built with arithmetic rather than intuition, and it answers the question both seats kept raising and neither could resolve.

The question, and both of its answers
By volume: video, and it is not close. A 256² frame at patch 16 is 256 tokens; at 8 fps that is 7.37 M tokens per hour — about 600× denser than speech — times roughly 10⁹ hours, so about 7 × 10¹⁵ tokens, two to three orders beyond all web text.

By marginal value per dollar: on-policy corrections, then contact force, then your own teleoperation. All public real robot interaction is ~10³–10⁴ hours; all public contact-force interaction is ~10¹–10² hours.

Top to bottom is seven orders of magnitude, the two rankings are almost exactly inverted, and that inversion is the field's central engineering problem.

Why the inversion is permanent

Not an accident of what has been collected so far. Three mechanisms, none reversible:

Asymmetric growth — video accumulates free as a by-product; simulation grows superlinearly in engineering; real interaction grows strictly linearly in human hours. It is the only row with no multiplier.lesson 06
Structural zeros — no camera records a newton, no unlabeled video records a command. Absent columns are not small numbers, so scale is the wrong instrument.lessons 07, 08
Expiry — the most valuable row depreciates as ρj with a half-life of 2–4 retraining generations. You cannot accumulate it; you can only rent it.lesson 09

Which gives the classification that most changes how you plan: force is capital (buy once, every later hour carries it), demonstrations are inventory (buy to saturation), corrections are rent (pay continuously or lose the position). Treating all three alike is the most common planning error in the field.

The trade every method implements
put the scale where data is free, and the grounding where it is dear
V-JEPA 2 is the cleanest instance: over 1,000,000 hours of internet video under about 62 hours of action-grounded robot video — a grounding stage that is 0.006% of the data and does all the work the free part cannot. Inverse dynamics, latent actions, simulation, cross-embodiment pooling, human video and training inside a learned model are six instruments for one trade.

The fourteen lessons

00
Two rankings that disagree
Video wins by volume at ~7 × 10¹⁵ tokens; action-grounded contact data wins by value at ~3,000× the price per hour. Why observation accumulates passively while force needs a body — so the spread widens.
01
Measure in the right unit
One hour of video is 5.7 GB, 7.4 M tokens, one hour, and ~7 × 10⁵ decision-relevant bits. Token count is a property of your engineering; decision bits are a property of the task. And size(corpus) is not a scalar.
02
Cost per useful bit
Eight sources, four capabilities, one axis. Semantics has an almost-free full-yield source; control and contact have none. Why a narrow cost spread is the definition of a bottleneck.
03
Video: the ocean
Filters compose multiplicatively, so a million raw hours is ~30,000 usable ones. What survives is successful, photogenic, edited and third-person — and contains no failure-recovery, which is a selection effect no scale repairs.
04
Egocentric human video
Camera motion is body motion, so part of the action column is in the pixels. Ego4D is 3,670 h; Ego-Exo4D is 221 egocentric hours, not the 1,286 usually quoted. Its underrated asset: it contains real failures.
05
Simulation: infinite, bounded
$0.0006 per robot-hour of compute — and $96,000 to author 200 scenarios. Quote it in dollars per unit of coverage and it competes directly with real collection. What generative augmentation does and does not change.
06
Real robot data: the expensive kernel
All public interaction data is ~10³–10⁴ hours: 200× less than a language corpus, 5,700× for DROID alone. The only source with no multiplier, which is why pooling was the community's answer. And diversity, not duration, is what it lacks.
07
Action labels are the currency
~50 bits per step against 7.4 M tokens per hour, carrying all the control-relevant information. Direct purchase is $45/hour flat; pseudo-labelling has an interior minimum — and its value is set by the ocean you already hold.
08
Contact and force: the dearest bits
~10¹–10² public hours against 10⁹ of video — the only column with zero passive supply and zero recoverability. But the only capital row: a $3,000 rig reaches ~$3 per force-hour within a year, and keeps falling.
09
On-policy data is perishable
Value decays as ρj, half-life 2–4 generations. Cycle value saturates, so collecting longer adds nothing; throughput has an interior optimum, so cadence is computable. Corrections are rent, not capital.
10
The allocation problem
max min over capabilities, because they compose like a product. Saturation scales differing by orders of magnitude make the answer a sequence — with instrumenting deliberately out of price order. Then three ways the model lies.
11
Quality, duplicates, and collapse
100k episodes over 800 clusters is an 800-item dataset, and duplicates are up-weighted not merely wasted. Tail coverage decays as (1−sλ)ⁿ while aggregate metrics improve — which is why collapse is invisible.
12
The infrastructure bill
298 GB/hour raw, ~800 stored bytes per token against text's 4. So the bottleneck is decode: ~41 cores flat out for a modest batch, accelerators at 30–50%, and the diagnosis usually wrong.
13
So what does it eat the most?
Both rankings, the seven-order gap, the three mechanisms that make it permanent, the capital/inventory/rent classification, and an eight-step procedure for what to buy next given what you hold.

How to read this track

  1. Always state the unit. "Bigger corpus" is ill-posed until you say bytes, tokens, hours, or decision bits — and the four orderings differ.
  2. Divide every mixture weight by availability. If the ratio exceeds one, you bought repetition rather than information.
  3. Distinguish a small number from a structural zero. No amount of money buys a column that is absent from the medium.
  4. Ask whether a purchase is capital, inventory, or rent. It changes when you buy it and whether you can stop.
  5. Convert money into calendar time and headcount before believing a plan. An affordable allocation is frequently physically impossible.
