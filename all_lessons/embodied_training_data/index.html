What the Training Eats, from first principles

A robot model eats data, and data comes in kinds that cost very different amounts and are worth very different amounts: another lab's demonstrations on another arm, video with no actions in it, a simulator, corrections made while a robot works, recordings with force in them. This track builds the ledger that compares them, on the same Bench as Training a Robot Model. It measures every source in one unit, the hours of your own robot's demonstrations that an hour of it replaces; puts a price on each; lets the value of an hour fall as you buy; asks how long an hour of each kind keeps its value, and finds that a stock of corrections keeps it while the supply of failures to correct runs out; asks whether a fleet of working robots pays for its own supervision; spends a budget as a sequence of purchases; counts effective hours instead of recorded ones; prices moving the bits; and reads the ledger to say what embodied training eats the most.

The seed question
You have a budget, one robot, and a choice among another laboratory's demonstrations on a different arm, a hundred thousand hours of video, a simulator, and a person watching your robot work. Which of them should the next dollar go to, and what would you have to know to say?
Who this is for
An engineer, researcher or lead who has to decide what data a robot programme buys, and has met the numbers (a million trajectories here, a hundred thousand hours there) without a way to compare them. You need the robot track's vocabulary (a policy, a demonstration, a correction) more than its mathematics; Training a Robot Model is the prerequisite for the first lesson, and the Financials track is good company. By the end you can say what an exchange rate between two data sources is and how to measure it; why the ranking by cost per recorded hour and the ranking by cost per useful hour disagree by orders of magnitude; how to buy under falling returns; which data keeps its value (capital, inventory) and what runs out (the supply of failures to correct); when a fleet becomes a flywheel; why a budget is spent as a sequence; how to count a dataset's effective size; and where the machine stalls on data it cannot read fast enough.
How this track is built
An original first-principles track, not a market survey. (1) A relay, not a list. Every lesson opens with the failure the previous one left standing and closes by stating the next; that hand-off is one sentence, the baton, which appears word for word at the end of one lesson, at the start of the next and in the table below, and a script diffs them. (2) One world. The value of data is measured on the robot track's Bench, so an exchange rate is a computation and not an opinion. (3) Prices are assumptions, and labelled as such. Each price enters through a visible table of assumptions that the widget lets you move; a published figure appears only where a primary source gives it, cited by name and year. (4) The widgets compute one line of the ledger each, and every quoted number is checked by an independent script. The Bench's exchange rates and curves teach the method and the shape of the answer (which way, which order of magnitude, where curves cross); real values depend on the task, the policy and the year, and the lessons say so.

The exam

The whole track is graded by one formula: value per dollar = marginal value × exchange rate ÷ price. Each lesson measures or derives one factor of it, or one reason the factors change as you buy.

  • Value and price. Every source in one unit, then in dollars, and the two rankings that disagree (lessons 1–2).
  • How value moves. Diminishing returns, a stock of corrections that keeps its value while its supply runs out, and a fleet that pays for its supervisors only above a success rate (lessons 3–5).
  • Spending it. The allocation problem, the effective size of a dataset, the bill for moving the bits, and the honest answer (lessons 6–9).

The flywheel: a fleet as a source
A fleet with supervisors on the Bench: data per day, value per correction, supervision cost, the take-off condition and the cost of each percentage point.

Part III · Spending it (lessons 06–09)

The allocation problem, the effective size of a dataset, the bill for moving it, and the honest answer.

06
Spending the budget: the allocation problem
Capabilities that compose like a product, saturation scales that differ by orders of magnitude, the greedy sequence, and why the instrument for a column comes before its price rank.
07
Effective hours: duplicates, quality and collapse
Effective sample size, clusters of duplicates that are up-weighted and not merely wasted, tail coverage that decays as a dataset repeats, and training on your own samples.
08
Moving the bits: the infrastructure bill
Raw and stored bytes per training token, decode-bound pipelines, the cores needed to feed one accelerator, and utilisation against resolution and frame rate.
09
What does embodied training eat the most?
Both rankings, the structural zeros, the capital, inventory and corrections classification, the flywheel threshold, and a worked purchase plan.

Shelf life and the supply of corrections: Classify data by how long it stays useful: capital that keeps its value, inventory that saturates, and corrections, whose value lasts as long as the policy stays among the states they were made in.A correction does not lose its value when the policy it was made for is retrained: a better policy stays among the states of a worse one, and on the Bench a set made for the first clone is worth as much per frame to the fourth clone as a fresh set, losing value only when the world moves outward, by about a fifth when the gust doubles. Demonstrations and force recordings, which the expert made and no policy did, keep theirs whoever is trained. What runs out is the supply: a correction teaches what its policy got wrong, the first set adds about eighteen points of success and the fourth about one, and a policy that fails one run in twenty has little left to show. The cheapest continuing supply of corrections is the deployed robots themselves, working, with a person stepping in when they fail. How to read this
Straight through, in order: the track is one argument, and each lesson's "Linear position" box names the problem it inherits, the single idea it adds and the problem it hands on. If you have time only for the spine, read 01 → 02 → 03 → 06 → 09. Each lesson has one widget that computes one line of the ledger. To re-check every quoted number yourself, run python3 tools/chain/validate_chain.py --series dat --all --oracles from the repository root: it re-derives each one with an independent script.
