Video: the ocean

Web video wins every volume ranking and it costs almost nothing, which makes it tempting to treat as unlimited. It is unlimited in hours and sharply limited in the thing you need, because each curation filter removes a large fraction and the filters compose multiplicatively.

Where we are
Lesson 02's ledger put web video at about $0.02 per useful hour for semantics — the best deal in the table by orders of magnitude — and a structural zero for control and contact. This lesson examines the row properly: how much there is, what surviving curation looks like, and what each filter costs you beyond the hours it removes.

1 · The scale, derived rather than asserted

It is worth feeling the size before reasoning about it. In the time it takes to read this paragraph, several hours of new video have been uploaded somewhere. Nobody commissioned them, nobody was paid per hour to make them, and the pile has been growing that way for two decades. For any question of the form "do we have enough hours?" the answer here is simply yes, permanently — which means the interesting question has to be a different one.

Video upload rates are commonly reported at hundreds of hours per minute for a single large platform. Take 500 hours per minute:

500 × 60 × 24 = 720,000 hours/day  ⟹  ≈ 2.6 × 10⁸ hours/year

Accumulated over the platform era, the total is on the order of 10⁹ hours. Convert at lesson 01's rate:

10⁹ h × 7.37 × 10⁶ tok/h ≈ 7 × 10¹⁵ tokens

against roughly 1.5 × 10¹³ tokens of curated web text. Video is two to three orders of magnitude larger as a token supply, and NVIDIA reports curating on the order of 20 million hours of video for its Cosmos world foundation models — which is itself only about 2% of the estimated total, and already about 1.5 × 10¹⁴ tokens. Nobody is close to exhausting this supply.

So the constraint is never "how many hours"
For video the binding constraints are compute (you cannot afford to train on 10⁹ hours), decode throughput (lesson 12 — you cannot even read it fast enough), and the fraction that contains what you need. Only the third is a data question, and it is the one this lesson computes.

2 · The funnel, and why the filters compose

Each curation stage keeps a fraction, and the fractions multiply. That is why the survival rate is so much lower than any single filter suggests.

  1. De-duplication. Re-uploads, clips of clips, near-identical intros, and the same footage across a thousand reaction videos. Typically removes 20–40%. Cost beyond the hours: almost none — this filter is nearly free of bias and should always be applied first, since every later filter then costs less compute.
  2. Motion and static filtering. A large fraction of video is a mostly-static frame with a talking head, a slideshow, or a screen recording. For learning physical dynamics this is empty. Removes 30–60%. Cost: it also removes slow, careful manipulation — precisely the most instructive kind — so a naive motion threshold selects for fast dramatic motion over patient fine work.
  3. Caption and text-alignment quality. Auto-generated captions are often unrelated to the visual content. Keeping only well-aligned pairs removes 20–50%. Cost: this selects for professionally produced content, which is systematically edited, lit, and framed — and therefore systematically unlike a robot's camera view.
  4. Shot-boundary and continuity filtering. Edited video cuts. A cut is a discontinuity in the dynamics that a world model will faithfully learn as physics — objects teleporting, viewpoints jumping — so contiguous single-shot segments must be extracted rather than whole videos used. Cost: this is the filter most often skipped and the one whose omission most directly corrupts a dynamics model.
  5. Interaction filtering. The one that matters for embodiment: does a hand manipulate an object? Across general web video this is perhaps 5–15%. Cost: nothing — but it is the multiplier that turns a large corpus into a modest one.

Tighten the three filters one at a time and watch the survival figure rather than the bars, because the bars are a log scale and hide the compounding. Each filter individually looks reasonable — keep 70%, keep 55%, keep 65% — and together they keep a quarter. That is what multiplicative means in practice, and it is why a corpus quoted at its raw size is off by roughly two orders of magnitude for embodied purposes.

At the default settings, three moderate filters keep about a quarter of the corpus, and the interaction filter takes that to roughly 3%. So one million raw hours is about thirty thousand hours of manipulation-relevant video — still a great deal, and two orders of magnitude less than the headline. Plan against the bottom bar, never the top one.

3 · What survives is biased, and the bias has a direction

Filtering is not a neutral reduction. Every filter selects, and the selections point the same way — which makes the composite bias larger than any individual one.

Uploaded video is interesting — nobody films an ordinary drawer opening normallyunder-represents the routine
It is edited — cuts, music, jump cuts, speed rampscorrupts dynamics
It is well-framed and well-lit, by a human choosing the shotunlike a robot's view
It succeeds — failures are cut before uploadno recovery data
It is third-person — the actor is in frame, not holding the camerano ego-motion signal
Composite: successful, photogenic, edited, observed from outsidethe opposite of a robot's experience

The fourth row is the one with real consequences and it is rarely named. Track 2 lesson 02 showed that the states a policy needs training on are the mildly-wrong ones, and the entire mechanism of web video publication removes those. A cooking channel does not upload the take where the pan was dropped. So the corpus that dominates every volume ranking is systematically missing exactly the recovery behaviour that the central theorem of robot learning says you need.

Which sets a hard boundary on what scale can do
This is a selection effect, so more hours do not fix it — a billion hours of successful, edited, third-person video contains no more failure-recovery than a million did. It is the same structure as lesson 00's force argument: when a column is absent by construction, scale is the wrong instrument. Recovery data has to come from somewhere that records failure, which means your own collection with outcome labels (track 2 lesson 04) or on-policy corrections (lesson 09).

4 · The practical planning rules

  1. De-duplicate first. It is the only bias-free filter and it makes every subsequent stage cheaper. Deduplicating before decoding, on hashes rather than pixels, saves the most.
  2. Extract shots, not videos. Segment on cuts and treat each contiguous shot as the unit. Skipping this teaches your model that objects can teleport, and it will learn that faithfully.
  3. Filter for interaction, and report the number. The manipulation-relevant fraction is the honest size of your corpus for embodied purposes. State it alongside the raw hours, because the two differ by two orders of magnitude.
  4. Do not use a naive motion threshold. It discards slow careful manipulation, which is the most instructive content in the corpus. Filter on hand-object proximity or optical-flow structure, not on gross motion magnitude.
  5. Never expect recovery behaviour from it. Budget for that separately, from a source that records failure.
Takeaway
Web video is effectively inexhaustible — ~10⁹ hours, about 7 × 10¹⁵ tokens, two to three orders beyond all web text, with NVIDIA's reported ~20M-hour Cosmos curation being roughly 2% of it. So hours are never the constraint; compute, decode throughput, and the useful fraction are. Curation filters compose multiplicatively: three moderate filters keep ~25%, and the hand-object interaction filter takes that to ~3% — one million raw hours is about thirty thousand usable ones. And what survives is biased in one consistent direction: successful, photogenic, edited, third-person — the opposite of a robot's experience. Most consequentially it contains no failure-recovery, because failures are cut before upload, and that is a selection effect no amount of scale repairs.

Where this points next

Two of this lesson's filters — third-person framing and the absence of ego-motion — point at a corpus that avoids both by construction. When the camera is mounted on the actor, the hands are in frame at close range doing the manipulation, and the camera's own motion is a partial record of the body's. Lesson 04 examines egocentric video, its real sizes, and the number in that literature most often quoted wrongly.
