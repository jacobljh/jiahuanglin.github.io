Cost per useful bit

One axis, eight sources, four capabilities. Once every source is priced in dollars per useful hour rather than dollars per hour, the ledger stops being a list and becomes a decision procedure — and it exposes a gap that no amount of buying closes.

Where we are
Lesson 01 gave us a unit for usefulness and the rule that corpus size is a function of the capability. Now attach a price to each source and divide. The resulting table is the object the rest of the track examines row by row.
Forced by 01Usefulness is per-capability, so cost must be too. This stepBuild the ledger: dollars per useful hour, per capability, across eight sources. Forces 03The cheapest source by far is video — so examine what curating it actually leaves.

1 · The four capabilities, and why these four

Imagine buying data the way you would buy parts. You do not ask "how much material is this?" — you ask what it is for, because a tonne of the wrong metal is worth less than a gram of the right one. So before pricing anything we have to say what we are buying, and it turns out there are four distinct things, with four different suppliers and four wildly different prices.

Not an arbitrary decomposition. These are the four things both prior tracks kept separating, each with its own source profile and its own saturation point.

semantics
What things are, what tasks mean
Object identity, affordance, language grounding, plausible arrangements. Saturates within a few thousand hours.
dynamics
How the world responds
What happens when you push, drop, tip. Needs real physics and long observation; simulation gets it approximately.
control
Which command produces which effect
Requires the action column, in your body's units. No passive source exists.
contact
Forces, slip, seating, deformation
Requires a sensor in contact on a real body doing the real task. No passive source is even conceivable.

Now the two structural facts, which are what the ledger is for.

fact 1Semantics has an almost-free source at full yield. Web video at $0.02/hour with a 1.0 yield is $0.02 per useful hour. This is why vision-language pretraining transformed the field first and fastest: the capability that was easiest to buy was also the one with the best supply.
fact 2Control and contact have exactly one cheap source, and it is the contested one. Simulation carries a 0.9 control yield at $0.02/hour, which is about $0.022 per useful hour — a hundred times cheaper than anything else. Remove that row and the picture is stark: every other source with a non-zero control yield costs at least $8/hour, and real contact yield costs about $55. So the field's reliance on simulation is not a preference, it is the only affordable route to the two capabilities that gate deployment — which is precisely why lesson 05 spends a whole lesson on what that cheapness costs you: bounded authored support, and a contact model computed by the least reliable part of the engine.

For semantics, several sources sit within a factor of a few of each other and the cheapest is nearly free — source choice barely matters because everything works. A ledger with one cheap outlier and a high cluster is the signature of a capability whose economics rest entirely on one substitution holding up.

3 · The two corrections the ledger needs before you trust it

Saturation. A per-hour price implies a constant return per hour, and none of these capabilities behaves that way. Each saturates at its own scale — semantics within thousands of hours, control within hundreds, contact within tens. So the meaningful figure is not average cost but marginal cost at your current holdings:

marginal value = ∂capability/∂hours  ∝  e−h/k  ⟹  $ per marginal unit = cost · eh/k

The consequence is worth stating: the cheapest source becomes worthless first, because you can afford so much of it that you pass its saturation point immediately. Ten thousand hours of web video costs $200 and exhausts the semantics it can teach. The next $200 buys nothing. Cheap sources are cheap all the way to their ceiling, and then they are unavailable at any price.

Availability. A price is only meaningful up to supply. Web video's $0.02/hour holds for ~10⁹ hours; your teleoperation's $45/hour holds for as many hours as you can staff; on-policy corrections' $60/hour holds only for as long as the current checkpoint is deployed, because they expire — which is lesson 09. Track 1 lesson 10's rule applies unchanged: divide your request by availability, and if the ratio exceeds one you bought repetition rather than information.

The corrected decision rule
Do not buy the cheapest source. Buy the source with the lowest marginal cost per useful bit for your weakest capability, subject to its availability. That is three qualifiers, all of which are routinely dropped — and dropping them is exactly what produces a team with fifty thousand hours of video and a robot that cannot insert a connector.

4 · Reading the ledger as a strategy

The four capabilities have profiles distinct enough that the ledger prescribes a sequence rather than a mixture. It is the same staging both prior tracks derived, now with prices attached.

1. Buy semantics from web video until it saturates — costs almost nothing~$200 for 10⁴ h
2. Buy dynamics from web and pooled robot data — cheap and abundant~$10³
3. Buy control from simulation first (the one cheap source), calibrated; then your own teleoperation for what its support misses~$10⁴
4. Buy contact by instrumenting, then collecting — a one-off, then free~$10³ once
5. Buy on-policy corrections continuously, and re-buy them, because they expire~$10⁴/yr
Total, and note where it is concentratedrows 3–5 are ~95%

The order is forced by saturation: buy the cheap saturating things first because they are cheap and finite, and spend the remaining budget on the expensive non-saturating things. Row 4 is the anomaly worth noticing — contact is the only row that is a capital expense rather than an operating one. Instrument once and every subsequent hour carries force at no additional cost, which makes it the highest-return single purchase in the ledger. Lesson 08 works through that step function.

Takeaway
Price sources in dollars per useful hour — cost divided by per-capability yield — and two structural facts appear. Semantics has an almost-free source at full yield ($0.02/useful hour from web video), which is why vision-language pretraining came first. Control and contact have exactly one cheap source and it is the contested one: simulation is ~$0.02 per useful control hour, a hundred times below everything else, while every other source with control yield costs ≥ $8/h and real contact yield ~$55/h — so the whole economics of the frontier capabilities rests on how far simulation's bounded support and contact model can be trusted. A wide spread across sources is good news; a narrow spread at a high level is a bottleneck. Then correct twice — use marginal cost (the cheapest source saturates first and then is unavailable at any price) and check availability. Buy in saturation order, and note that contact is the only capital expense: instrument once, and every later hour carries force free.

Where this points next

Row one of the ledger is nearly free and dominates every volume ranking, so it deserves scrutiny rather than acceptance. An hour of raw web video is not an hour of usable training data: de-duplication, motion filtering, caption-quality filtering, and interaction filtering each remove a large fraction. Lesson 03 runs the funnel and computes what actually survives — and it is a small enough fraction to change how you plan.
