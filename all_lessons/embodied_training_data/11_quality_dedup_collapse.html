Quality, duplicates, and collapse

Two corrections to every number in this track, both large. Your hours are less unique than you counted, and the flywheel that promises to multiply them removes precisely the rare cases you were paying for — with a decay rate you can compute and a fix that is embarrassingly simple.

Where we are
Lesson 10's optimisation used effective hours heff without saying how to get them from raw hours. That conversion is this lesson's first half. The second is the failure mode of the most attractive idea in this ledger: using your own models to manufacture more data.

1 · Raw is not effective

An operator instructed to collect a thousand demonstrations of one task produces a thousand highly correlated trajectories. Same scene, same object pose within centimetres, same approach, same lighting. As bytes it is a thousand episodes; as evidence it is closer to one episode with error bars.

So define the count that matters. Cluster trajectories on the features that determine the task — object pose, scene identity, approach direction, lighting — and report the number of clusters, or an entropy-based effective count:

Neff = exp( H(cluster distribution) )   or simply   Nclusters

The discrepancy is routinely an order of magnitude. A dataset advertised as 100,000 episodes across 800 distinct scene-task combinations behaves, for generalisation, like an 800-item dataset with good error bars — which is a perfectly respectable thing to have and an entirely different thing from what the headline said.

Which reframes DROID's design, again
Lesson 06 noted DROID is disproportionately useful for its 350 hours. Now we can say why in one number: 564 scenes across 52 buildings. Its effective count is close to its raw count because the diversity was designed in — 50 collectors in 13 institutions could not have produced correlated data if they had tried. Diversity is what makes raw hours effective, and it is a collection-design decision, not a post-processing one.

2 · Duplicates are not merely wasted — they are weighted

Worse than paying for nothing. A duplicated example appears many times in the training distribution, so gradient descent weights it accordingly. Five hundred copies of one grasp means the loss is dominated by that grasp, and the model becomes excellent at it and mediocre elsewhere.

This interacts badly with how manipulation data gets collected. Operators naturally do the easy tasks fast and the hard ones slowly, so per hour they produce far more episodes of the easy ones. Left uncorrected, your training distribution is skewed toward exactly the cases that were already working.

The last row is worth noticing as a bonus: failures are intrinsically diverse, because there are many ways to fail and one way to succeed. So a corpus's failed episodes tend to have an effective-to-raw ratio near one, which makes them disproportionately valuable per hour — the opposite of how they are usually treated.

3 · The flywheel, and how fast it eats the tail

Now the attractive idea. You have a world model (track 1) and a policy (track 2). Generate synthetic experience, train on it, get better models, generate more. Data multiplication with no collection cost.

The failure is specific and it is not "the data is lower quality." A generative model samples from its learned distribution, which under-represents the tail — the rare cases are rare in the training data, so they are rare in the model's estimate, so they are rarer in its samples. Train on those samples and the next model's tail is thinner still. The rare cases do not degrade; they disappear.

Model it. Let each generation train on a fraction s of synthetic data, and let synthetic data retain a fraction (1−λ) of the tail. Then tail coverage after n generations is

coverage(n) = (1 − s·λ)n

At s = 0.5 and λ = 0.55, three generations leave (1−0.275)³ ≈ 38% of the tail. Five leave 20%. And the catastrophe is that this is invisible in aggregate metrics: average performance holds or improves, because the common cases are represented better than ever. Only the rare cases vanish — and in manipulation the rare cases are the awkward object pose, the partial occlusion, the unexpected slip. They are the entire remaining difficulty.

Set the synthetic share to 10% and read the generation-3 figure: 84% of the tail survives, which is a perfectly workable flywheel. Now set it to 60% and read it again: 30%. The control is linear and the consequence is geometric, so the difference between a safe recycling ratio and an unsafe one is much narrower than it feels when you are choosing it.

The dashed line is the whole fix and it is simple: anchor every generation with fresh real data. Not a large fraction — enough that the tail is re-supplied from measurement rather than from the model's estimate of itself. That converts a geometric decay into a bounded one, and it is why world_models/15 insists on closing the data flywheel without training on your own fiction.

4 · The rules that follow

  1. Never let a generated sample into an evaluation set. The hardest rule to enforce and the most important, because once it happens every measurement afterwards is comparing the model to itself. Track the provenance of every episode as a first-class field.
  2. Cap the synthetic share and hold it constant. A share that drifts upward across generations compounds silently. Pick a fraction, enforce it in the sampler, and log it per run.
  3. Measure tail coverage directly, not average performance. Report performance on the rarest decile of your evaluation distribution. That is the number collapse moves, and it is the only one that shows the problem while it is still fixable.
  4. Keep a locked, measured, never-regenerated holdout. Real episodes, real actions, real forces, collected once and never touched by any model. It is the only anchor to reality you will have, and its value grows as everything around it becomes model-derived.
  5. Prefer synthetic augmentation to synthetic generation. Restyling a real trajectory keeps the real action and contact sequence and varies only appearance — the tail is preserved because the underlying situation was measured. Wholly generated trajectories have no such anchor. This distinction is the practical difference between a safe and an unsafe flywheel.
Takeaway
Two corrections, both large. Raw is not effective: cluster on task-relevant features and report Neff — a 100,000-episode dataset over 800 scene-task clusters behaves like an 800-item dataset with good error bars, and duplicates are not merely wasted but up-weighted, skewing training toward the cases that already worked. DROID's 564 scenes across 52 buildings is why its effective count nearly equals its raw count: diversity is a collection-design decision. And the flywheel eats the tail: coverage decays as (1−sλ)n — at a 50% synthetic share, three generations leave ~38% of the rare cases — while aggregate metrics improve, because only the rare cases vanish and they are the entire remaining difficulty. Anchor every generation with fresh real data, measure the rarest decile rather than the average, keep a locked measured holdout, and prefer augmenting real trajectories over generating whole ones.

Where this points next

We now have honest effective hours, correctly weighted, safely augmented. They still have to physically move: read off disk, decoded, and delivered to accelerators fast enough to keep them busy. That turns out to be where a surprising fraction of embodied training budgets actually goes, and the bottleneck is not where anyone plans for it. Lesson 12 computes the bill in bytes rather than tokens.
