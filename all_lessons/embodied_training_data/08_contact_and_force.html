Contact and force: the dearest bits

Force is the only column that no corpus in this ledger contains, that no labelling technique can manufacture, and that decides whether the frontier tasks work. It is also — uniquely among the expensive rows — a one-time purchase rather than a per-hour one, which makes it the highest-return item in the entire ledger.

Where we are
Lesson 07's currency works for actions whose effects are visible, and contact actions are exactly the ones whose effects are not. So the labelling route is closed, the video route is closed by track 2 lesson 07's zero, and the simulation route is the contested one from lesson 05. This lesson looks at what remains.

Tens of hours, maybe a few hundred, across the whole field — against 10⁹ hours of video. That is a ratio around 10⁷, and it is the largest single gap anywhere in this track. And the reason is not neglect; it is that no passive accumulation mechanism exists even in principle. Cameras record because people point them at things. Nobody has ever accidentally recorded a torque trace.

the structural factForce is the only column with zero passive supply and zero recoverability from observation. Every other scarce thing in this ledger has at least one substitution path — pool it, pseudo-label it, simulate it, or transfer it. Force has exactly one: put a sensor on a body and have it do the task.

2 · But the cost structure is completely different

Here is the good news, and it is a genuinely different shape from every other row. Every other expensive row is an operating cost: another grounded hour costs another $45, forever, linearly. Force is a capital cost.

Buy a wrist force-torque sensor — a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, once. Or simply record the joint torque estimates your arm already produces, at kHz, at zero marginal cost. From that moment on, every hour you collect for any other reason also carries force. The force data is a free by-product of collection you were already paying for.

So the unit cost of force-hours is not a rate; it is a hyperbola that falls toward zero:

$ per force-hour = Crig / hours collected since instrumenting

At a $3,000 rig and 90 hours of collection per month, after a year you own about 1,000 force-hours at roughly $3 per force-hour — cheaper than any other grounded row in the ledger — and the number keeps falling. Meanwhile the team that did not instrument owns zero force-hours, and no amount of money spent later recovers the ones they already collected.

Now hold the rig cost at $3,000 and sweep the months. The unit cost falls hyperbolically and never bottoms out — at 24 months it is under two dollars a force-hour. Then set the rig cost to $20,000, the most expensive option on the slider, and note that by a year it is still cheaper per hour than teleoperation. There is no setting of these controls that makes not instrumenting the better decision.

At default settings you cross 350 force-hours — more than DROID's total interaction hours, in a channel DROID does not have — inside about five months of ordinary collection. That is the argument in one sentence: a single lab that instruments can own more force data than the entire public corpus, for the price of one sensor and no change to its collection plan.

3 · What to record, in priority order

  1. Joint torque or current, from the arm's own controller. Already computed, already streaming, costs nothing but disk. There is no argument against recording this and it is routinely discarded. Start here today.
  2. Wrist force-torque, six axes. The single best value purchase. It sits between the arm and the tool, so it measures the interaction directly rather than inferring it through the arm's dynamics.
  3. Gripper width and grasp force. Cheap, often already available, and it distinguishes "closed on nothing" from "closed on an object" — a distinction vision frequently cannot make.
  4. Tactile fingertips. More expensive and more fragile, and the only thing that observes slip and local deformation. Decisive for deformables and in-hand manipulation; skippable for rigid pick-and-place.
  5. Full clock synchronisation across every stream. Not a sensor but a prerequisite: a force spike 40 ms out of alignment with the frame that caused it is worse than no force data, because it teaches a wrong causal relationship. synthetic_vision/04 gives the provenance and synchronisation contract; adopt it.
And store it at its own rate
Force is meaningful at 1 kHz while vision arrives at 30 Hz. Do not resample force down to the frame rate at storage time — that discards the transients irreversibly, and a 30 ms slip is precisely a transient. Store the fast stream at full rate and summarise it at training time (max, mean, variance, time-since-transition over each control interval, as track 2 lesson 08 prescribed). Storage is cheap; the information is not recoverable.

4 · Why nobody has done this at scale, and what that implies

The obvious question: if instrumenting is this cheap and this valuable, why is there no large force dataset? Three reasons, and none of them is that it is a bad idea.

1 · heterogeneity
No shared convention
Force is sensor-, mount- and calibration-specific. Pooling RGB across 22 embodiments works because a pixel is a pixel; pooling force requires agreement on frames, units, mounting and calibration that the community has not reached.
2 · it was not the bottleneck
Until recently
While the field was learning to reach and grasp, vision was the constraint. Force only becomes decisive at insertion, wiping, and deformables — which is where the frontier arrived roughly now.
3 · it is invisible in benchmarks
No leaderboard rewards it
Success rate on pick-and-place does not improve much with force, so the metric that drives dataset design does not ask for it — a classic case of the benchmark shaping the corpus.

The implication is worth stating plainly, because it is actionable rather than merely observational. Reasons 2 and 3 are expiring: the frontier has moved to contact-rich tasks, and evaluation is following. Reason 1 is a coordination problem of exactly the kind Open X-Embodiment solved for RGB. So the highest-leverage thing an individual lab can do right now is record force on everything and publish it with an explicit calibration and frame convention — it costs nearly nothing, it accumulates while you do other work, and it is the one column that scale cannot supply to anyone who did not.

Takeaway
Public real contact-force data is on the order of 10¹–10² hours against 10⁹ hours of video — a gap near 10⁷, the largest in this track — because force has zero passive supply and zero recoverability from observation, the only column with no substitution path at all. But its cost structure is unique: it is a capital expense, so $/force-hour = Crig/hours since instrumenting falls hyperbolically — a $3,000 rig at 90 h/month reaches ~$3/force-hour within a year and keeps falling, while an uninstrumented programme owns zero forever and cannot go back. Record joint torque today (free), then wrist force-torque, then gripper force, then tactile — with full clock synchronisation, stored at native rate and summarised only at training time.

Where this points next

Every row so far has been an asset: buy it, own it, use it. One row in the ledger does not behave that way at all. On-policy corrections are the most valuable data per hour in the entire table — track 2 lesson 05 showed they change the error exponent rather than its coefficient — and they are the only data here with a shelf life. Lesson 09 computes the depreciation and derives the retraining cadence it forces.
