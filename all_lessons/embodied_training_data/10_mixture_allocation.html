The allocation problem

Every row is now priced, ceilinged, and — for one of them — dated. That is enough to write the allocation down as an optimisation rather than argue about it. Doing so produces one structural result: because the returns saturate at wildly different scales, the answer is not a mixture but an order.

1 · The problem, written down

You have a budget and a shopping list with seven aisles, and you have now priced every one of them. So the question stops being "which data is good" and becomes the ordinary, tractable one a buyer faces: given this much money, exactly how much of each? Writing that down properly turns out to answer something the intuitive version never does — not just the proportions, but the order to buy them in.

Choose hours hi from each source i to maximise the weakest capability, subject to a budget:

maxh minc   Sc( Σi yic · hieff )    s.t.   Σi hi · pi ≤ B

with four pieces, each of which came from an earlier lesson:

pi — price per hour of source ilesson 02
yic — yield of source i for capability c, often exactly zerolesson 02
Sc(x) = 1 − e−x/kc — saturating return, with kc differing by orders of magnitudelesson 02 §3
heff — effective hours after availability and repetition discountinglesson 02, wmt/10
Plus, for corrections only, a decay factor ρjlesson 09

Two modelling choices are doing real work here and both deserve defending.

The objective is a min over capabilities, not a sum. A policy with excellent semantics and no contact capability does not perform the task at all — the capabilities compose more like a product than a sum, so the weakest one governs. Maximising a sum reliably recommends buying more of whatever is cheapest, which is how a team ends up with fifty thousand hours of video and a robot that cannot insert a connector. Maximising the minimum recommends buying whatever you are worst at, which is the correct instinct.

The saturation scales kc differ enormously. Semantics saturates around 10⁴ hours, dynamics around 10⁴, control around 10³, contact around 10². That spread is what makes the answer a sequence, as §2 shows.

2 · The structural result: allocation is a sequence

Solve it mentally rather than numerically. Under a min-objective with saturating returns, the optimum equalises the capabilities — you buy up whichever is lowest until it is no longer lowest, then move on. And because each capability has a different cheapest source and a different saturation point, that procedure visits the sources in a fixed order:

  1. Semantics, from web video. $0.02 per useful hour, saturating around 10⁴ hours ⟹ about $200 to exhaust it. Do this first because it is cheap, finite, and everything downstream is easier on a good perception trunk.
  2. Dynamics, from web plus pooled robot data. Cheap and abundant, saturating around 10⁴ hours ⟹ order $10³. Still very cheap relative to the budget.
  3. Contact, by instrumenting. Out of price order deliberately — it is a capital purchase (lesson 08), so buying it early means every hour collected in steps 4 and 5 also carries force. Order $10³, once. Delaying it is the single most expensive scheduling mistake in this ledger, because the hours you collect before instrumenting can never be retrofitted.
  4. Control, from calibrated simulation then your own teleoperation. Simulation first because it is far cheaper per useful hour, but bounded by authored coverage (lesson 05) and contested on contact — so real teleoperation follows. Order $10⁴.
  5. Corrections, continuously, forever. Not a purchase but a subscription (lesson 09), sized by the cadence T* rather than by a total. Order $10⁴ per year.

Rows 4 and 5 are roughly 95% of the spend, and rows 1–3 are what make them work. So the honest summary of the whole ledger is: the cheap sources are prerequisites, and the budget is control and corrections.

Why order 3 before 4 is the non-obvious one
Every other step is in ascending price order, which is what a greedy solution to the optimisation gives. Step 3 breaks that on purpose, because its cost structure is different in kind: instrumenting is not consumption, it is changing what future consumption yields. Any purchase with that property should be made as early as possible regardless of its position in the price ordering — and a static optimisation over hours cannot express it, which is exactly the sort of thing worth noticing when a model prescribes a plan.

Run two experiments. Put everything into web video and note that semantics maxes out while control and contact stay near zero — an unbounded budget on the cheapest source cannot fix a structural zero. Then set the budget to its minimum and spread it evenly: every score is low but none is zero, which under a min-objective is strictly better than three high scores and one zero. That asymmetry is the practical content of choosing min over Σ.

3 · Three ways this optimisation lies to you

It is a useful model and it is wrong in three specific ways, each of which matters.

1 · yields are not constants
They depend on what else you have
Human video's semantic yield is high only if you have enough grounded data to attach it to. The yic matrix has interaction terms the linear form omits — which is why staging beats simultaneous purchase.
2 · hours are not unique
Duplicates inflate every count
Every hi above should be an effective count. Lesson 11 shows the correction is often an order of magnitude, which is enough to change the optimum.
3 · the budget is not only money
Calendar and people bind too
Teleoperation is bounded by staffed hours and simulation by engineering hours (lesson 05), so two sources can be affordable and still unreachable this quarter. A single scalar budget hides that.
The one to actually act on
Correction 3 is the one that most often invalidates a plan. A budget that says "buy 5,000 grounded hours" may be arithmetically fine and physically impossible — 5,000 hours at 60 episodes an hour is over two person-years of teleoperation, which four operators cannot deliver in a quarter no matter what the budget says. Always convert a monetary allocation into calendar time and headcount before believing it, which is exactly what track 2's capstone did and what surfaced evaluation as the true constraint.
Takeaway
Write the allocation as max minc Sc(Σ yichieff) subject to a budget. Two choices carry the result: the objective is a min over capabilities, because they compose like a product — maximising a sum recommends the cheapest source and produces a robot with great semantics that cannot insert a connector — and the saturation scales differ by orders of magnitude (10⁴ semantics, 10³ control, 10² contact). That spread makes the answer a sequence: semantics (~$200) → dynamics (~$10³) → instrument for contact (~$10³, deliberately out of price order because it changes what all later hours yield) → control (~$10⁴) → corrections as a subscription (~$10⁴/yr). Rows 4–5 are ~95% of the spend. Then distrust the model three ways: yields interact, hours are not unique, and the real budget includes calendar and headcount.

Where this points next

Correction 2 above is the one we can compute rather than merely note. Every hour count in this optimisation assumed the hours were distinct and honest, and they routinely are neither: operators repeat, episodes near-duplicate, and the tempting shortcut of training on your own model's output quietly removes exactly the rare cases you were paying for. Lesson 11 quantifies both, and shows how fast the tail disappears.
