So what does it eat the most?

The question that opened this track, answered with everything the intervening lessons computed. There are two answers, the gap between them is about seven orders of magnitude, it is permanent, and there is a definite procedure for what to buy next.

Where we are
Thirteen lessons of arithmetic. This one collects it, states the answer without hedging, and turns the ledger into a decision procedure you can run against your own holdings.

1 · Answer one: by volume, video, and it is not close

Web video — ~10⁹ hours at 7.37 M tokens/hour~7 × 10¹⁵ tokens
Simulation — unbounded within authored scenariosunbounded

All public real contact-force interaction — ~10¹–10² hours~7 × 10⁸ tokens

Top to bottom is about seven orders of magnitude. If the question is which corpus consumes the FLOPs, the tokens, the storage and the decode cores, the answer is video, by a factor of 10⁵ over everything with an action column and 10⁷ over anything with a force column. Every world-model training run and every vision-language-action pretraining run is, by mass, a video training run.

2 · Answer two: by value, the four orders of magnitude at the bottom

Now the same table, sorted by what a marginal dollar buys — and it inverts almost exactly.

On-policy corrections — the only data that changes the error exponenthighest, and perishable
Contact and force — the only source of the deciding quantity in manipulationvery high, capital cost
Your own teleoperation — actions in your units, real physicshigh
Pooled robot data — real actions on other bodiesmoderate, shareable
Calibrated simulation — perfect actions within a bounded authored supportmoderate, coverage-limited
Egocentric human video — semantics, affordance, partial ego-motionlow per hour, large in total
Web video — semantics, saturating within ~10⁴ hourslowest per hour, and effectively free

Two things are worth saying precisely about this inversion, because both are commonly misstated.

It is not that video is worthless. Video's total contribution is enormous — it produces the perception and semantic competence that every one of these systems is built on, and it does so for a few hundred dollars. What is low is its marginal value, because it saturates within about 10⁴ hours and you can afford 10⁶. Cheap sources are cheap right up to their ceiling and then unobtainable at any price.

And it is not that robot data is merely "the bottleneck." It is more specific than that: the expensive rows carry columns that the cheap rows structurally lack — an action in your units, a newton, a state your policy actually visits. Absent columns are not small numbers. No quantity of video contains a force, so this is not a gap that scale narrows.

3 · Why the mismatch is permanent

Lesson 00 asserted this; the intervening lessons earned it. Three mechanisms, none reversible:

1 · asymmetric growth
One row has no multiplier
Video accumulates free as a by-product; simulation grows superlinearly in engineering; real interaction grows strictly linearly in human hours (lesson 06). The gap widens by construction.
2 · structural zeros
Absent, not small
No camera records a newton; no unlabeled video records a command (lessons 07, 08). Scale cannot fill a column that is absent from the medium.
3 · expiry
The best data has a shelf life
On-policy corrections depreciate as ρj with a half-life of 2–4 generations (lesson 09). You cannot accumulate the most valuable row at all — you can only rent it.

Point 3 is the one that most changes how you plan, so it is worth stating as a classification. This ledger has three kinds of asset, and treating them alike is the most common planning error in the field:

Capital — instrumentation for force: buy once, and every later hour carries itbuy early
Inventory — demonstrations, pooled data, simulation, video: buy and holdbuy to saturation
Rent — on-policy corrections: pay continuously or lose the positionsubscribe

4 · The procedure

  1. Instrument first, before collecting anything. Force is capital and unretrofittable. Record joint torque today at zero cost; add a wrist force-torque sensor. Every hour collected before this is permanently missing a column.
  2. Buy the free capabilities to saturation. Semantics and dynamics from web and pooled data, roughly $10³ total. They are prerequisites, they cost almost nothing, and they make everything downstream cheaper.
  3. Count your unlabeled corpus before building anything. Lesson 07's crossover is set by the size of your ocean, so the "should we train an inverse dynamics model" question is answered by an inventory, not by an argument.
  4. Author simulation coverage, not simulation hours. Lesson 05: the cost is scenarios. Generating more experience from the same scenarios is free and buys nothing.
  5. Spend human hours only where nothing else can substitute. Contact-rich demonstrations and on-policy corrections. Script or simulate everything else.
  6. Match the retraining cadence to the correction half-life. Lesson 09's T*. A slow release cycle discards the most expensive data you buy.
  7. Report effective, not raw, and never regenerate your holdout. Lesson 11. Anchor every generation with fresh measurement.
  8. Price evaluation before training. Track 2's capstone found it was 10× everything else. It is the constraint no budget increase fixes.
And the shortest version of the whole ledger
Every technique in these three tracks implements one trade: put the scale where data is free, and the grounding where it is dear. V-JEPA 2 is the cleanest instance — over 1,000,000 hours of internet video under about 62 hours of action-grounded robot video, a grounding stage that is 0.006% of the data and does all the work that the free part cannot. If you remember one thing from this track, remember that ratio and what it implies: the question is never how to get more data. It is which four columns you need and which of them the abundant source structurally lacks.
Takeaway
By volume: video, by a factor of 10⁵ over anything with an action column and 10⁷ over anything with a force column — every world-model and VLA pretraining run is, by mass, a video run. By marginal value: on-policy corrections, then force, then your own teleoperation — the rows that carry columns the cheap rows structurally lack. Video is not worthless; its marginal value is low because it saturates within ~10⁴ hours and you can afford 10⁶. The mismatch is permanent for three reasons: asymmetric growth (real interaction is the only row with no multiplier), structural zeros (absent columns are not small numbers), and expiry (the best row has a 2–4 generation half-life). Classify accordingly — force is capital, demonstrations are inventory, corrections are rent — and instrument before you collect, because that is the one decision that cannot be made retroactively.

Where this leaves the arc

Forty-five lessons across three tracks, and they converge on one sentence. Track 1 built the forward map p(o′|o,a) and found its controllability capped by the grounded corpus. Track 2 built the inverse map p(a|o,g), proved cloning fails as O(εT²), and found evaluation costing ten times the training. This track priced the resource both were short of. In all three the model was never the constraint.

Which of the constraints are actually moving is worth naming, because it is where the field's effort is going. Under active attack: the action column, by inverse dynamics and latent action models; simulation's authoring cost, by generative augmentation; the collection rate, by pooling and by cheaper teleoperation hardware. Genuinely immovable, so far: the structural zeros. No camera will record a newton, and no unlabeled corpus will record a command it never contained. A serious answer to those does not look like a larger corpus — it looks like instrumented bodies operating continuously, which is to say deployed fleets, which is why every credible robotics roadmap ends there rather than at a dataset.

And the trade that organises all of it stays the same at every scale: put the scale where data is free, and the grounding where it is dear.
