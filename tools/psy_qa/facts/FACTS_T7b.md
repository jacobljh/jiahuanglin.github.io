## 10 · 痛不等于伤

### 10-A · Definition: what IASP says pain is (and what it does not say)
- [def] ★ IASP revised definition of pain 国际疼痛学会2020年修订定义 (Raja et al. 2020, *Pain* 161(9):1976–1982, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001939, PMID 32694387; abstract read; IASP terminology page read): "An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." The 1979 definition it replaced read "An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage"; a 14-member Presidential Task Force (formed 2018) revised the definition and turned the accompanying notes into a bulleted list including the etymology; accepted unanimously by IASP Council in 2020.
- [def] ★ The six notes (as shown on the IASP terminology page, https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/terminology/, read 2026-10-14; the page text came through a summariser, so the quotation marks below follow the page's fragments — treat the wording as exact for notes 1, 2, 4 and as paraphrase-grade for the clause breaks): (1) "Pain is always a personal experience that is influenced to varying degrees by biological, psychological, and social factors." (2) "Pain and nociception are different phenomena. Pain cannot be inferred solely from activity in sensory neurons." (3) "Through their life experiences, individuals learn the concept of pain." (4) "A person's report of an experience as pain should be respected." (5) "Although pain usually serves an adaptive role, it may have adverse effects on function and social and psychological well-being." (6) "Verbal description is only one of several behaviors to express pain; inability to communicate does not negate the possibility that a human or a nonhuman animal experiences pain."
- [def] Nociception 伤害性感受: "The neural process of encoding noxious stimuli." (IASP terminology page). Nociceptor activity is an input; pain is an experience. NOTE for writers: the lesson's slogan is note 2 + note 4 together — pain is not a direct readout of tissue damage AND a person's report of pain is to be respected.
- [def] Nociplastic pain 痛觉可塑性疼痛 (IASP terminology page): "Pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain." Introduced by Kosek et al. 2016 (*Pain* 157(7):1382–1386, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000507, PMID 26835783; PubMed shows no abstract — not read). Häuser & Kosek 2026 (*Eur J Pain* 30(1):e70175, doi:10.1002/ejp.70175, PMID 41294344; abstract read): nociplastic pain is "not synonymous with chronic primary pain nor with central sensitization" and "not a new term for pain of unknown origin"; controversies: the term itself, fibromyalgia as prototype, whether the requirement to "exclude" nociceptive and neuropathic pain is workable; future tasks include field testing and possibly revising the criteria. As of January 2026 a contested construct.
- [def] ICD-11 chronic pain 慢性疼痛 (Treede et al. 2019, *Pain* 160(1):19–27, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001384, PMID 30586067; abstract read): "pain that persists or recurs for more than 3 months"; split into chronic PRIMARY pain (e.g. fibromyalgia, nonspecific low-back pain: pain "may be conceived as a disease in its own right") and six chronic SECONDARY pain groups (cancer-related, neuropathic, secondary visceral, posttraumatic/postsurgical, secondary headache/orofacial, secondary musculoskeletal) where pain is at least initially a symptom of an underlying disease.
- [def] Chronic primary pain 慢性原发性疼痛 (Nicholas et al. 2019, *Pain* 160(1):28–37, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001390, PMID 30586068; abstract read): pain persisting more than 3 months, associated with significant emotional distress and/or functional disability, "not better accounted for by another condition"; the diagnosis assumes a biopsychosocial framework (biological, psychological and social factors contribute to every subtype) and "is considered to be appropriate independently of identified biological or psychological contributors, unless another diagnosis would better account for the presenting symptoms".
- [V] "Approximately 85% of cases [of chronic back pain] are primary CBP, for which peripheral etiology cannot be identified" — stated in the introduction of Ashar et al. 2022 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 79(1):13–23, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.2669, PMID 34586357; abstract read); the abstract gives no citation for the 85% — [~] use as "about 85% by the trial authors' statement", not as a measured number.
- [def] Gate control theory 闸门控制理论 (Melzack & Wall 1965, *Science* 150(3699):971–979, doi:10.1126/science.150.3699.971, PMID 5320816; PubMed shows no abstract): proposed that spinal-cord circuitry modulates nociceptive transmission and that central (brain) control signals also act on it, so that pain could not be a fixed line from injury to brain. History/definition only: the specific spinal "gate" circuit has been revised over 60 years, while the principle — descending and local modulation of nociceptive signals — is accepted. (Not re-graded here; no source opened that quantifies what survives of the 1965 circuit diagram — write only "the principle survives; the 1965 circuit does not".)

### 10-B · Imaging findings in people without pain
- [V] ★ Brinjikji, Luetmer, Comstock, Bresnahan, Chen, Deyo et al. 2015 (*AJNR Am J Neuroradiol* 36(4):811–816, doi:10.3174/ajnr.A4173, PMID 25430861; abstract read; PubMed lists no correction or retraction): systematic review of CT/MRI findings in ASYMPTOMATIC people, literature through April 2014, 33 articles, 3,110 asymptomatic individuals; age-specific prevalence modelled by decade (20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 y; generalized linear mixed model clustered by study). Prevalence in people WITHOUT back pain rises: disk degeneration 37% (age 20) → 96% (age 80); disk bulge 30% → 84%; disk protrusion 29% → 43%; annular fissure 19% → 29%. Authors: "Many imaging-based degenerative features are likely part of normal aging and unassociated with pain" and the findings "must be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical condition".
  Only the age-20 and age-80 values are in the abstract; the full by-decade table (ages 30–70) sat behind a 403 / cookie wall, so intermediate-decade numbers are NOT given here (see COULD NOT VERIFY).
- [V] ★ Brinjikji, Diehn, Jarvik, Carr, Kallmes & Murad 2015 (*AJNR* 36(12):2394–2399, doi:10.3174/ajnr.A4498, PMID 26359154; abstract read; the companion paper): meta-analysis of adults ≤ 50 y, 14 of 280 studies met criteria, 3,097 people (1,193 asymptomatic, 1,904 with low back pain): findings MORE prevalent in people with back pain — disc bulge OR 7.54 (95% CI 1.28–44.56), spondylolysis OR 5.06 (1.65–15.53), disc extrusion OR 4.38 (1.98–9.68), Modic 1 changes OR 4.01 (1.10–14.55), disc protrusion OR 2.65 (1.52–4.62), disc degeneration OR 2.24 (1.21–4.15); NOT significantly associated: any Modic change OR 1.62 (0.48–5.41), central canal stenosis OR 20.58 (0.05–798.77), high-intensity zone OR 2.10 (0.73–6.02), annular fissures OR 1.79 (0.97–3.31), spondylolisthesis OR 1.59 (0.78–3.24). READING: structure and pain ARE associated, but the association is non-specific and wide-CI; a finding present in a person with pain is often also present in people without it.
- [V] Jensen, Brant-Zawadzki, Obuchowski, Modic & Malkasian 1994 (*N Engl J Med* 331(2):69–73, doi:10.1056/NEJM199407143310201, PMID 8208267; abstract read): lumbar MRI in 98 asymptomatic people read blind by two neuroradiologists, with 27 abnormal scans from people with back pain mixed in; averaged readings: 36% normal at all levels; 52% had a bulge at ≥ 1 level, 27% a protrusion, 1% an extrusion; 38% had an abnormality at more than one disc; Schmorl's nodes 19%, annular defects 14%, facet arthropathy 8%; bulges (not protrusions) rose with age; similar in men and women. Authors: with such high prevalence "the discovery by MRI of bulges or protrusions in people with low back pain may frequently be coincidental".
- [V] Boden, Davis, Dina, Patronas & Wiesel 1990 (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 72(3):403–408, PMID 2312537; abstract read): 67 people who never had low-back pain, sciatica or neurogenic claudication; about one-third had a "substantial abnormality"; under 60 y, 20% had a herniated nucleus pulposus; ≥ 60 y, 36% HNP and 21% stenosis (≈ 57% abnormal); degeneration or bulging at ≥ 1 level in 35% of 20–39-year-olds and in all but one of the 60–80-year-olds.
- [V] Guermazi et al. 2012 (*BMJ* 345:e5339, doi:10.1136/bmj.e5339, PMID 22932918; abstract read), Framingham Osteoarthritis Study: 710 people aged > 50 with NO radiographic knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence 0), mean age 62.3 y, 29% had knee pain in the past month; "any abnormality" on MRI in 89% overall; osteophytes 74%, cartilage damage 69%, bone-marrow lesions 52%; any abnormality in 90–97% of painful knees (depending on the pain definition) vs 86–88% of painless knees. Authors: MRI shows lesions in most middle-aged/older people without radiographic OA "regardless of pain".
- [V] Culvenor et al. 2019 (*Br J Sports Med* 53(20):1268–1278, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2018-099257, PMID 29886437; abstract read): meta-analysis, 63 studies, 5,397 ASYMPTOMATIC UNINJURED knees (4,751 adults): pooled prevalence cartilage defects 24% (15–34), meniscal tears 10% (7–13), bone-marrow lesions 18% (12–24), osteophytes 25% (14–38); cartilage defect < 40 y 11% vs ≥ 40 y 43%; meniscal tear < 40 y 4% vs ≥ 40 y 19%; the abstract summarises 4–14% in < 40 y and 19–43% in ≥ 40 y.
- [V] Shoulder: Sher, Uribe, Posada, Murphy & Zlatkin 1995 (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 77(1):10–15, doi:10.2106/00004623-199501000-00002, PMID 7822341; abstract read): MRI of 96 asymptomatic shoulders: rotator-cuff tears in 34% (15% full-thickness, 20% partial-thickness; 54% of those > 60 y; 4% full-thickness in 40–60 y; none full-thickness in 19–39 y). Minagawa et al. 2013 (*J Orthop* 10(1):8–12, doi:10.1016/j.jor.2013.01.008, PMID 24403741; abstract read): ultrasound of 664 village residents, full-thickness cuff tears 22.1%; 0% in the 20s–40s, 10.7% in the 50s, 15.2% 60s, 26.5% 70s, 36.6% 80s; asymptomatic tears were 65.3% of all tears ("twice as common as symptomatic").

### 10-C · Damage without pain, pain without damage (insensitivity, phantom limbs, wounded soldiers)
- [V] ★ Cox et al. 2006 (*Nature* 444(7121):894–898, doi:10.1038/nature05413, PMID 17167479; abstract read): three consanguineous families from northern Pakistan with "complete inability to sense pain in an otherwise healthy individual"; autosomal-recessive, mapped to 2q24.3; three distinct homozygous nonsense mutations in SCN9A (S459X, I767X, W897X), loss of function of the Nav1.7 sodium channel (currents "no greater than background" in HEK293 cells); SCN9A is "an essential and non-redundant requirement for nociception in humans". Number of affected people is not in the abstract.
- [V] Goldberg et al. 2007 (*Clin Genet* 71(4):311–319, doi:10.1111/j.1399-0004.2007.00790.x, PMID 17470132; abstract read): nine families of seven nationalities with congenital indifference to pain; 10 SCN9A mutations co-segregating with the phenotype, nine causing truncation and loss of function — independent replication of Cox 2006 in other populations.
- [V] Minett et al. 2015 (*Nat Commun* 6:8967, doi:10.1038/ncomms9967, PMID 26634308; abstract read): the picture is not "no signal = no pain, full stop": Nav1.7 deletion up-regulates the enkephalin precursor in sensory neurons; naloxone potentiated noxious input in Nav1.7-null mice and "dramatically" reduced analgesia in both Nav1.7-null mice and "a human Nav1.7-null mutant" (a single human) — endogenous opioids contribute to the insensitivity; this is also why selective Nav1.7 blockers were weak analgesics.
- [V] Weiss et al. 2011 (*Nature* 472:186–190 — page not opened; PubMed record PMID 21441906 title only, "Loss-of-function mutations in sodium channel Nav1.7 cause anosmia"): ADDED — the same gene is needed for smell; so "insensitivity to pain" is not an isolated pain-switch story. Use title only.
- [V] What "no pain" costs (clinical series; NOTE these are different conditions from the SCN9A channelopathy — congenital insensitivity to pain WITH anhidrosis (CIPA, HSAN IV, NTRK1) is the best-documented): Rosemberg, Marie & Kliemann 1994 (*Pediatr Neurol* 11(1):50–56, doi:10.1016/0887-8994(94)90091-4, PMID 7527213; abstract read): review of the 31 reported cases plus one: CIPA "leads to self-mutilation in the first months of life and to bone fractures, multiple scars, osteomyelitis, joint deformities, and limb amputation as the children grow older"; mental retardation common; "death from hyperpyrexia occurs within the first 3 years of life in almost 20% of the patients" (1994 literature, anhidrosis-related; not a lifespan estimate for SCN9A people). Bar-On et al. 2002 (*J Bone Joint Surg Br* 84(2):252–257, doi:10.1302/0301-620x.84b2.11939, PMID 11922368; abstract read): 13 patients with congenital insensitivity to pain: type A multiple infections (5), type B fractures, growth disturbance and avascular necrosis (3), type C Charcot arthropathies, dislocations, fractures, infections (5; four with mental retardation). READING: pain is a protective output — lacking it is dangerous (this is the lesson's anchor for "pain is an ESTIMATE OF DANGER, and a useful one").
- [V] ★ Phantom limb pain 幻肢痛 prevalence: Limakatso, Bedwell, Madden & Parker 2020 (*PLoS One* 15(10):e0240431, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0240431, PMID 33052924; abstract read): systematic review/meta-analysis of articles 1980–July 2019, pooled prevalence 64% (95% CI 60.01–68.05), heterogeneity I² = 95.95% (very high); lower in developing than developed countries (53.98% vs 66.55%, p = 0.03); risk factors: persistent pre-operative pain, proximal amputation site, stump pain, lower-limb amputation, phantom sensations. Same group's 2024 cross-sectional telephone survey of amputees in South Africa's Western/Eastern Cape (Limakatso et al. 2024, *BMC Neurol* 24(1):57, doi:10.1186/s12883-024-03547-w, PMID 38321380; abstract read): prevalence 71.73% (95% CI 65.45–77.46). Guémann et al. 2023 (*Eur J Pain* 27(1):3–13, doi:10.1002/ejp.2035, PMID 36094758) open with "PLP concerns > 50% of amputees". SAFE LINE: "about six in ten people with an amputation report phantom limb pain (pooled 64%, very heterogeneous)".
- [V] Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran 1996 (*Proc R Soc Lond B* 263(1369):377–386, doi:10.1098/rspb.1996.0058, PMID 8637922; abstract read; case reports): ten patients with upper-limb phantoms used a mirror "virtual reality box"; in six, movement of the intact hand seen in the mirror made the phantom FEEL as if it moved; five had painful "clenching spasms" in the phantom hand and in four of these the spasms were relieved when the mirror made "opening" possible; touch referral in three. Descriptive observations in 10 people, no control group.
- [~] Beecher 1946 "Pain in men wounded in battle" (*Ann Surg* 123(1):96–105, PMID 17858731; also *Bull US Army Med Dep* 1946;5:445–454, PMID 21017778; PubMed shows no abstract for either; the paper's own text was NOT retrievable here (PMC and Europe PMC blocked) — figures below come from a slide-deck transcription of the paper's table (SlideShare "1946 Pain in men wounded in battle"), hence [~]): 225 seriously wounded men approached in Italy, 10 excluded (unconscious/not mentally clear), N = 215; pain rated by Beecher's interview: no pain 69 (32.1%), slight 55 (25.6%), moderate 40 (18.6%), "bad" pain 51 (23.7%); wanted pain relief 58 (27.0%), did not want it 157 (73.0%). Morphine: of the 157 who did not want relief, 32 had received none, the other 125 had received morphine (mean total about 27 mg); of the 51 with bad pain, 46 had received morphine (mean total about 30 mg). Bad pain by wound: penetrating abdomen 48%, compound long-bone fracture 24%, soft tissue 16%, thorax 12%, head 7%. The paper contains NO civilian comparison; Beecher's explanation was speculation ("Strong emotion can block pain"; the wound "releases him from an exceedingly dangerous environment"; "whether this actually reduces the pain remains unproved" — quotes as relayed by the transcription).
- [✗] ★ "75% of badly wounded soldiers felt no pain / needed no morphine" (textbook version of Beecher): the 1946 numbers are 73% who did not WANT pain relief, not 73% with no pain (only 32% reported no pain, 26% slight, 24% "bad" pain); most of those who declined relief HAD received morphine (125 of 157 per the transcription); it was an uncontrolled interview study of selected, conscious men (10 of 225 excluded), by the same physician who drew the conclusions, with no blinding and no comparison group in the paper. The civilian comparison ("civilians with similar surgical wounds asked for far more narcotic") belongs to Beecher 1956 (*JAMA* 161(17):1609–1613, doi:10.1001/jama.1956.02970170005002, PMID 13345630, no abstract in PubMed, NOT opened) — the percentages circulating for it (e.g. "83% of civilians") could not be traced here. What survives: severity of wound and intensity of pain are loosely coupled and context (survival, evacuation) plausibly matters; it is a hypothesis-generating observation, not a measurement of "pain without damage".
- [V] ADDED — the better-evidenced modern version of Beecher's idea is stress-induced analgesia 应激性镇痛: Butler & Finn 2009 (*Prog Neurobiol* 88(3):184–202, doi:10.1016/j.pneurobio.2009.04.003, PMID 19393288; abstract read; review): "pain suppression upon exposure to unconditioned or conditioned stressful stimuli", studied for over 30 years, mediated by activation of the descending inhibitory pain pathway with opioid, monoamine, cannabinoid, GABA and glutamate systems involved; sensitivity "can vary greatly" between individuals and is influenced by age, gender and prior experience. (A mechanism review, mostly animal and lab work; no battlefield numbers.)

### 10-D · Expectation, context and nocebo change rated pain (and the brain's pain-processing, partly)
- [V] Wager et al. 2004 (*Science* 303(5661):1162–1167, doi:10.1126/science.1093065, PMID 14976306; abstract read): two fMRI experiments; placebo analgesia was related to DECREASED activity in pain-sensitive regions (thalamus, insula, anterior cingulate) and INCREASED activity in prefrontal cortex during anticipation of pain — "providing evidence that placebos alter the experience of pain". N and rating changes are not in the abstract (not used). Classic; later large synthesis below shows how much of it is nociceptive.
- [V] ★ Zunhammer, Bingel & Wager (Placebo Imaging Consortium) 2018 (*JAMA Neurol* 75(11):1321–1330, doi:10.1001/jamaneurol.2018.2017, PMID 30073258; abstract read): participant-level meta-analysis, 20 of 28 eligible fMRI studies, 603 healthy people, stimulus-matched placebo vs control. Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS; a brain pattern tracking nociceptive pain) responded to painful stimulation in 95.4% of people, g = 2.30 (95% CI 1.92–2.69). Placebo reduced PAIN RATINGS in 17 of 20 studies, pooled g = −0.66 (−0.80 to −0.53); placebo effect on the NPS was significant in only 3 of 20 studies and "very small", pooled g = −0.08 (−0.15 to −0.01); low-risk-of-bias studies g = −0.07 (−0.15 to 0.00); placebo responders only g = −0.22 (−0.34 to −0.11). Authors: placebo treatments affect pain "via brain mechanisms largely independent of effects on bottom-up nociceptive processing". READING: a moderate drop in reported pain with almost no drop in the nociceptive brain signal — expectation changes what is felt without much changing what is signalled (on this measure).
- [V] Spisak et al. 2026 (*Nat Commun* 17, doi:10.1038/s41467-026-74743-0, PMID 42469238; abstract read): newest participant-level meta-analysis, 16 within-participant placebo neuroimaging studies, n = 409: verbal suggestion alone and suggestion + conditioning both raise dorsolateral prefrontal/inferior parietal activity and lower insula/putamen/primary sensory activity during pain; adding conditioning gives greater placebo analgesia and stronger decreases in nociceptive regions and a stronger negative association between analgesia and the NPS — "partially distinct mechanisms". (Refines the 2018 result: conditioned placebo effects touch nociceptive processing more than verbal ones.)
- [V] Koyama, McHaffie, Laurienti & Coghill 2005 (*PNAS* 102(36):12950–12955, doi:10.1073/pnas.0408576102, PMID 16150703; abstract read): fMRI + psychophysics; as expected pain increased, thalamus/insula/prefrontal/ACC activation increased; "expectations of decreased pain powerfully reduced both the subjective experience of pain and activation of pain-related brain regions" (S1, insula, ACC). Sample size and rating numbers are not in the abstract (full text blocked) — not used.
- [V] Atlas, Bolger, Lindquist & Wager 2010 (*J Neurosci* 30(39):12964–12977, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0057-10.2010, PMID 20881115; abstract read): auditory cues set expectations of barely painful vs highly painful heat; matched heat stimuli; cues changed heat-evoked responses in most canonical pain regions; anterior cingulate, anterior insula and thalamus "formally mediated cue effects on pain", and those effects were mediated by anticipatory activity in medial orbitofrontal cortex and ventral striatum: "activity in pain-processing regions reflects a combination of nociceptive input and top-down information related to expectations". Atlas & Wager 2012 review (*Neurosci Lett* 520(2):140–148, doi:10.1016/j.neulet.2012.03.039, PMID 22465136; abstract read): expectancies "shape pain-intensity processing in the central nervous system, with strong effects on nociceptive portions of insula, cingulate and thalamus".
- [V] Atlas et al. 2022 (*eLife* 11, doi:10.7554/eLife.73353, PMID 36317867; abstract read): n = 40 healthy volunteers, contingencies between cues and heat REVERSED three times; predictive cues "dynamically modulated pain perception as contingencies changed" whether participants were instructed or learned from experience; expected value in the uninstructed group "updated dynamically ... as a function of error-based learning" (a delta-rule-type model fits). Motzkin et al. 2023 (*Cortex* 166:188–206, doi:10.1016/j.cortex.2023.04.017, PMID 37390595; abstract read): five adults with bilateral vmPFC lesions vs 20 controls — larger cue-based differences in expected pain, failure to update expectations after unexpected medium-heat stimuli, no group difference in overall pain sensitivity.
- [V] Jepma, Koban, van Doorn, Jones & Wager 2018 (*Nat Hum Behav* 2(11):838–855, doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0455-8, PMID 31558818; abstract read): two experiments; reported and neural pain "assimilated towards cue-based expectations", and pain responses in turn predicted later expectations (a positive feedback loop); confirmation bias in learning (higher/lower-than-expected pain updated expectations more for high/low-pain cues); computational modelling: expectations influence both perception and learning — "helping to explain why beliefs can be resistant to change".
- [V] Grahl, Onat & Büchel 2018 (*eLife* 7, doi:10.7554/eLife.32930, PMID 29555019; abstract read): n = 62 healthy participants, heat pain during fMRI; Bayesian framework: expectation (prior) is combined with nociceptive input (likelihood) and both are weighted by precision; placebo effects were larger in people with MORE PRECISE treatment expectations and correlated with the relative precision of the prior; precision effects found in periaqueductal gray and rostral ventromedial medulla. Yoshida, Seymour, Koltzenburg & Dolan 2013 (*J Neurosci* 33(13):5638–5646, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4984-12.2013, PMID 23536078; abstract read): the mean of observed other people's ratings pulled own pain toward it (assimilation) and observed UNCERTAINTY "had a specific and potent hyperalgesic effect", linked to periaqueductal gray.
- [V] Eippert et al. 2009 (*Neuron* 63(4):533–543, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2009.07.014, PMID 19709634; abstract read): placebo analgesia involves endogenous opioids — naloxone reduced behavioural and neural placebo effects, and "abolished placebo-induced coupling between rACC and PAG", which predicted both neural and behavioural placebo effects (and RVM activation). N not in the abstract.
- [V] The COMPLICATION — does expectation change how strong a real opioid is? Bingel et al. 2011 (*Sci Transl Med* 3(70):70ra14, doi:10.1126/scitranslmed.3001244, PMID 21325618; abstract read): within-subject, healthy volunteers, constant heat pain, fixed remifentanil concentration; "positive treatment expectancy substantially enhanced (doubled) the analgesic benefit of remifentanil", "negative treatment expectancy abolished remifentanil analgesia". Atlas, Whittington, Lindquist et al. 2012 (*J Neurosci* 32(23):8053–8064, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0383-12.2012, PMID 22674280; abstract read): two studies, open-hidden remifentanil delivery: remifentanil and expectancy BOTH reduced pain, but "drug effects on pain reports and fMRI activity did not interact with expectancy" — opiates and placebo "operate without mutual interference". Amanzio et al. 2001 (*Pain* 90(3):205–215, doi:10.1016/S0304-3959(00)00486-3, PMID 11207392; abstract read): hidden infusions of buprenorphine, tramadol, ketorolac and metamizol were "significantly less effective and less variable" than open ones; in healthy volunteers with ischemic arm pain the same for hidden ketorolac, and adding naloxone to an open injection removed the open-injection advantage. Colloca et al. 2004 review (*Lancet Neurol* 3(11):679–684, doi:10.1016/S1474-4422(04)00908-1, PMID 15488461; abstract read): concealed treatment is generally less effective than overt. READING: two labs/designs disagree on whether expectation multiplies drug effects (Bingel: yes, doubled; Atlas: additive, no interaction); do not teach "expectation doubles opioid effect" as settled.
- [V] Placebo size depends on how it is measured: Vase, Riley & Price 2002 (*Pain* 99(3):443–452, doi:10.1016/S0304-3959(02)00205-1, PMID 12406519; abstract read): two meta-analyses — 23 clinical analgesic trials using only a placebo control: mean effect size 0.15; 14 studies of placebo-analgesia MECHANISMS: mean effect size 0.95 (difference P = 0.003); the authors attribute the gap to differences in expected pain level produced by placebo suggestions and to conditioning. SEE FACTS_T6a STUDY "Placebo effects across conditions (Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche)" (already graded B): Cochrane 2010, pain SMD −0.28 (−0.36 to −0.19), placebo vs no treatment, 234 trials — point to it; do not redo.
- [V] Nocebo 反安慰剂效应 (negative expectation worsens pain): Petersen et al. 2014 (*Pain* 155(8):1426–1434, doi:10.1016/j.pain.2014.04.016, PMID 24780622; abstract read): meta-analysis of 10 studies (inert treatment + verbal suggestion of worsening, with a no-treatment control): effect "moderate to large", lowest g = 0.62 [0.24–1.01], highest g = 1.03 [0.63–1.43], highly variable (g range −0.43 to 4.05); similar to mechanistic placebo effects (d = 0.81); verbal suggestion + conditioning larger (g 0.76–1.17) than suggestion alone (g 0.64–0.87, lower CI crossing zero). Benedetti et al. 2006 (*J Neurosci* 26(46):12014–12022, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2947-06.2006, PMID 17108175; abstract read): verbally induced nocebo hyperalgesia in experimental ischemic arm pain went with HPA-axis hyperactivity, was antagonised by diazepam (anxiety component) and, in its hyperalgesic part, blocked by the CCK antagonist proglumide, which did nothing to basal pain. Colloca & Barsky 2020 (*N Engl J Med* 382(6):554–561, doi:10.1056/NEJMra1907805, PMID 32023375; review, no abstract in PubMed — not read).

### 10-E · Threat, attention, mood and catastrophizing modulate pain
- [V] Vlaeyen & Linton 2000 (*Pain* 85(3):317–332, doi:10.1016/S0304-3959(99)00242-0, PMID 10781906; abstract read): the fear-avoidance model (Lethem et al. 1983): fear of pain → "confrontation" (reduces fear over time) or "avoidance" (maintains/exacerbates fear, possibly a phobic state), with hypervigilance, disuse and disability; "pain-related fear and avoidance appear to be an essential feature of the development of a chronic problem for a substantial number of patients with musculoskeletal pain". Vlaeyen & Linton 2012 update (*Pain* 153(6):1144–1147, doi:10.1016/j.pain.2011.12.009, PMID 22321917; no abstract in PubMed — not read). Leeuw et al. 2007 review (*J Behav Med* 30(1):77–94, doi:10.1007/s10865-006-9085-0, PMID 17180640; abstract read): reviews evidence for each component of the model (severity, catastrophizing, attention, escape/avoidance, disability, disuse, vulnerabilities), "unresolved issues" remain.
- [V] Zale, Lange, Fields & Ditre 2013 (*J Pain* 14(10):1019–1030, doi:10.1016/j.jpain.2013.05.005, PMID 23850095; abstract read): meta-analysis, 46 independent samples (N = 9,579) of people with acute or chronic pain: cross-sectional association between pain-related fear and disability "moderate to large", stable across demographic and pain characteristics; CROSS-SECTIONAL (correlational: cannot show that fear causes disability).
- [V] Wertli et al. 2014 (*Spine J* 14(11):2639–2657, doi:10.1016/j.spinee.2014.03.003, PMID 24607845; abstract read): systematic review of prognostic studies of catastrophizing in low back pain (19 publications, 16 studies, ≥ 100 patients, follow-up ≥ 3 months): of studies with self-reported outcomes, 8 (66%) found catastrophizing associated with pain and disability at follow-up; of 6 studies with cut-offs, 5 (83%) found worse outcome in high catastrophizers; authors: "some evidence" that catastrophizing might delay recovery, "not fully established". Quartana, Campbell & Edwards 2009 (*Expert Rev Neurother* 9(5):745–758, doi:10.1586/ern.09.34, PMID 19402782; abstract read): critical review — catastrophizing as "a negative cognitive-affective response to anticipated or actual pain"; mechanisms (appraisal, attention bias, communal coping, CNS pain processing) proposed; "problem areas" in the concept.
- [V] Villemure & Bushnell 2009 (*J Neurosci* 29(3):705–715, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3822-08.2009, PMID 19158297; abstract read): heat pain with pleasant/unpleasant odours manipulating mood and attention separately; pleasant odours (positive mood) decreased pain UNPLEASANTNESS and pain-related activity in ACC, medial thalamus, S1, S2 independent of attention; attention effects were less robust (anterior insula): mood and attention modulate pain through partly separable circuits. Yoshida 2013 (above): uncertainty is hyperalgesic.
- [V] Social buffering of pain: SEE FACTS_T6c STUDY "Partner touch buffers threat (social buffering)" (already graded B) and its line on Packheiser 2024 (touch interventions g = 0.69 for pain) — point only.

### 10-F · Treatments built on the "pain is protective and modifiable" account (RESULTS ONLY — descriptive; no advice, no recommendation)
- [V] ★ Pain Reprocessing Therapy 疼痛再加工疗法 (PRT), Ashar et al. 2022 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 79(1):13–23, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.2669, PMID 34586357, NCT03294148; abstract read; PubMed lists no correction): RCT, N = 151 adults with primary chronic back pain (mean age 41.1; 54% female; mean pain 4.10/10; mean duration 10.0 y; ~85% of chronic back pain said by the authors to be "primary"), one telehealth physician session + 8 psychological sessions over 4 weeks aimed at helping people "reconceptualize their pain as due to nondangerous brain activity rather than peripheral tissue injury" (cognitive, somatic and exposure-based techniques) vs an OPEN-LABEL saline injection vs usual care. Post-treatment mean pain 1.18 (PRT) vs 2.84 (placebo) vs 3.13 (usual care) (Hedges g −1.14 vs placebo, −1.74 vs usual care). Pain-free or nearly pain-free (0 or 1 of 10): 33 of 50 (66%) PRT vs 10 of 51 (20%) placebo vs 5 of 50 (10%) usual care. At 1 year: pain 1.51 vs 2.79 vs 3.00 (g −0.70 vs placebo, −1.05 vs usual care). fMRI: reduced evoked-pain responses (anterior midcingulate, anterior PFC) vs placebo. In this trial the comparison was an open-label placebo and usual care, not another active psychological therapy; participants and therapists were not blind to treatment type (as is unavoidable for psychological treatment); one research group, one site/community sample.
- [V] PRT follow-ups and replications (as of October 2026): Ashar et al. 2025 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 82(10):1049–1051, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.1844, PMID 40736996): a research-letter "secondary analysis" giving 5-year follow-up data of the same trial — numbers NOT retrievable (no abstract beyond one line); Sturgeon et al. 2025 (*Reg Anesth Pain Med*, doi:10.1136/rapm-2025-107076, PMID 40973446; abstract read): single-arm open-label pilot, 35 adults with fibromyalgia, a 3-session brief PRT by telehealth; 33 (94.3%) completed; pain d = 0.56/0.80/0.89 at 1/2/3 months; 42.3% of completers "much" or "very much improved" at 3 months; the authors call larger RCTs necessary. Sit et al. 2026 (*J Psychosom Res* 208:112688, doi:10.1016/j.jpsychores.2026.112688, PMID 42061275; abstract read): 53 people with chronic widespread pain randomised 27 PRT vs 26 psychoeducation; a FEASIBILITY pilot with "favourable trends", no powered efficacy result. No independent, adequately powered replication of the 2022 RCT was found (as of October 2026).
- [V] Related emotion-focused pain treatment, for contrast: Yarns et al. 2024 (*JAMA Netw Open* 7(6):e2415842, doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.15842, PMID 38869899; abstract read): Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy vs CBT, 126 randomised veterans aged 60–95 (92% male) with ≥ 3 months of musculoskeletal pain: pain severity difference −1.59 (95% CI −2.35 to −0.83) post-treatment and −1.01 (−1.78 to −0.24) at 6 months; ≥ 30% pain reduction 63% vs 17% (OR 21.54, 4.66–99.56); ≥ 50% reduction 35% vs 7%. One trial, one VA site, authors' group also developed EAET.
- [V] Cognitive Functional Therapy (CFT), RESTORE trial: Kent et al. 2023 (*Lancet* 401(10391):1866–1877, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00441-5, PMID 37146623; abstract read; **two erratum notices** — *Lancet* 2023;401(10393):2040 and 2023;402(10406):964 — see sweep): 20 Australian primary-care physiotherapy clinics, 492 adults with low back pain > 3 months and at least moderate activity limitation, randomised 1:1:1 to CFT (up to 7 sessions over 12 weeks + booster at 26 weeks), CFT + movement-sensor biofeedback, or usual care; primary outcome Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (0–24) at 13 weeks: CFT mean difference vs usual care −4.6 (95% CI −5.9 to −3.4); CFT + biofeedback −4.6 (−5.8 to −3.3); similar at 52 weeks; physiotherapists and patients not masked; usual-care comparator. 3-year follow-up (Hancock et al. 2025, *Lancet Rheumatol* 7(11):e789–e798, doi:10.1016/S2665-9913(25)00135-3, PMID 40780241; abstract read): 312 of 492 followed up (63–65% of CFT arms, 62% usual care); activity-limitation difference −3.5 (−4.9 to −2.0) CFT only, −4.1 (−5.6 to −2.6) CFT + biofeedback; pain intensity (0–10) −1.0 (−1.6 to −0.5) and −1.5 (−2.1 to −0.9); attrition high. Meta-analysis Thiveos et al. 2024 (*Phys Ther* 104(12), doi:10.1093/ptj/pzae128, PMID 39236249; abstract read): 7 RCTs; "low to moderate certainty" evidence for disability and pain; "high certainty" for pain self-efficacy (medium/long term); variability by clinician training; much of the evidence comes from the originating group (O'Sullivan, Kent, Hancock are authors).
- [V] ★ Psychological therapies, the big-picture synthesis: Williams, Fisher, Hearn & Eccleston 2020 (*Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 8:CD007407, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD007407.pub4, PMID 32794606; abstract read): 75 RCTs, 9,401 participants at treatment end (mostly fibromyalgia, chronic low back pain, rheumatoid arthritis, mixed pain); CBT (59 studies): vs ACTIVE control very small benefit — pain SMD −0.09 (−0.17 to −0.01; 23 studies, 3,235 participants), disability −0.12 (−0.20 to −0.04), distress −0.09 (−0.18 to −0.00); vs treatment-as-usual small benefit — pain SMD −0.22 (−0.33 to −0.10; 29 studies, 2,572), disability −0.32 (−0.45 to −0.19; low-quality), distress −0.34 (−0.44 to −0.24); effects largely maintained at follow-up vs usual care but not vs active control; BT (8 studies) and ACT (5 studies): evidence "moderate to very low", "very uncertain"; adverse events inadequately recorded; most risk-of-bias domains high or unclear. Authors: CBT "has small or very small beneficial effects for reducing pain, disability, and distress in chronic pain".
- [V] Ho et al. 2022 (*BMJ* 376:e067718, doi:10.1136/bmj-2021-067718, PMID 35354560; abstract read): network meta-analysis, 97 RCTs, 13,136 participants, chronic non-specific low back pain; with physiotherapy care as the reference, CBT + physio improved function (SMD 1.01, 0.58–1.44) and pain education + physio (SMD 0.62, 0.08–1.17) post-intervention (moderate quality); the most sustained effects at mid-term were pain education + physio (function 0.63, 0.25–1.00; low quality); "uncertainty remains as to their long term effectiveness".
- [V] Pain neuroscience education 疼痛神经科学教育 (PNE): Watson et al. 2019 (*J Pain* 20(10):1140.e1–e22, doi:10.1016/j.jpain.2019.02.011, PMID 30831273; abstract read): 12 RCTs (n = 755) in chronic musculoskeletal pain: pooled PNE vs control effects had "low clinical relevance" for pain — short term −5.91/100 (95% CI −13.75 to 1.93), medium term −6.27/100 (−18.97 to 6.44), both CIs crossing zero — and small for disability (−4.09/100, −7.72 to −0.45; medium −8.14/100, −15.60 to −0.68); kinesiophobia −13.55/100 (−25.89 to −1.21) short term (clinically relevant); catastrophizing −5.26/52 (−10.59 to 0.08) medium term. Cancela et al. 2025 umbrella review (*Ann Phys Rehabil Med* 68(8):102020, doi:10.1016/j.rehab.2025.102020, PMID 41005108; abstract read): 19 systematic reviews, 5,200 participants: PNE alone short-term pain reductions, inconsistent long-term; PNE + exercise pooled mean difference −1.11 points (95% CI −1.57 to −0.66) for pain, disability SMD −0.42 (−1.09 to 0.24; crossing zero). Louw et al. 2016 (*Physiother Theory Pract* 32(5):332–355, PMID 27351541; not read). READING: knowledge of pain biology alone is a small lever on pain; results are better when combined with exercise or a broader programme.

### 10-G · Phantom limbs and mirror therapy (evidence for "pain can persist where no tissue exists")
- [~] Chan et al. 2007 (*N Engl J Med* 357(21):2206–2207, doi:10.1056/NEJMc071927, PMID 18032777; a letter — PubMed has no abstract; NEJM page blocked; numbers as relayed by a secondary search summary, hence [~]): 22 lower-limb amputees randomised to mirror therapy, covered mirror or mental visualization; 18 (six per group) completed 4 weeks of 15 min daily; all 6 in the mirror group (100%) had significant pain improvement (median VAS decrease 24 mm), vs 1 of 6 (17%) with the covered mirror and 3 of 6 (50%) with mental visualization; 8 of 9 who crossed over to mirror therapy improved. Tiny, unblinded-by-nature, a letter.
- [V] Brodie, Whyte & Niven 2007 (*Eur J Pain* 11(4):428–436, doi:10.1016/j.ejpain.2006.06.002, PMID 16857400; abstract read): RCT, 80 lower-limb amputees, mirror (viewing a "virtual" limb) vs control (viewing only the intact limb) during repeated attempts to move both legs: the mirror condition produced more phantom limb MOVEMENTS but "did not attenuate phantom limb pain and sensations any more than the control condition".
- [V] Xie et al. 2022 (*Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 103(5):988–997, doi:10.1016/j.apmr.2021.07.810, PMID 34461084; abstract read): meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (from 2,094 articles): pain lower with mirror therapy within 1 month, SMD −0.46 (95% CI −0.79 to −0.13), I² = 0%; people with pain > 1 year benefited more (SMD −0.46, −0.85 to −0.07); no evidence of a long-term effect (limited data). Guémann et al. 2023 (*Eur J Pain* 27(1):3–13, doi:10.1002/ejp.2035, PMID 36094758; abstract read): systematic review restricted to placebo-controlled RCTs: of five studies "only one reported a significant difference" at week 4 — "does not find any evidence of efficacy", low methodological quality and low power.

### 10-H · "Pain is a prediction": the framing is a lens, not a finding
- [V] Büchel, Geuter, Sprenger & Eippert 2014 (*Neuron* 81(6):1223–1239, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2014.02.042, PMID 24656247; abstract read): Perspective proposing that the ascending and descending pain systems form a recurrent system implementing predictive coding — "the brain is not passively waiting for nociceptive stimuli to impinge on it but is actively making inferences based on prior experience and expectations"; the Bayesian formulation accounts for magnitude AND precision of expectations; proposes experimental tests. A conceptual account, not a test.
- [V] Ongaro & Kaptchuk 2019 (*Pain* 160(1):1–4, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001367, PMID 30086114; PubMed shows no abstract — not read; title only: "Symptom perception, placebo effects, and the Bayesian brain"). Empirical tests of the Bayesian reading that I did open: Grahl 2018 (n = 62: larger placebo effects with more precise expectations; PAG/RVM), Yoshida 2013 (uncertainty increases pain), Jepma 2018 (self-reinforcing expectancy), Atlas 2022 (error-based updating) — all in 10-D. Tabor & Burr 2019 ("Bayesian learning models of pain: a call to action", *Curr Opin Behav Sci*) could not be located in PubMed/Europe PMC — not cited.

### 10-I · Social pain and physical pain
- [V] Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams 2003 (*Science* 302(5643):290–292, doi:10.1126/science.1089134, PMID 14551436; abstract read): exclusion from a virtual ball-tossing game increased anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) activity, correlated with self-reported distress; right ventral prefrontal cortex activity correlated negatively with distress — read as "social pain overlaps physical pain". Woo et al. 2014 (*Nat Commun* 5:5380, doi:10.1038/ncomms6380, PMID 25400102; abstract read): 60 participants experienced painful heat and viewed photos of ex-partners (rejection); multivariate fMRI classifiers discriminated pain and rejection from controls at 92% and 80% out of sample, but "the rejection classifier performs at chance on pain, and vice versa"; patterns uncorrelated even in dorsal ACC — "separate representations underlie pain and rejection despite common fMRI activity at the gross anatomical level". Eisenberger 2012 review (*Psychosom Med* 74(2):126–135, doi/PMID 22286852; not opened beyond the record).

### 10-J · Brain measures of pain: a nociceptive signature, a "beyond nociception" signature, and the "pain matrix" critique (ADDED)
- [V] Wager et al. 2013 (*N Engl J Med* 368(15):1388–1397, doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1204471, PMID 23574118; abstract read): four studies, 114 participants; a machine-learning fMRI pattern (thalamus, posterior/anterior insulae, S2, ACC, PAG and others) — the Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS) — discriminated painful heat from non-painful warmth with sensitivity and specificity ≥ 94% (95% CI 89–98) in study 1 and 93% (84–100) in a new sample; physical vs social pain at 85% sensitivity and 73% specificity (forced choice 95%); the signature response was "substantially reduced" with remifentanil. Not yet shown to predict clinical pain ("Future studies are needed").
- [V] Woo et al. 2017 (*Nat Commun* 8:14211, doi:10.1038/ncomms14211, PMID 28195170; abstract read): the stimulus intensity independent pain signature-1 (SIIPS1), trained on four data sets (N = 137) and tested on two independent sets (N = 46): predicts trial-by-trial pain ratings "above and beyond nociceptive input" (not captured by the NPS) and "mediate[s] the pain-modulating effects of three psychological manipulations of expectations and perceived control"; includes nucleus accumbens, lateral prefrontal and parahippocampal regions. READING: there are measurable brain contributions to pain that are not nociceptive, and they carry expectation effects.
- [V] Legrain, Iannetti, Plaghki & Mouraux 2011 (*Prog Neurobiol* 93(1):111–124, doi:10.1016/j.pneurobio.2010.10.005, PMID 21040755; abstract read): the "pain matrix" (S1/S2, insula, cingulate, frontal, parietal) is not pain-specific — pain intensity can be dissociated from its response magnitude, responses depend on context, and non-nociceptive stimuli can evoke similar responses; they propose it is a salience-detection / defensive system, "emphasis is no longer on the quality of the sensation ... but on the action prompted by the occurrence of potential threats". NOTE for writers: do NOT say "a pain centre lights up"; say a pattern (NPS) discriminates painful heat and is largely unchanged by placebo, while another (SIIPS1) tracks the expectation-driven part.

- STUDY Spinal MRI findings occur in pain-free people — original: Boden 1990 (N = 67) and Jensen 1994 (N = 98: 52% bulge at ≥ 1 level). Later evidence: Brinjikji 2015 (33 studies, 3,110 asymptomatic: degeneration 37% at 20 y → 96% at 80 y; bulge 30% → 84%); Brinjikji 2015b (14 studies: several findings more common with pain, ORs 2.2–7.5, wide CIs); knee (Guermazi 2012, N = 710: 86–88% of painless knees abnormal; Culvenor 2019: 63 studies) and shoulder (Sher 1995, Minagawa 2013) agree.
  GRADE A: independent cohorts and systematic reviews across spine, knee and shoulder agree that common degenerative findings are frequent without pain and rise with age. Scope: it does NOT show that scans are useless, that pain is unrelated to structure, or that a specific finding (e.g. a large extrusion with matching nerve signs) is irrelevant; the direction of the pain–finding association is positive but weak and non-specific. — Brinjikji 2015a/b; Jensen 1994; Guermazi 2012; Culvenor 2019
- STUDY Beecher's wounded soldiers (1946) — original: Beecher 1946, 215 seriously wounded men interviewed at the front: 27% wanted pain relief, 24% reported "bad" pain, 32% no pain (second-hand table). Later evidence: none found that replicates the design; the 1956 civilian comparison was not opened; critiques cited in the placebo literature concern Beecher's separate 1955 "powerful placebo" paper (see FACTS_T6a, Kienle & Kiene 1997), not this one.
  GRADE C: a historically important uncontrolled observation whose popular summary ("soldiers feel no pain") overstates what the paper reports; no independent replication of the design found, numbers not verified from the primary text. Teach as a hypothesis (context shapes pain), never as a statistic. — Beecher 1946 via transcription
- STUDY Nav1.7 loss-of-function abolishes pain in humans — original: Cox 2006 (3 Pakistani families, 3 nonsense mutations). Later evidence: Goldberg 2007 (9 families, 7 nationalities, 10 SCN9A mutations, 9 truncating); Minett 2015 (opioid contribution; one human mutant plus mice).
  GRADE A for "SCN9A loss-of-function causes congenital insensitivity to pain in humans" (independent families and labs, converging genetics and electrophysiology); GRADE B for the interpretation "pure absence of the nociceptive signal" (endogenous opioids contribute; selective Nav1.7 blockers were weak analgesics). — Cox 2006; Goldberg 2007; Minett 2015
- STUDY Phantom limb pain is common after amputation — original: clinical case literature. Later evidence: Limakatso 2020 (pooled 64%, 95% CI 60.01–68.05, I² ≈ 96%, lower in developing countries 54% vs 67%); Limakatso 2024 survey (71.7%, 65.5–77.5).
  GRADE A for "a majority of people with an amputation report phantom limb pain at some point, with very large between-study variation (about 50–75%)"; the pooled number depends strongly on definitions, time since amputation and country. — Limakatso 2020; Limakatso 2024
- STUDY Expectation changes rated pain, not nociception — original: Wager 2004 / Koyama 2005 / Atlas 2010 (small fMRI studies: cues and placebo cut pain-region activity and rated pain). Later evidence: Zunhammer 2018 (20 studies, N = 603: ratings g = −0.66; NPS g = −0.08); Spisak 2026 (16 studies, n = 409: conditioning adds nociceptive-region effects); Atlas 2022 (n = 40: cues track reversing contingencies); Jepma 2018 (self-reinforcing loop).
  GRADE A for "positive/negative expectations and context change reported pain by a moderate amount in lab pain" (large participant-level meta-analysis plus independent replications); GRADE B for "this is mostly independent of the nociceptive brain signal" (one consortium, one signature, partly qualified by 2026 conditioning data). — Zunhammer 2018; Spisak 2026
- STUDY Expectation multiplies a real analgesic's effect — original: Bingel 2011 (remifentanil benefit doubled with positive expectancy, abolished with negative). Later evidence: Atlas 2012 (two studies: drug and expectancy effects additive, no interaction); Amanzio 2001 and Colloca 2004 (hidden infusions less effective than open).
  GRADE C: the hidden-vs-open clinical findings favour an expectation contribution, but the two best-controlled imaging/drug studies disagree on interaction; small samples, healthy volunteers, experimental pain only. — Bingel 2011; Atlas 2012
- STUDY Nocebo suggestion increases pain — original: Benedetti 2006 (verbal suggestion → hyperalgesia, HPA axis and CCK involvement). Later evidence: Petersen 2014 (10 studies: g 0.62–1.03, very variable, range −0.43 to 4.05).
  GRADE B: direction consistent in experimental pain and moderate to large on average, but only 10 eligible studies with huge variability and mostly lab pain; clinical sizes unknown. — Petersen 2014; Benedetti 2006
- STUDY Lab placebo analgesia exceeds clinical-trial size — original: Vase 2002 (14 mechanism studies mean ES 0.95 vs 23 clinical trials 0.15). Later evidence: Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche 2010 Cochrane (pain SMD −0.28 vs no treatment; FACTS_T6a); Zunhammer 2018 (healthy, lab: g −0.66).
  GRADE B: the ordering (lab suggestion + conditioning ≫ clinical trial placebo) is consistent across three syntheses; the clinical effect is small and partly unblinded-reporting-bias; do not teach "placebo = 30%". — Vase 2002; Hróbjartsson 2010; Zunhammer 2018
- STUDY Pain-related fear goes with more disability — original: Lethem 1983 fear-avoidance model; Vlaeyen & Linton 2000 review. Later evidence: Zale 2013 (46 samples, N = 9,579: moderate-to-large cross-sectional fear–disability association); Wertli 2014 (catastrophizing prognostic in 66–83% of studies, "not fully established"); Leeuw 2007 (components supported, unresolved issues).
  GRADE B: the association is large and replicated across hundreds of studies, but evidence is mostly correlational/prognostic and the causal pathway (fear → avoidance → disuse) is only partly tested; do not teach it as a proven chain. — Zale 2013; Wertli 2014; Vlaeyen & Linton 2000
- STUDY Mood and attention modulate pain separately — original: Villemure & Bushnell 2009 (fMRI: pleasant odour lowers unpleasantness via ACC/thalamus/S1/S2 independent of attention). Later evidence: Yoshida 2013 (observed uncertainty → hyperalgesia, PAG); no large replication found.
  GRADE C: neuroimaging samples are small (N not in the abstracts), single-lab designs, and the "separable circuits" claim rests on contrasts of limited power. — Villemure & Bushnell 2009; Yoshida 2013
- STUDY Pain Reprocessing Therapy for chronic back pain — original: Ashar 2022 (N = 151: 66% vs 20% vs 10% pain-free or nearly pain-free at post-treatment, PRT vs open-label placebo vs usual care; g −0.70 vs placebo at 1 year). Later evidence: 5-year follow-up research letter 2025 (numbers not retrieved); Sturgeon 2025 (single-arm pilot, fibromyalgia, n = 35); Sit 2026 (pilot RCT n = 53, feasibility only); no independent powered replication as of October 2026.
  GRADE C: one randomized trial from the developing group with large effects against a non-active-psychotherapy comparator, unblinded by nature, not yet independently replicated; teach as "an unusually large result from a single trial". — Ashar 2022; Sit 2026
- STUDY Cognitive Functional Therapy for low back pain — original: Kent 2023 RESTORE (N = 492: RMDQ −4.6 vs usual care at 13 weeks). Later evidence: Hancock 2025 3-year follow-up (312 followed up; −3.5 to −4.1 on RMDQ; pain −1.0 to −1.5); Thiveos 2024 meta-analysis (7 RCTs, low–moderate certainty).
  GRADE B: a large well-conducted multicentre RCT with a 3-year follow-up and a supportive 7-trial meta-analysis, but non-blinded, compared with usual care (not with an equally intensive active treatment), high attrition at 3 years, and much of the evidence from the model's originators. — Kent 2023; Hancock 2025; Thiveos 2024
- STUDY Psychological therapies for chronic pain — original: CBT/behavioural/ACT trials from the 1980s onward. Later evidence: Williams 2020 Cochrane (75 RCTs, N = 9,401: CBT pain SMD −0.22 vs usual care, −0.09 vs active control); Ho 2022 (97 RCTs, N = 13,136: effects larger when delivered with physiotherapy care).
  GRADE A for "psychological therapies produce small average benefits for pain, disability and distress compared with usual care" (two large syntheses, consistent direction); GRADE B for "better than other active treatments" (very small or none vs active controls). — Williams 2020; Ho 2022
- STUDY Pain neuroscience education improves chronic pain — original: Louw 2016 (systematic review, not read). Later evidence: Watson 2019 (12 RCTs, n = 755: pain −5.9/100, CI crossing zero; kinesiophobia −13.6/100); Cancela 2025 umbrella review (19 reviews: PNE + exercise −1.11 points).
  GRADE C: effects on pain alone are small and imprecise, larger when bundled with exercise or other care; low–moderate quality trials. — Watson 2019; Cancela 2025
- STUDY Mirror therapy relieves phantom limb pain — original: Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran 1996 (10 patients, observations); Chan 2007 (22 randomised, 18 completed: 6/6 vs 1/6 vs 3/6, second-hand). Later evidence: Brodie 2007 (N = 80: no pain advantage over control); Xie 2022 (10 RCTs: SMD −0.46 within 1 month); Guémann 2023 (5 placebo-controlled RCTs: one positive, "no evidence of efficacy").
  GRADE C: small positive trials and a meta-analysis of short-term benefit versus a placebo-controlled review finding no evidence; mixed, low-power, hard-to-blind. Teach the phantom itself (A) separately from the therapy (C). — Xie 2022; Guémann 2023; Brodie 2007
- STUDY Pain as a Bayesian/predictive-coding estimate — original: Büchel 2014 (Perspective); Ongaro & Kaptchuk 2019 (not read). Later evidence: Grahl 2018 (n = 62: placebo effect scales with relative precision of the prior); Yoshida 2013 (observed uncertainty hyperalgesic); Jepma 2018 (confirmation-biased learning); Atlas 2022 (n = 40: cue effects follow reversing contingencies with error-based learning).
  GRADE B for "expectations (their mean AND their reliability) and learned cue values combine with nociceptive input to shape reported pain" (several labs, consistent direction, small fMRI samples); GRADE C for the stronger claim that pain IS a Bayesian posterior or that predictive coding is THE mechanism (a framework; no unique prediction yet isolated; no published parameter set for a precision-weighted pain model beyond within-study fits). — Büchel 2014; Grahl 2018; Jepma 2018
- STUDY Social rejection shares the neural basis of pain — original: Eisenberger 2003 (fMRI, ball-toss exclusion; ACC). Later evidence: Woo 2014 (N = 60: separate multivariate patterns; classifiers at chance across domains); Eisenberger 2012 (shared-representation review, not opened).
  GRADE C: the shared-activation finding is real at coarse anatomy but the strong "same circuitry" reading was contradicted by multivariate pattern analysis; contested. Use as "social pain also uses the word pain, with overlapping but not identical brain activity". — Woo 2014; Eisenberger 2003
- STUDY Brain signatures split nociceptive from other pain — original: Wager 2013 (NPS, N = 114: ≥ 93–94% accuracy for heat pain vs warmth; 85%/73% vs social pain); Woo 2017 (SIIPS1, N = 137 + 46). Later evidence: Zunhammer 2018 (20 studies, N = 603: NPS responded in 95.4%, g = 2.30; placebo g = −0.08 on NPS vs −0.66 on ratings); Legrain 2011 (pain-matrix non-specificity).
  GRADE B: the signatures replicate in healthy volunteers across labs in experimental heat pain, but they are not diagnostic tests, have not been shown to predict clinical pain, and the "nociceptive vs beyond-nociceptive" split depends on a specific model; do not teach a pain "readout". — Wager 2013; Woo 2017; Zunhammer 2018

### Myths and corrections
- [✗] "Pain tells you how much tissue is damaged." — IASP 2020 note 2: "Pain and nociception are different phenomena. Pain cannot be inferred solely from activity in sensory neurons." Evidence both ways: no pain with damage (Cox 2006 SCN9A families; CIPA series) and no damage required for pain (phantom limb pain 64% pooled; nociplastic pain, contested). Pain is real as an experience in every case (IASP note 4: a person's report "should be respected").
- [✗] "A scan abnormality shows why your back/knee/shoulder hurts." — findings that are common without pain: disc degeneration 37% at age 20 and 96% at age 80, bulge 30% → 84% (Brinjikji 2015, 3,110 pain-free people); any knee MRI abnormality in 86–88% of painless knees without radiographic OA (Guermazi 2012); rotator-cuff tears 34% of 96 asymptomatic shoulders (Sher 1995) and 65.3% of full-thickness tears in a village screening were asymptomatic (Minagawa 2013). Counter-correction ([✗] "so scans are meaningless"): in adults ≤ 50, several findings are MORE common with back pain (disc bulge OR 7.54, extrusion 4.38, protrusion 2.65, degeneration 2.24; wide CIs; Brinjikji 2015b) and the authors say findings "must be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical condition".
- [✗] "Soldiers in battle feel no pain / most refused morphine" (Beecher). — only 32% of 215 wounded men reported no pain; 73% did not WANT relief, most of whom had already received morphine; 24% reported bad pain; uncontrolled; the civilian contrast is from a different (1956) paper not verified here (see 10-C). Grade C.
- [✗] "Placebo analgesia means the pain was 'only imagined'." — placebo lowered REPORTED pain by g ≈ −0.66 in 20 fMRI studies while the nociceptive brain signature barely moved (g −0.08, Zunhammer 2018); naloxone reduces placebo analgesia (Eippert 2009) — so the change in felt pain is a real change in a brain-generated experience, not a fiction; and in clinical trials the effect vs no treatment is small (pain SMD −0.28, FACTS_T6a).
- [✗] "About 30% of people respond to a placebo." — FACTS_T6a [✗] (Beecher 1955; Kienle & Kiene 1997); point only.
- [✗] "Pain is all in your head" / "it's just your brain" — a slogan the evidence does not support: IASP note 1 says biological, psychological AND social factors; the best-described brain effects of expectation are moderate (g ≈ 0.6–1.0 in the lab, ≈ 0.15–0.28 in clinical trials), cannot erase a strong stimulus, and the "nociplastic" category is itself contested (Häuser & Kosek 2026). Writers: say "pain is produced by the brain from many inputs", never "is imaginary".
- [✗] "Congenital insensitivity to pain would be a gift." — the clinical series show repeated unnoticed injuries, fractures, joint destruction, infections and (in CIPA with anhidrosis) death from hyperthermia in almost 20% in the first 3 years (Rosemberg 1994; Bar-On 2002).
- [✗] "Mirror therapy cures phantom pain" — mixed: short-term benefit in a meta-analysis (SMD −0.46 within 1 month, Xie 2022) vs "no evidence of efficacy" in placebo-controlled RCTs (Guémann 2023); an 80-person RCT found none (Brodie 2007). Grade C.
- [✗] "The pain matrix is the brain's pain centre" — Legrain 2011: not pain-specific; salience/defence network; use the NPS/SIIPS1 vocabulary.
- [✗] "Expectation doubles opioid analgesia" (a popular summary of Bingel 2011) — a second well-controlled study found additive, non-interacting effects (Atlas 2012). Grade C.
- Better than its reputation: the 1965 gate-control idea (pain is modulated top-down and locally) is now textbook; Cox 2006 SCN9A genetics replicated in nine more families; imaging-in-the-pain-free findings (Boden 1990 → Brinjikji 2015) replicated across spine, knee and shoulder for 35 years; fear-avoidance's cross-sectional correlation (N = 9,579) is robust.

### Widget constants (a toy "pain = nociception + expectation, precision-weighted" model)
- [V] Published effects on a pain scale that the toy model may reproduce in SHAPE (not in exact value): placebo/expectation effect on rated pain, healthy lab pain: g = −0.66 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.53; 20 fMRI studies, N = 603; Zunhammer 2018) while nociceptive brain response g = −0.08 (−0.15 to −0.01); nocebo g = 0.62–1.03 (Petersen 2014, 10 studies); mechanism-study placebo ES 0.95 vs clinical-trial ES 0.15 (Vase 2002); clinical placebo vs no-treatment pain SMD −0.28 (Hróbjartsson 2010, FACTS_T6a).
- [V] PRT trial means on 0–10 (post-treatment): 1.18 (PRT) vs 2.84 (open-label placebo injection) vs 3.13 (usual care); baseline mean 4.10 (SD 1.26) (Ashar 2022) — a published real-world example for "same back, different belief about threat"; one trial, do not generalise.
- [V] Precision-weighted structure is published qualitatively: Büchel 2014 (prior × likelihood weighted by relative precision), Grahl 2018 (n = 62: placebo effect correlates positively with the relative precision of the prior). The standard Gaussian cue-combination formula is the lesson-03 machinery (SEE FACTS_T2 STUDY "Perception as Bayesian inference"): posterior mean = (π_prior·μ_prior + π_input·x) / (π_prior + π_input), with π = 1/variance (DERIVED standard Bayes; not a pain-specific finding).
- [~] Stimulus–rating relation: Stevens' power-law exponents as tabulated second-hand — electric shock ≈ 3.5, radiant heat on skin ≈ 1.0 (from Wikipedia/RIT-type tables reproducing Stevens 1957; tables differ between sources; exponents depend on experimental conditions and on how the 0-point is set). No exponent for heat pain was verified from a primary source (searches returned only the shock figure with confidence). The widget should therefore label the stimulus→rating curve 示意.
- NOT in the literature (widget labels these 示意): numeric values for prior mean, prior precision, likelihood precision of a pain model that apply across people; a "threat estimate" scale; any conversion from g to points on a 0–100 scale (the abstracts give g, not points; only PRT and Cochrane/PNE meta-analyses give points on 0–10 or 0–100).
- [V] Dynamic learning: Atlas 2022 (n = 40, reversing cue-outcome contingencies; error-based updating fits the uninstructed group) and Jepma 2018 (confirmation-biased updating; positive feedback loop between pain and expectation) justify a "delta-rule updating with expectation pulling reports toward the cue" toy loop; no published learning-rate values were extracted from the abstracts.

### Boundary (feeds 「这个模型的边界」)
- Expectation does not abolish pain: placebo/nocebo effects are moderate in the lab (g ≈ 0.6–1.0), small in clinical trials against no treatment (pain SMD −0.28), and the nociceptive brain pattern barely changes (g −0.08).
- Imaging findings in the pain-free do not mean ignoring symptoms or that structure is irrelevant: associations with pain exist (ORs 2–7.5 for several lumbar findings in adults ≤ 50) and specific findings with matching signs matter clinically; the lesson makes no claim about when to image or treat.
- Chronic pain mechanisms are heterogeneous (nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic; ICD-11 secondary vs primary pain); the "primary pain ~85% of chronic back pain" figure is the trial authors' statement, not a measured prevalence; nociplastic pain is a contested construct (Häuser & Kosek 2026).
- Treatments that target pain beliefs have strongest single-trial results (PRT 66% vs 20% vs 10%) and modest meta-analytic ones (CBT SMD −0.22 vs usual care; PNE −5.9/100 with CI crossing zero); PRT lacks independent powered replication; none of this is advice.
- "Pain is in the brain" misleads if heard as "pain is imaginary" or "it is only psychological"; IASP note 1 (biological + psychological + social) and note 4 (respect the report) are the guardrails.
- Predictive-coding is a lens: several labs show precision-weighted/learned expectations modulate reported pain, but no study isolates a unique prediction of "pain IS a prediction" against simpler gain-control accounts; all models here are 示意.
- Most expectation/imaging results are healthy volunteers, short heat/ischemic pain, small fMRI samples; the sizes do not transfer one-to-one to years-long clinical pain.

### 14-A · What a day of desire looks like (experience sampling)
- [V] ★ Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs 2012 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 102(6):1318–1335, doi:10.1037/a0026545, PMID 22149456; abstract AND full text read — author-hosted PDF at assets.csom.umn.edu, run through pdftotext; PubMed lists no correction): Würzburg (Germany), 208 recruited, 205 analysed (data of 3 lost), 66% female, aged 18–55 (M 25.24), 73% university students; BlackBerry beepers, 7 consecutive days, 7 random signals per day within a 14-hour window; 92.2% of signals completed (7.5% unanswered); 10,558 responded occasions; 7,827 desire reports. Results as printed in the paper: a current desire on 49.9% of responded occasions (a recent desire on 26.7%; neither on 27.6% — percentages as printed, they overlap); most frequent domains: eat 28.1%, sleep 10.3%, drink 8.6%, media 8.1%, leisure 7.2%, social 7.1%, hygiene 5.9%, tobacco 4.8%, sex 4.6%, work 3.0%, coffee 2.9%, alcohol 2.7%, sports 2.6%, spending 2.2%; mean desire strength 4.08 on 0–7, 6.3% rated "irresistible"; median duration 16–20 min; conflict with a personal goal: mean 1.08 on 0–4 — 53.2% of desires not conflicting at all, 14.7% mildly, 12.4% somewhat, 10.9% quite, 8.8% highly (so about 47% conflicted at least slightly; about 20% "quite" or "highly"); desires were actively resisted on 42% of occasions and enacted on 48%. Authors: "desires are frequent, variable in intensity, and largely unproblematic".
- [V] ★ Resistance works, mostly: without an attempt to resist the desire-related behaviour was enacted 69.6% of the time; with an attempt, 17.4% ("self-control reduced the enactment ... from 70% to 17%"); about 40% of desires were resisted, which made up about 20% of all sampling occasions; failing to resist on about 3% of occasions (authors' discussion). Desire strength was unrelated to conflict (r = .0003) and did not predict resistance; when people resisted, strong desires were more often enacted (2.4× at strength 7 vs 1: 25.8% vs 10.8%), while without resistance strength made no difference. "Truly irresistible motivations are quite uncommon."
- [V] ★ Trait self-control (Brief Self-Control Scale, M 3.06, SD 0.59, α = .80; Level-2 predictor, controlling domain, age, gender): the paper's own test of two rival hypotheses — (a) high-SC people resist MORE or more effectively; (b) they avoid temptation. Results: higher trait self-control went with LOWER average desire strength (B −0.27, p < .001; ±1 SD ≈ 3.88 vs 4.20 on 0–7), LESS conflict (B −0.16, p < .001), and LESS resistance once conflict was controlled (log-odds B −0.23, p = .045; ±1 SD ≈ .35 vs .42 probability), no direct effect on enactment, and it moderated neither the conflict→resistance nor the resistance→enactment link. The absolute frequency of desires was NOT related to any of the five traits (all p > .15, mean |r| = .04) — so high-SC people did not have FEWER desires; they had weaker, less conflicting ones. An independent sample of 69 raters scored each desire type for "how problematic"; trait self-control was the only trait correlating with that problem index (r = −.20, p < .01): high-SC people encounter the kinds of desires others judge less problematic. Authors: "high self-control operates more by avoiding temptations in the first place than by resisting them"; "the result is not a desire-free life ... they mainly have desires that they can satisfy"; they enact their desires "at roughly the same rate" as low-SC people.
- [~] Printed-label glitch: the paper's text gives conflict "M+1SD = 1.21; M−1SD = 1.02" for trait self-control, which contradicts its own coefficient and its words ("less conflict"); DERIVED check — B = −0.16 × 1.18 (±1 SD of 0.59) = −0.19, so high-SC ≈ 1.02 and low-SC ≈ 1.21; the labels in the printed sentence appear transposed. Use the coefficient and the words, not the two printed means.
- [V] Hofmann, Vohs & Baumeister 2012 (*Psychol Sci* 23(6):582–588, doi:10.1177/0956797612437426, PMID 22547657; abstract read; same sample): desires for sleep and sex strongest, for tobacco and alcohol weakest on average ("despite ... thought of as addictive"); leisure and sleep conflicted most with goals; media use and work brought the most self-control failure; "support for a limited-resource model": the frequency and recency of prior self-control that day negatively predicted success at resisting later desires (this is the ego-depletion-style claim graded D elsewhere; it is a correlational ESM result, not a replication).
- [V] Hofmann, Luhmann, Fisher, Vohs & Baumeister 2014 (*J Pers* 82(4):265–277, doi:10.1111/jopy.12050, PMID 23750741; abstract read): three studies (N = 414, 208, 234; Study 2 is the same ESM sample, M age 25.24): trait self-control positively related to affective well-being and life satisfaction, with "managing goal conflict" the key reason; high-SC people felt better "even as they experience desire", partly through lower conflict and distress; "Self-control positively contributes to happiness through avoiding and dealing with motivational conflict."
- [V] ★ Milyavskaya & Inzlicht 2017 (*Soc Psychol Personal Sci* 8(6):603–611, doi:10.1177/1948550616679237; abstract via Crossref + full text read from the lab-hosted PDF): 159 first-year McGill students (smartphones), baseline trait self-control + four self-nominated goals; 7 days × 5 text-message surveys (10 a.m.–10 p.m.) + nightly diary; 151 students answered ≥ 1 signal (3,615 momentary surveys, 68% response; 955 nightly surveys); analysed observations with a current/recent desire (64.3% of occasions; N = 2,323); end-of-semester goal progress from 107 students (68%; 399 goal reports). Self-control = a single item ("tried to resist or control the desire", −3…+3, M 0.36); temptation = desire strength × goal conflict (÷ 7). Results (exploratory, multilevel SEM): goal progress was predicted by how tempted people were, not by how much they resisted; "effortful self-control was consistently unrelated to goal attainment"; Bayesian comparison (BIC-based): the model with self-control fixed at 0 beat the free model, BF01 ≈ 24.4 (within-person) and 23.5 (between-person) in the main model, ≈ 201.8 in the mediation model; temptation also raised felt depletion directly, and depletion (between-person) mediated temptation → poorer goal progress (indirect effect −.15 [−.29, −.02]). Authors flag the study as "primarily exploratory". Limits: one university-student sample, a one-item self-report of resistance, BIC-approximated Bayes factors, goal reports lost to attrition (68% completed).
- [V] Milyavskaya, Inzlicht, Hope & Koestner 2015 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 109(4):677–693, doi:10.1037/pspp0000045, PMID 25984785; abstract read): four studies (implicit affect, obstacle reports, expended effort, ESM): "want-to" motivation lowered impulsive attraction to goal-disruptive temptations and went with fewer obstacles — which explained why want-to goals were more likely attained; "have-to" motivation related to more perceived obstacles and tempting desires, unrelated to automatic reactions. (Temptation reduced rather than control increased; sample sizes not in the abstract.)
- [V] ★ Counter-evidence — strategies in daily life: Milyavskaya, Saunders & Inzlicht 2021 (*J Pers* 89(4):634–651, doi:10.1111/jopy.12604, PMID 33128774; abstract read): ESM, N = 197 (M age 20.4; 63% female), 4,462 desires over a week; participants used at least one of six strategies (situation modification, distraction, reminding self of goals, promising to give in later, reminder of why it is bad, willpower) 89% of the time and more than one 25% of the time; "In contrast to recent theoretical predictions, we find that many strategies, including inhibition, are similarly effective and that using multiple strategies is especially effective." READING: situation modification was NOT shown to beat plain willpower in this ESM study — the "situation > willpower" story is theory plus lab/field findings, not yet a settled ESM result.

### 14-B · Habits and "effortless" self-control
- [V] ★ Galla & Duckworth 2015 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 109(3):508–525, doi:10.1037/pspp0000026, PMID 25643222; abstract read): six studies, total N = 2,274; the proposal that people with better self-control "use less effortful inhibition, yet make better progress" because they rely on beneficial habits: Study 1 habits for eating healthy snacks, exercising and sleeping consistently mediated the effect of self-control on automaticity and on lower reported effortful inhibition; Studies 2–3 study habits mediated reduced motivational interference and greater ability to study under difficulty; Study 4 homework habits mediated classroom engagement and homework completion; Study 5 (prospective, teenagers after a 5-day meditation retreat): better self-control before the retreat predicted stronger meditation habits 3 months later, and habits mediated goal accomplishment; Study 6 study habits mediated the effect on homework completion, GPA and first-year college persistence. Mediation, mostly self-reported habits and correlational/longitudinal; the authors' own claim: habits "perhaps more so than effortful inhibition" link self-control with outcomes.
- [V] Gillebaart & de Ridder 2015 (*Soc Personal Psychol Compass* 9(2):88–99, doi:10.1111/spc3.12160; abstract via Crossref): conceptual review proposing that "effortless" strategies for dealing with response conflict — avoiding it through adaptive, habitual behaviours and downregulating it efficiently — distinguish successful from less successful self-controllers; people with high trait self-control "do not seem to subjectively experience this conflict as much". Gillebaart, Schneider & De Ridder 2016 (*J Pers* 84(6):789–798, doi:10.1111/jopy.12219, PMID 26269964; abstract via Crossref): Study 1 N = 146, Study 2 N = 118: self-reported evaluative response conflict about food items was smaller for people high in trait self-control; higher trait self-control predicted faster resolution of conflict and an earlier peak of response conflict. Gillebaart 2018 (*Front Psychol* 9:1231, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01231, PMID 30072939; abstract read): proposes an "operational" definition — self-regulation = scaffolding for goal pursuit (standards, monitoring), self-control = everything done in the "operate" phase of the TOTE model (definitional, no data).
- [V] Imhoff, Schmidt & Gerstenberg 2014 (*Eur J Pers* 28(5):413–424, doi:10.1002/per.1899; abstract via Crossref): two studies (total N = 264): "greater ego depletion effects for high (vs. low) self-control abilities" in candy consumption, risk taking and achievement motivation; proposed reason: high-trait-self-control people "less frequent active inhibition of impulses in everyday life" and so less practice resisting acute temptations; Study 3 (N ≥ 358): trait self-control associated with LESS frequent impulse inhibition in daily routines. (The depletion paradigms themselves are graded D — read the ironic effect as additional evidence for rare inhibition, not as evidence of a depleting muscle.)
- [V] Counter-evidence: van der Weiden, Benjamins, Gillebaart, Ybema & de Ridder 2020 (*Front Psychol* 11:560, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00560, PMID 32292376; abstract read): 90-day longitudinal field study of habit formation for self-chosen behaviours — habit strength "increased substantially over the course of three months, especially for participants who consistently performed the desired behavior"; but "contrary to our expectations ... self-control capacity did not seem to affect the habit formation process" (N not in the abstract). The mechanism "high-SC people form habits faster" is NOT confirmed here.

### 14-C · Situation selection and modification — the taxonomy and what has been tested
- [V] ★ Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(1):35–55, doi:10.1177/1745691615623247, PMID 26817725; abstract read): using the process model of self-control, strategies can be organised along the timeline of a developing tempting impulse; situational strategies ("choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones" — a seat far from the drinks, no dessert cart, the library without a phone) "can nip a tempting impulse in the bud" and may be "especially effective"; "Ironically, we may underappreciate situational self-control for the same reason it is so effective — ... we are often able to minimize the in-the-moment experience of intrapsychic struggle". A conceptual review (it proposes, it does not measure effectiveness).
- [~] Duckworth, Milkman & Laibson 2018 (*Psychol Sci Public Interest* 19(3):102–129, doi:10.1177/1529100618821893, PMID 30760176; PubMed abstract read; SAGE full text 403 — the paper's own table of evidence was NOT readable; the APS press release was read instead): strategies sorted along two dimensions — situational vs cognitive targets, self-deployed vs other-deployed — giving four types: self-initiated situational changes (e.g. apps limiting phone use, keeping junk food out of the house), self-initiated changes in thinking (e.g. if-then plans), other-initiated changes in thinking (e.g. social-comparison energy reports), other-initiated situational constraints (incentives, taxes, defaults). The release does not rank strategies. Caveat quoted from the release: many studies tested strategies "with small groups over short periods" and it is unclear if they stay effective "if implemented at a broader scale"; "There is an urgent need for a cumulative and applied science of self-control." Do not attribute per-strategy evidence statements to this review (not read).
- [V] Restraint bias 抑制偏差, Nordgren, van Harreveld & van der Pligt 2009 (*Psychol Sci* 20(12):1523–1528, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02468.x, PMID 19883487; abstract read): four studies — people overestimate their capacity to control impulses; inflated impulse-control beliefs led them to "overexpose themselves to temptation", promoting impulsive behaviour; in Study 4, recovering smokers with more inflated beliefs exposed themselves to more tempting situations and had higher relapse rates 4 months later (N not in the abstract). - [V] Perfectionism contrast: in Hofmann 2012 perfectionism went the opposite way to trait self-control — stronger desires, more conflict, more resistance — so "tries hard to resist" and "is high in self-control" are different people.

### 14-E · Mischel's strategy findings (the part of the marshmallow work that is NOT about prediction)
- [~] Mischel & Ebbesen 1970 ("Attention in delay of gratification", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 16(2):329–337, doi:10.1037/h0029815; no abstract on PubMed; read only as the Semantic Scholar summary of the abstract, hence [~]): preschool children's attention to the rewards they were waiting for was manipulated; "voluntary waiting time was substantially increased when subjects could not attend to rewards during the waiting period" — i.e. having the treat in view made waiting HARDER. ### 14-F · Trait self-control and life outcomes — what is correlational
- [V] Tangney, Baumeister & Boone 2004 (*J Pers* 72(2):271–324, doi:10.1111/j.0022-3506.2004.00263.x, PMID 15016066; abstract read; no N in the abstract): the Self-Control Scale (and brief version) showed good internal consistency and retest reliability; higher scores correlated with higher GPA, better adjustment (fewer reports of psychopathology, higher self-esteem), less binge eating and alcohol abuse, better relationships, secure attachment and more optimal emotional responses; tests for curvilinearity found no drawbacks of "overcontrol"; effects remained after controlling for social desirability. Self-report scale, cross-sectional/self-report outcomes.
- SEE FACTS_T3b STUDY "Childhood self-control predicts adult outcomes" (already graded B): Moffitt et al. 2011 (Dunedin, 1,000 children followed to 32; physical health, substance dependence, finances, crime; ~500 sibling pairs: the sibling with lower self-control did worse) and de Ridder et al. 2012 (102 studies, N = 32,648: small-to-medium effect that varies by life domain). Point only.
- [V] Duckworth, Taxer, Eskreis-Winkler, Galla & Gross 2019 (*Annu Rev Psychol* 70:373–399, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010418-103230; abstract via Crossref): review of self-control and academic achievement; "nearly all students experience conflict between academic goals that they value in the long run and nonacademic goals that they find more gratifying in the moment"; longitudinal evidence relates self-control to attainment, grades and test scores; the process model implies "opportunities for students to deliberately strengthen impulses that are congruent with, and dampen impulses that are incongruent with, academic goals". Review, effect sizes not in the abstract.
- [V] Saunders, Milyavskaya & Inzlicht 2022 (*Nat Commun* 13, doi:10.1038/s41467-022-30786-7; abstract via Crossref): N = 201 followed 1, 3 and 6 months: "self-reported trait self-control predicts goal attainment months later", while event-related-potential measures of performance monitoring and reward reactivity showed a null relationship with attainment ("not meaningfully associated with everyday goal attainment").
- NOTE (no new STUDY block): the Moffitt/de Ridder evidence is graded in FACTS_T3b; the grade for the claim "trait self-control predicts outcomes through mechanisms we can name" is carried by the 14-A / 14-B STUDY blocks here.

### 14-G · Ego depletion, glucose, willpower beliefs, training — post-2021 status and what is added
- SEE FACTS_T1 STUDY "Ego-depletion RRR" and FACTS_T6b STUDY "Willpower depletes like a fuel tank" (both D): Hagger 2016 (23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15]); Vohs 2021 (36 labs, N = 3,531, preregistered d = 0.06, Bayes 4:1 for the null; exploratory full sample d = 0.08, Bayes about even; hint that depletion was larger in people reporting more fatigue; no moderation by trait self-control, willpower beliefs or action orientation); Friese 2019 ("inconclusive"); Inzlicht & Schmeichel 2012 (process model). Point only.
- [~] Post-2021 developments (searched Europe PMC/PubMed titles 2021–2026 for ego depletion/strength model/registered reports as of October 2026): I found NO new large preregistered multisite test or meta-analysis of the sequential-task effect after Vohs 2021 (the 2022–2026 "ego depletion" titles are applied/correlational studies in other fields); Dang 2018 (*Psychol Res* 82(4):645–651, doi:10.1007/s00426-017-0862-x, PMID 28391367; abstract read) is an earlier updated meta-analysis that argued the PET-PEESE "zero" conclusion was premature and that depleting-task type matters (emotion video most effective, attention video ineffective) — pre-Hagger/Vohs; Schrader et al. 2025 scoping review (*Pers Individ Dif* 241:113185, doi:10.1016/j.paid.2025.113185, PMID 40521177; abstract read; 23 studies from 8,116 records of repeated naturalistic assessments): state self-control "fluctuated over time" and tracked outcomes — descriptive, not a test of depletion.
- [V] ★ "Willpower runs on glucose" is not supported: Gailliot et al. 2007 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 92(2):325–336, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.92.2.325, PMID 17279852; abstract read) reported that self-control acts lowered blood glucose and a glucose drink removed the impairment; Dang 2016 (*Appetite* 107:222–230, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2016.07.021, PMID 27492453; abstract read) meta-analysed the three corollaries: "clear and consistent evidence against the glucose view ... none of the three corollaries was supported"; glucose RINSING had a significant effect "with alarming signs of publication bias". Molden et al. 2012 (*Psychol Sci* 23(10):1137–1144, doi:10.1177/0956797612439069, PMID 22972907; abstract read): four experiments — exerting self-control did not increase carbohydrate metabolism (precise glucose measures), rinsing the mouth with but not ingesting carbohydrate bolstered self-control, and rinsing did not raise blood glucose: a motivational, not metabolic, effect. Lange & Eggert 2014 (*Appetite* 75:54–63, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2013.12.020, PMID 24389240; abstract read): N = 70 and N = 115: glucose drinks or rinses did not counteract ego depletion.
- [V] Willpower beliefs 意志力信念: Job, Dweck & Walton 2010 (*Psychol Sci* 21(11):1686–1693, doi:10.1177/0956797610384745, PMID 20876879; abstract read; N not in the abstract): four studies — people who viewed willpower as NOT limited "did not show diminished self-control after a depleting experience" (Study 1), replicated by manipulating lay theories (Study 2), and in a longitudinal field study (Study 4) theories about willpower predicted change in eating, procrastination and goal striving in demanding circumstances. Job, Walton, Bernecker & Dweck 2015 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 108(4):637–647, doi:10.1037/pspp0000014, PMID 25844577; abstract read): longitudinal study of students — a nonlimited theory predicted better self-regulation (time management, less procrastination, unhealthy eating, impulsive spending) under high demands and, among those taking a heavy course load, higher grades mediated by less procrastination. Miller, Walton, Dweck, Job & Trzesniewski 2012 (*PLoS One* 7(6):e38680, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0038680, PMID 22745675; abstract read): participants led to view willpower as non-limited showed greater SUSTAINED learning on a strenuous mental task. Sun, Cortina, Miller & Ning 2019 (*Front Psychol* 10:988, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00988, PMID 31133929; abstract read): Chinese students believed less in depletion of mental resources than US students but MORE in depletion of resistance to temptation — willpower theories are not a single culture-free construct. COUNTER: Vohs et al. 2021 (36 labs, N = 3,531) found no moderation by willpower beliefs (FACTS_T6b); the original 2010 moderation was of an effect (depletion) that did not hold up.
- [V] ★ Self-control training 自控训练: Friese, Frankenbach, Job & Loschelder 2017 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 12(6):1077–1099, doi:10.1177/1745691617697076, PMID 28846503; abstract read): meta-analysis, 33 studies, 158 effect sizes: g = 0.30 [0.17, 0.42]; larger for self-control "stamina" than "strength", with inactive than active controls, in males, and "when proponents of the strength model ... were (co)authors"; bias-corrected estimates g = .13 to .24; "There is not enough evidence to conclude that the repeated control of dominant responses is the critical element driving training effects." READING: the "muscle you can train" claim has small, bias-prone support.

### 14-H · What "self-control" measures are measuring (measurement boundary)
- [V] ★ Duckworth & Kern 2011 (*J Res Pers* 45(3):259–268, doi:10.1016/j.jrp.2011.02.004; abstract via Semantic Scholar; Crossref has none): meta-analysis of convergent validity of self-control measures — executive function tasks, delay of gratification, self- and informant-report questionnaires; 282 samples, N = 33,564; overall moderate convergence, r(random) = .27 [.24, .30], r(fixed) = .34 [.33, .35]; strongest for informant-report questionnaires, weakest for executive-function tasks; "self-control is a coherent but multidimensional construct".
- [V] Saunders, Milyavskaya, Etz, Randles & Inzlicht 2018 (*Collabra Psychol* 4(1):, doi:10.1525/collabra.134; abstract via Crossref): five data sets, overall N = 2,641: Bayesian analyses suggest "little-to-no relationship" between the Self-Control Scale (self-reported trait self-control) and Stroop/Flanker performance; theoretical conclusions from one measure cannot be generalised to the other. Wennerhold & Friese 2020 (*Collabra Psychol* 6(1), doi:10.1525/collabra.276; abstract via Crossref): explain the non-correspondence as typical vs maximum performance, aggregates vs single trials, and general vs domain-specific inhibition — "implausible to hypothesize more than a low correlation".
- [V] Dang, King & Inzlicht 2020 ("Why are self-report and behavioral measures weakly correlated?", *Trends Cogn Sci*) was found only as a PsyArXiv record (doi:10.31234/osf.io/v796c) — not cited beyond the title.

### Myths and corrections
- [✗] "High-self-control people resist temptation harder." — Hofmann 2012 (N = 205; 7,827 desires): trait self-control went with LESS resistance once conflict was controlled (probability ≈ .35 vs .42 at ±1 SD), weaker desires (3.88 vs 4.20 on 0–7), less conflict (B −0.16), and it moderated neither conflict→resistance nor resistance→enactment; the authors: the data favour avoiding temptation "in the first place" over resisting it. Milyavskaya & Inzlicht 2017 (N = 159): effortful self-control "consistently unrelated" to semester goal progress (BF01 ≈ 24). Grade B.
- [✗] "…because they have fewer desires." — also overstated: the absolute frequency of desires was not related to trait self-control (|r| ≈ .04, all p > .15, Hofmann 2012); what differed was strength, conflict and how problematic the desires were (r = −.20 with independent raters' problem index). Say "weaker, less conflicting, less problematic desires", never "fewer temptations" without the qualifier.
- [✗] "People spend half their waking hours desiring something." — the paper's figure is 49.9% of RESPONDED beeper moments (7 random signals per day, 14-hour window) with a current desire, in 205 mostly young German adults (73% students); it is not a time-budget measurement.
- [✗] "Irresistible urges drive behaviour." — only 6.3% of desires were rated "irresistible"; even at maximal strength 7, resisted desires were enacted 25.8% of the time (10.8% at strength 1); and without resistance, strength did not predict enactment (Hofmann 2012). "Truly irresistible motivations are quite uncommon."
- [✗] "Willpower is a muscle that tires (ego depletion) and runs on glucose." — SEE FACTS_T1/T6b (D): d = 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15] (23 labs, N = 2,141) and d = 0.06 (36 labs, N = 3,531); glucose: none of three corollaries supported (Dang 2016), motivational not metabolic (Molden 2012), null replication (Lange & Eggert 2014). Keep: effort feels tiring and shifts motivation (process model; exploratory fatigue hint in Vohs 2021).
- [✗] "Believing willpower is unlimited protects you from depletion." — Job 2010 reported it for an effect (depletion) that failed large preregistered replication; Vohs 2021 (N = 3,531) found no moderation by willpower beliefs; beliefs differ by culture (Sun 2019). Grade C.
- [✗] "Train self-control like a muscle and it generalises." — Friese 2017: g = 0.30 [0.17, 0.42] across 33 studies but bias-corrected g = .13–.24, larger when model proponents authored the study; "not enough evidence" that repeated overriding is the active ingredient. Grade C.
- [✗] "Situational strategies clearly beat willpower in everyday life." — theory (Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016) and lab/field availability studies (Hollands 2019, food, low certainty) point that way, but the one ESM test found many strategies "including inhibition ... similarly effective" with the best results from using several (Milyavskaya 2021). Grade C.
- [✗] "A self-control questionnaire and a Stroop task measure the same thing." — r ≈ .27 (random) to .34 (fixed) across 282 samples, weakest for executive-function tasks (Duckworth & Kern 2011); Bayesian null in five data sets, N = 2,641 (Saunders 2018). Grade A.
- [✗] "Self-imposed deadlines/precommitment is proven by Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002)." — RETRACTED 2 September 2026 (the notice cites the failed replication and Data Colada's data analyses; see 14-D and the sweep); an independent replication of its Study 2 found "negligible" effects (Hyndman & Bisin 2026). Use the field commitment-contract trials (Giné 2010; Halpern 2015; FACTS_T6b) with their limits (11–14% take-up).
- [✗] "Marshmallow waiting at age 4 determines success; 21 days makes a habit." — SEE FACTS_T3b (C/D) and FACTS_T6b/T3b (Lally 2010: median 66 days, individuals 18–254).
- Better than its reputation: (1) resistance WORKS — enactment 70% → 17% when people try to resist (Hofmann 2012); willpower is a lever, not a myth. (2) The intention–behaviour gap (Webb & Sheeran 2006: intention d = 0.66 → behaviour d = 0.36; FACTS_T6b, grade A) is the cleanest reason that wanting is not enough. (3) Trait self-control (self-report) does predict goal attainment months later (Saunders 2022, N = 201) and a gradient of outcomes (Moffitt 2011; FACTS_T3b). (4) If-then plans (d ≈ .27–.66; FACTS_T6b) are a real, modest tool.

### Widget constants (a toy: temptation arrivals × strength vs effortful resistance vs exposure reduction)
- [V] Published rates to anchor the toy (Hofmann et al. 2012, N = 205, Würzburg, 7 days × 7 beeps; self-report): a current desire at 49.9% of responded beeps; mean desire strength 4.08 on a 0–7 scale (6.3% rated 7 "irresistible"; SD not read); median duration 16–20 min; conflict with a goal (0–4): 53.2% none, 14.7% mild, 12.4% somewhat, 10.9% quite, 8.8% high (mean 1.08); resisted 42% of desires (≈ 20% of all beeps involve resisting); enacted 48% overall; enactment 69.6% if not resisted vs 17.4% if resisted; among resisted desires enactment rose with strength from 10.8% (strength 1) to 25.8% (strength 7); among unresisted desires strength did not change enactment; conflict and strength are unrelated (r ≈ 0); resistance rises with conflict (log-odds B = 0.49 per conflict point, base model).
- [V] Trait self-control shifts the INPUTS, not the resisting skill (Hofmann 2012): per SD of trait self-control (SD 0.59): strength −0.32 on 0–7 (3.88 vs 4.20 for ±1 SD), conflict about −0.19 on 0–4 (DERIVED from B = −0.16; printed labels transposed), resistance probability .35 vs .42; trait self-control did not change enactment given resistance (no moderation); desire frequency unchanged (|r| ≈ .04).
- [V] Milyavskaya & Inzlicht 2017 (N = 159; observations n = 2,323): observation-level means — temptation 1.27 (SD 1.28; defined as strength × conflict ÷ 7), self-reported resistance 0.36 (SD 2.16, −3…+3 item), felt depletion 3.10 (SD 1.78, 0–6); goal-level progress mean 4.26 (SD 1.53, 1–7). ### Boundary (feeds 「这个模型的边界」)
- Self-control failures exist and matter: 17.4% of resisted desires were still enacted (≈ 3% of all beeps); trait self-control (self-reported) still predicts goal attainment months later (Saunders 2022) and, in cohorts, a gradient of health, wealth and crime outcomes (Moffitt 2011, FACTS_T3b) — this lesson changes the account of HOW, not WHETHER.
- Willpower is not nothing: resistance cut enactment from about 70% to about 17% (Hofmann 2012) and effortful control is real in tasks (Stroop-type) — it is not a depleting muscle in the strong form (D) and is probably a weak-to-modest lever, with a small, bias-prone effect of training (g = 0.30; .13–.24 corrected).
- The strongest evidence is correlational/ESM self-report in young adults and students (Germany, Canada, USA, Netherlands); the "less temptation, not more resisting" result is replicated in direction across labs but not tested by randomisation, and a one-item self-report of resisting cannot rule out that high-SC people simply notice less conflict (partly addressed by Hofmann's independent raters, r = −.20).
- Situation design is not equally available to everyone — an inference; no source opened measures differences in access to situation control, and the only ESM comparison found situational strategies no better than willpower (Milyavskaya 2021).
- Habit evidence in humans is limited and mostly self-reported (FACTS_T3b/T6b: Lally 2010, Buyalskaya 2023); one longitudinal test found self-control did not predict habit formation speed (van der Weiden 2020).
- "Self-control" is several things: scale vs inhibition-task correlations ≈ .27–.34 (Duckworth & Kern 2011) and a Bayesian null between the Self-Control Scale and Stroop/Flanker (Saunders 2018); results depend on which measure was used.
- Addiction and compulsion are not covered (lesson 13); desire for tobacco and alcohol was among the WEAKEST on average in the ESM sample (Hofmann, Vohs & Baumeister 2012) — a caution against generalising the "ordinary desires" picture to dependence.
