## 07 · 回归均值 Regression to the mean

### Core evidence
- SEE FACTS_T6a §00-C (Galton 1886 [V]; Barnett, van der Pols & Dobson 2005 [def]; DERIVED rule E[z₂|z₁] = r·z₁, "share of the gap that closes by chance = 1 − r"; worked 10%/25%/5% truncated-normal examples). Not repeated here.
- SEE FACTS_T6a STUDY "Placebo effects across conditions" (already graded): the classic "30–35% placebo response" is largely RTM + natural course; Beecher [✗] in §00-D.
- SEE FACTS_T1 STUDY "Hot-hand fallacy" (Miller & Sanjurjo 2018): that is a *different* selection bias (see Myths below).
- [def] ★ 回归均值 in one line: when a group is picked because it scored extremely on a noisy first measurement, the second measurement of the same group is on average closer to the mean, with no cause at work. The larger the noise relative to the stable part (the lower the test–retest correlation r), the larger the artefact. — Morton & Torgerson 2003 (BMJ 326:1083–1084, doi:10.1136/bmj.326.7398.1083, PMID 12750214; full text read): "occurs whenever a non-random sample is selected from a population and two imperfectly correlated variables are measured, such as two consecutive blood pressure measurements. The less correlated the two variables, the larger the effect…; the more extreme the value… the more room there is to regress" (paraphrase; they add that it "occurs whenever a group is selected with extreme values for one variable and another variable is then measured").

- STUDY ★ The flight-instructor story (Kahneman) — original: told by Kahneman in his Nobel biographical statement (2002): while he and a colleague (Ozer Schild) designed a training course for instructors in the flight school of the (Israeli) Air Force, he tried to teach that praise works better than punishment for skill learning; "one of the most seasoned instructors" objected, in Kahneman's quotation: "On many occasions I have praised flight cadets for clean execution of some aerobatic maneuver, and in general when they try it again, they do worse. On the other hand, I have often screamed at cadets for bad execution, and in general they do better the next time." Kahneman's reading: because people reward good performance and punish bad performance, and because of regression to the mean, "we are statistically punished for rewarding others and rewarded for punishing them". He says he then ran a demonstration in which each participant tossed two coins at a target behind his own back without feedback: those who did best the first time mostly deteriorated on the second try, and vice versa.
  - Later evidence: the passage is an anecdote told 40 years later: no number of instructors, cadets or manoeuvres is given, no data were collected on the cadets; Tversky & Kahneman 1974 (*Science* 185:1124–1131) and Morton & Torgerson 2003 retell it, and the BMJ retelling hardens "in general" into "invariably" (see [✗] below). The real mechanism it illustrates (selection on an extreme first score) is not in doubt (see the controlled studies below). — Kahneman, Nobel biographical statement 2002, https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/biographical/ (page opened and read, passage quoted from the page)
  - Tversky & Kahneman 1974, doi:10.1126/science.185.4157.1124, PMID 17835457 (abstract only; the flight-training paragraph was NOT opened, so its wording is known only second-hand — treat the Nobel text as the primary source)
  GRADE C: as evidence that "punishment beats praise" the story is worthless (an anecdote, no control group, and the instructors' inference is exactly the error); as an illustration of RTM it is excellent. Teach it as a story, not as a study.
- STUDY ★ Intuitive predictions are not regressive (insensitivity to predictability) — original: Kahneman & Tversky 1973, "On the psychology of prediction", *Psychol Rev* 80(4):237–251, doi:10.1037/h0034747: people predicting a numeric outcome (e.g. a student's grade-point average) from a description or score give predictions that track how impressive the evidence is and ignore how predictive it is — the 1972 report version's abstract (Oregon Research Institute Bulletin 12(4), as quoted by a search summary; not opened as a document) says numerical predictions are "nonregressive" with respect to an evaluation of the impression on which they are based
  - the studies used percentile GPA, percentile on a concentration test and percentile on a sense-of-humour test as cues (the design as described by a later study, Biolsi 1993; not opened).
  - Later evidence: Kahneman & Tversky 1982, "On the study of statistical intuitions", *Cognition* 11(2):123–141 (full text read, UCSD copy): review of the literature on "radical, or insufficiently regressive" prediction (Jennings, Amabile & Ross 1982 is the review they cite); they propose a "negative analysis" of why the correct rule is counter-intuitive: (i) a prediction rule that "guarantees error" on every pair seems odd, (ii) regression is asymmetric (predicting B from A and A from B use different equations) although symmetry seems natural, (iii) "any systematic effect must have a cause" — the drop after a high first score has no cause, so people invent one (e.g. "overconfidence following an initial success", "intense striving after an initial failure"). I did not open the 1973 article and have no N or effect size from it
  - the sizes of the insensitivity are therefore not given. — Kahneman & Tversky 1973, doi:10.1037/h0034747 (bibliographic record only); Kahneman & Tversky 1982, doi:10.1016/0010-0277(82)90022-1, https://pages.ucsd.edu/~mckenzie/Kahneman&Tversky1982Cognition.pdf (OCR text read)
  GRADE B: the direction ("people do not regress their predictions enough, and attribute regression to causes") is a long-standing and widely replicated result, but I opened no replication with sizes; the 1973 numbers are not verified here.
- [V] ★ Regression runs both ways in time — the "backwards" regression is a statistical fact, not a causal story: Kahneman & Tversky 1982 (above) note that the regression of the first performance on the second is also surprising "because it cannot be given a simple causal explanation"; Bland (author's page, below): midparent height 70 in → children's mean 69.6 in; but children of height 70 in → midparents' mean 67.9 in (Galton data, both distributions with mean 68.2 in). Example in data: in six UK safety-camera partnerships the sites picked for cameras had *more* collisions in the baseline years than in the three years *before* the baseline (see Allsop below: 4,574 → 4,984 collisions, 757 → 910 serious/fatal collisions) — the high baseline is the one that was selected on. — Kahneman & Tversky 1982; Bland, M. "Regression towards the mean" (talk, Univ. York, https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~mb55/talks/regmean.htm, page opened); Allsop 2010 Table 5 (below)

#### Health care
- STUDY ★ Osteoporosis monitoring: those who "lose bone" in year 1 mostly gain in year 2 on the SAME treatment — original: Cummings, Palermo, Browner, Marcus, Wallace, Pearson et al. (Fracture Intervention Trial Research Group) 2000, *JAMA* 283(10):1318–1321, doi:10.1001/jama.283.10.1318, PMID 10714731 (abstract read): two randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (FIT, alendronate 5 mg/d, n = 2,634 who completed 2 years of BMD monitoring and adhered; MORE, raloxifene 60/120 mg/d, n = 3,954)
  - among women on alendronate whose hip BMD fell by more than 4% in year 1, 83% (95% CI 82–84%) gained hip BMD in year 2 (mean +4.7%); among those who "seemed to gain at least 8%" in year 1, the mean change next year was −1% (95% CI 0.1–1.9% loss); similar with raloxifene; conclusion: effective treatments should not be changed because of a year-1 BMD loss. Morton & Torgerson 2003 present the same trial data in a figure (women grouped by 12-month change; 24-month values).
  - Later evidence: the mechanism is textbook statistics (Barnett 2005, FACTS_T6a); I did not search for 2020s replications of this specific figure. — Cummings et al. 2000; Morton & Torgerson 2003
  GRADE A: two large randomised double-blind trials in the same direction, a mechanism that is pure arithmetic, and a dose-response by year-1 change (the more extreme year-1 change, the larger the opposite year-2 change).
- STUDY ★ Hypertension: the "placebo" arm falls too, and falls more the higher you start — original: Australian Therapeutic Trial in Mild Hypertension (Management Committee) 1980, *Lancet* 1(8181):1261–1267, PMID 6104081 (PubMed record has no abstract; figures below are as reproduced on Martin Bland's page, opened): entry criteria average diastolic BP (DBP) over four readings at two visits between 95 and 110 mmHg and systolic < 200 mmHg
  - placebo group: mean screening DBP 100.4 mmHg → 93.9 mmHg on trial, a fall of 6.6 mmHg with no active drug; active group 100.5 → 88.3, a fall of 12.2 mmHg; within the placebo group the fall by screening DBP band was 5.0 (95–99), 7.4 (100–104), 9.2 mmHg (105–109) — the sicker the entrant, the larger the "improvement". DERIVED: the active-minus-placebo difference is 12.2 − 6.6 = 5.6 mmHg, so ≈ 54% of the active group's fall was also seen without the drug.
  - Later evidence: the same design lesson underlies every randomised trial (control arm shows the regression); Bland adds the two-pulse example (185 students, r = 0.675 between two observers' pulse counts; students with first pulse > 90 b/min, mean 100.2, were predicted by r to average 91.2 on re-measurement, observed 90.4) as a clean demonstration with no treatment at all. — Management Committee 1980 via Bland (page opened); Davis 1976, *Am J Epidemiol* 104(5):493–498, doi:10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a112321, PMID 984023 (abstract read: regression "can have a sizeable effect and may lead to erroneous conclusions concerning treatment effects")
  GRADE A: a randomised design makes the regression visible in the control arm; the numbers are old (1980) and read via Bland's page, not from the Lancet abstract.
- [V] Check of the pulse arithmetic (DERIVED, my calculation): predicted second mean = mean + r·(first-group mean − mean) = 72.6 + 0.675 × (100.2 − 72.6) = 72.6 + 18.6 = 91.2 b/min; Bland reports 91.2 predicted and 90.4 observed — so r alone predicts almost the whole drop of 9.8 b/min. — Bland (page opened)
- [V] Bland on the "difference from baseline" trap: in a trial, using "after minus baseline" as the outcome does not correct for baseline imbalance — "any imbalance will be reversed, due to regression to the mean" (subjects with low baselines tend to show high change); use analysis of covariance with the baseline as covariate (Vickers & Altman 2001, *BMJ* 323(7321):1123–1124, doi:10.1136/bmj.323.7321.1123, PMID 11701584, abstract record only: no abstract text). Bland also names duplicate baseline measurements taken on a different occasion from the one used to select subjects. — Bland page (opened); Vickers & Altman 2001 (record)
- [V] Morton & Torgerson 2003 (BMJ, full text read) list where the artefact bites: diagnostic tests (a high first reading is likely partly chance, so "treatment worked" is misread; the remedy is to average serial measurements), new treatments tried on the sickest patients, placebo effects in trials that recruit patients with extreme symptom scores ("patients with the worst clinical scores have the biggest placebo effect" is a sign of RTM), public-health responses to sudden spikes (crash blackspots; meningitis), league tables of hospitals (poor hospitals "will suddenly climb", top hospitals "fall"), clinical audit after a bad run. Bone-density example: ">80%" of women who lost bone in year 1 gained in year 2 (Cummings 2000, above).
- [✗] "The 75–90% fall in meningitis C cases after the vaccination campaign was mostly regression to the mean" — this is the BMJ article's own assertion (headline reduction "is an overestimate as most of the reduction would have been due to regression to the mean"), with no analysis behind it in the piece; a vaccine's effect is shown by cohort/efficacy studies, not by this argument. I did not open an independent evaluation, so the correct statement is: it is an unsupported claim in a short opinion piece, not a finding; do not teach it. — Morton & Torgerson 2003 (page 1084, full text read)
- [✗] "The placebo effect is simply regression to the mean" (Morton & Torgerson 2003 on HRT trials) — overstated: regression and natural course explain a large part of improvement in the placebo arm, but the Cochrane review of trials with both placebo and no-treatment arms found small but real placebo effects on subjective outcomes (pain SMD −0.28) — SEE FACTS_T6a STUDY "Placebo effects across conditions" (graded). The correct version is: the *placebo arm's change* is mostly not a placebo effect; the placebo *effect* is the placebo-vs-no-treatment difference.

#### Road safety: the same data, two analyses
- STUDY ★ Speed cameras: naive vs regression-corrected before–after (Allsop 2010, evaluating the UK four-year national evaluation) — original: the four-year evaluation of the UK safety-camera programme covered > 4,000 camera sites (≈ 4,400 sites for personal-injury collisions, ≈ 4,200 for killed-or-seriously-injured, KSI), comparing numbers in the year ending March 2004 with what baseline rates would have produced "after allowing for trend and seasonal variation": Allsop's Table 2 (the unadjusted-for-RTM percentage reductions): fixed urban sites −22.4% personal-injury collisions (PIC) and −46.8% KSI
  - fixed rural −33.2% and −62.4%; mobile urban −22.4% and −34.9%; mobile rural −15.5% and −33.8%. Selection of sites required recent collision counts, with the selection period overlapping the baseline, so "the selected sites [tend] to have had, by chance, numbers… above average"; Allsop states that the four-year report itself "recognises that the resulting estimates will in all probability have exaggerated the effect of cameras because of regression to the mean". Corrected figures (Allsop Table 4, Empirical-Bayes RTM allowance from an unrepresentative subsample of urban sites): fixed urban PIC 19.9% / KSI 29.8%
  - mobile urban PIC 16.5% / KSI 9.7%. A second, independent hint of the size of the artefact (Table 5, six partnerships): collisions at the camera sites were 4,574 in the three years *before* the baseline vs 4,984 in the baseline years (+9%), and serious/fatal collisions 757 vs 910 (+20%), although the national KSI/FSC count was falling by about 3.5% a year; Allsop stresses these data "cannot be regarded as representative", and applying them gives Table 6: fixed urban 15.7% / 29.1%, fixed rural 27.4% / 49.9%, mobile urban 15.7% / 13.2%, mobile rural 8.2% / 11.7%. Allsop's overall judgement (Table 7: reductions "additional to any effect of regression to the mean"): PIC fixed urban 20–25%, fixed rural 20–30%, mobile urban 15–20%, mobile rural 10–15%
  - KSI fixed urban 35–40%, fixed rural 30–50%, mobile 15–30% (both). His words on the difficulty: "Allowance for RTM is important but hard to estimate", and he quotes an analyst: "Give me an appropriate mean, and I will gladly allow for regression to it!". DERIVED contrast: for fixed rural KSI the naive −62.4% falls to −49.9% (Table 6) and to a judged 30–50% (Table 7); for mobile urban KSI the naive −34.9% falls to −9.7%/−13.2% (Tables 4/6) and to a judged 15–30%.
  - Later evidence: (a) Graham, Naik, McCoy & Li 2019, *PLoS ONE* 14(9):e0221267, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0221267, PMID 31525209 (full text read): England, sites matched with untreated road links; the naive model (no adjustment for the rule that cameras go where collisions were high) gave an average treatment effect of −33.7% (95% credible interval −42.1 to −13.6) on the full sample and −17.6% on the matched sample, while the three causal models (outcome regression, inverse-propensity weighting, doubly robust) gave −14.4% to −15.5% (doubly robust −14.4%, interval −21.8 to −7.4) — "speed cameras do cause a significant reduction… by as much as 15% on average for treated sites"
  - the authors name RTM as "a well known manifestation of confounding" by site selection but the paper does not isolate RTM alone; (b) Mountain, Hirst & Maher 2005, *Accid Anal Prev* 37(4):742–754, doi:10.1016/j.aap.2005.03.017, PMID 15885612 (abstract read): controls for general trend, regression-to-mean and migration; the average fall in personal-injury accidents attributable to safety cameras on 30 mph roads was 22% (44% for vertical-deflection engineering schemes such as humps; 29% other engineering)
  - (c) Wilson, Willis, Hendrikz, Le Brocque & Bellamy 2010, Cochrane review (*Cochrane Database Syst Rev* CD004607, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD004607.pub4, PMID 21069682; abstract read): 35 studies met inclusion criteria; compared with controls, relative reduction in average speed 1–15% and in the proportion speeding 14–65%; near camera sites the pre/post reductions ranged 8–49% for all crashes and 11–44% for fatal and serious injury crashes
  - with controls, relative improvement in injury-crash proportions 8–50%; no meta-analysis possible (heterogeneity); conclusion: cameras are "a worthwhile intervention", but "an overall magnitude of this effect is currently not deducible". — Allsop 2010, RAC Foundation, *The Effectiveness of Speed Cameras: A Review of Evidence* (PDF opened and read, https://www.racfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/efficacy_of_speed_cameras_allsop_181110.pdf)
  - Graham et al. 2019; Mountain et al. 2005; Wilson et al. 2010
  GRADE B: the direction (cameras reduce speeds and collisions at the site) is consistent across designs; the size is the contested part and every serious analysis agrees it is smaller than the naive before–after number (naive −33.7% vs ≈ −15% in Graham; −22/−47% vs 16–20/29–40% in Allsop). The RTM share cannot be pinned to one number: Allsop calls any allowance "important but hard to estimate", and the 2019 analysis folds RTM into "confounding by site selection".
- [V] The 2010 RAC Foundation exec summary states the policy-level result of such corrections: after allowing for trend, RTM and diversion, cameras at > 4,000 sites "prevented some 3,600 personal injury collisions… saving around 1,000 people from being killed or seriously injured" in the year ending March 2004 (ranges in the report's table: 3,050–4,200 PIC and 800–1,300 KSI). — Allsop 2010 (executive summary, opened)
- [~] The exact RTM share of the naive reduction is NOT established: the figures a search summary attributed to a later slide deck (RTM "36% at fixed sites and 43% at mobile sites") were not found in the report I opened; do not use them (COULD NOT VERIFY).
- [def] Why the camera case is a clean teaching example: sites are chosen *because* of recent bad counts (the selection is on an extreme first measurement), the counts are small integers with large chance variation, and the "second measurement" is the next year. Hauer (1997, *Observational Before–After Studies in Road Safety*, Pergamon) is the standard text on correcting this (Empirical Bayes), cited by Allsop; I did not open the book.

#### Mental health: what changes with no treatment
- STUDY Depressive symptoms fall on a wait-list — original: Posternak & Miller 2001, *J Affect Disord* 66(2–3):139–146, doi:10.1016/s0165-0327(00)00304-9, PMID 11578666 (abstract read): meta-analysis of psychotherapy studies that randomised adult outpatients with major depressive disorder to a wait-list: 19 studies, 221 subjects; mean decrease in Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression scores over 2–20 weeks 11.9%, in Beck Depression Inventory scores 15.7%
  - in a subsample with individual outcomes 15 of 76 (19.7%) improved enough to count as a "response" in antidepressant trials; conclusion: roughly 10–15% average decrease without treatment.
  - Later evidence: Whiteford et al. 2013, *Psychol Med* 43(8):1569–1585, doi:10.1017/S0033291712001717, PMID 22883473 (abstract read; 19 studies; wait-list and observational cohorts): for adults recruited in primary care the model estimated that 23% of prevalent cases of untreated depression remit within 3 months, 32% within 6 months, 53% within 12 months; wait-listed samples remit less than primary-care samples
  - the authors warn the samples may over-represent mild-to-moderate cases; Furukawa et al. 2014, *Acta Psychiatr Scand* 130(3):181–192, doi:10.1111/acps.12275, PMID 24697518 (abstract read; network meta-analysis of 49 RCTs, 2,730 participants): the odds ratio of response for "no treatment" over wait-list was 2.9 (95% CI 1.3–5.7), i.e. a wait-list may depress improvement (a "nocebo" condition), but quality of evidence was "less than ideal" and the difference did not persist in post hoc publication-bias-adjusted analyses. — Posternak & Miller 2001; Whiteford et al. 2013; Furukawa et al. 2014
  GRADE B: all three agree that sizeable improvement happens without treatment, but this is spontaneous course PLUS regression PLUS expectation; none of the abstracts separates RTM from natural remission, so the sizes (10–15% in weeks; about half remit within a year in primary-care samples) must not be called "regression to the mean" alone. Condition-specific and not applicable to severe depression (Whiteford's own caveat).
- [~] How to say it: people usually enter trials, clinics and self-experiments at a bad moment (that is why they come), so the next measurement is expected to look better even if nothing is done; separating this from a real treatment effect needs a concurrent control group. (reasoned synthesis of Morton & Torgerson 2003, Posternak & Miller 2001, Barnett 2005 [FACTS_T6a]; RECOMMENDATION, not a finding)

#### Sports, schools and league tables: ranking by one noisy measurement
- STUDY ★ Early-season batting averages shrink toward the league mean (Efron & Morris 1977) — original: Efron & Morris, "Stein's paradox in statistics", *Scientific American* 236(5):119–127 (PDF opened and read; gwern.net copy): 18 major-league players who had each batted exactly 45 times in the 1970 season; the target was their average over the remainder of the season ("about nine times as much data")
  - the grand average of the 18 first-45 averages was .265 and the empirical-Bayes shrinking factor c = .212, so each average "will shrink about 80 percent of the distance to the grand average" — e.g. Roberto Clemente, the leading batter then, y = .400 → estimate .265 + .212 × (.400 − .265) = .294; Thurman Munson, in a slump, y = .178 → .247; accuracy against the rest-of-season averages: total squared error .077 for the raw first-45 averages vs .022 for the shrunk estimates, "3.5 times as accurate".
  - Later evidence: the example is the textbook case behind empirical-Bayes shrinkage (the same weighting Allsop's "Empirical Bayes" RTM allowance uses); I did not open a modern re-analysis. DERIVED reading (my interpretation, not a quote): c ≈ .2 means only about one-fifth of the spread among 45-at-bat averages reflects real hitting ability — the reliability of a 45-at-bat average is about .2, so "1 − r" ≈ 80% of an extreme early average is expected to evaporate. — Efron & Morris 1977, https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/bayes/1977-efron.pdf
  GRADE A: a prediction test on real data in which shrinking toward the group mean beats the raw extreme values by a factor of 3.5 in squared error; it is one season and 18 players, but the principle is statistical arithmetic and has held in every sports data set I know of (none opened).
- [~] Sophomore slump, Sports Illustrated "cover jinx", "Premier League manager of the month loses next game": Bland's talk lists these as RTM examples ("you only get on these covers if you have recently been unusually successful") without data; I found NO peer-reviewed analysis of the SI jinx. A magazine's own count (913 of 2,456 covers followed by "misfortune") surfaced in a search summary and is not peer-reviewed — do not use. Teach them as illustrations of the mechanism, with Efron–Morris as the measured case. — Bland page (opened)
- STUDY School rankings: the "most improved" and "worst" schools are largely noise (Kane & Staiger) — original: Kane & Staiger 2001 working paper (NBER w8156, PDF opened and read) → 2002, "The Promise and Pitfalls of Using Imprecise School Accountability Measures", *J Econ Perspect* 16(4):91–114, doi:10.1257/089533002320950993 (abstract read on the AEA page: scores are imprecise, so rewards and sanctions for schools at the extremes mainly hit small schools; median elementary school has 69 children per grade level): North Carolina 3rd–5th grade math and reading, 1993–1999
  - sampling variation alone accounts for roughly 15% of the variance across schools in 5th-grade math and 30% in reading for an average-size school; the non-persistent component is 8% of between-school variance in math score levels but 29% in math gains ("value added"); in Massachusetts the district with the biggest improvement in 1998–99 MCAS, Provincetown, had only 26 students tested in 10th grade
  - selecting the top 10% of schools on 5th-grade math gains in 1997 (+.453 student-level SD above the average school in 1997), the same schools were only +.115 SD above average in 1999; predicting 1998/1999 gains with the 1997 value alone gave a NEGATIVE forecast R² (worse than predicting the state average for every school); "filtered" empirical-Bayes forecasts reached R² of only .16 (math gains) and .04 (reading gains). DERIVED: the top-10% group kept .115/.453 = 25% of its lead, i.e. about 75% regressed in two years.
  - Later evidence: the working-paper numbers are from one state's 1990s data; I did not open the published JEP text or replications; the 2002 abstract confirms the same conclusion. — Kane & Staiger 2001 (https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8156/w8156.pdf) and 2002 (AEA abstract)
  GRADE B: a very clear and large case in real accountability data, from a careful empirical-Bayes decomposition; confined to one state and period in the version I read.
- STUDY League tables of hospitals/clinics: ranks move without real change (Marshall & Spiegelhalter 1998; Spiegelhalter 2005) — original: Marshall & Spiegelhalter 1998, *BMJ* 316(7146):1701–1704, doi:10.1136/bmj.316.7146.1701, PMID 9614016 (abstract read): 52 UK in-vitro-fertilisation clinics, April 1994–March 1995; substantial real differences in live-birth rates, but "great uncertainty… concerning the true ranks, particularly for the smaller clinics"
  - only one clinic could be confidently placed in the bottom quarter; many clinics had substantial rank changes between years although their rates did not change significantly.
  - Later evidence: Spiegelhalter 2005, *Stat Med* 24(8):1185–1202, doi:10.1002/sim.1970, PMID 15568194 (abstract read): funnel plots (an estimate against its precision, with control limits) are recommended to "avoid spurious ranking of institutions into 'league tables'". Morton & Torgerson 2003 apply the same logic to management: "poor hospitals… will suddenly climb the league table". — Marshall & Spiegelhalter 1998; Spiegelhalter 2005
  GRADE B: sound statistics and a sharp real example, but the clinic study is one data set from the 1990s and the funnel-plot paper is a method paper; I did not open a modern audit.

#### Designs that defend against it
- STUDY ★ Randomised control group, covariate adjustment, duplicate baselines — original: the logic in Bland (page opened): a concurrent control arm shows the same regression, so the treatment effect is the difference (Reader et al. 1980: active −12.2 vs placebo −6.6 mmHg); "do not use differences from baseline; use analysis of covariance instead" (Vickers & Altman 2001, BMJ, record only: PMID 11701584)
  - take a second baseline measurement on a different occasion from the one used to select subjects, "because the correlation between measurements on different occasions will be less than between measurements made on the same occasion"; estimators exist for an expected regression effect when the population mean/SD and r are known (Shepard & Finison 1983; Davis 1976: PMID 984023) — Bland's own check: r = .675, predicted second pulse 91.2 vs observed 90.4 b/min.
  - Later evidence: Barnett et al. 2005 (FACTS_T6a §00-C: design and analysis remedies); Goldenholz et al. 2026, *Seizure* 142:168–174, doi:10.1016/j.seizure.2026.09.002, PMID 42715842 (abstract read; simulation of epilepsy trials with a calibrated seizure-diary simulator, ≈ 220,000 simulated candidates): when the baseline period is also the eligibility window, baseline seizure counts are inflated (1.60 vs 1.20 seizures/month at a 4/month threshold) and the simulated placebo arm shows a larger apparent response (placebo median percent change 6.7% in the overlap design vs −2.1% in the separate design; the abstract does not state the sign convention, so quote the two numbers only)
  - but separating them required 5.7–8.8% (median-change endpoint) to 6.9–17.9% (responder endpoint) MORE randomised participants — "reducing RTM did not improve trial efficiency"; Fontanari & Santos 2026, *Am Nat* 208(3):314–331, doi:10.1086/741601, PMID 42623641 (abstract read): common RTM-correction methods (e.g. the Berry/Kelly–Price method) are "unreliable… leading to systematic bias and inflated error rates"
  - the Blomqvist method is unbiased but high-variance; the robust route is to compare the uncorrected slope with a null derived from measurement repeatability ("the proportion of total variance attributable to true individual differences" — the same quantity as r); Campbell 1957 (*Psychol Bull* 54(4):297–312, doi:10.1037/h0040950, PMID 13465924; record only) and Campbell & Stanley 1963 (not opened) are the standard sources for statistical regression as a threat to internal validity — [def] a before–after study with no control group cannot rule it out. — Bland page; Vickers & Altman 2001; Goldenholz et al. 2026; Fontanari & Santos 2026; Campbell 1957
  GRADE A for "a randomised concurrent control arm removes RTM from the treatment comparison"; GRADE B for statistical corrections (assumption-sensitive; the two 2026 papers are simulations and a methods critique).

### Myths and corrections
- [✗] "Regression to the mean means that things get closer to the middle over time, so everything evens out" — it is a statement about a GROUP selected for being extreme on one noisy measurement, relative to a second measurement of the same thing; Morton & Torgerson: "because regression to the mean is a group phenomenon, the improvement is not certain, and some hospitals will move in the opposite direction". The spread of the whole population does not shrink (Bland: "the whole distribution is the same for each variable"); only the selected subgroup's mean moves, and only part of the way (never past the mean).
- [✗] "It needs measurement error" — Galton's 1886 heights regressed with no measurement-error story (Bland: "a statistical, not a genetic phenomenon"); any component of the first score that does not carry over (luck, mood, sampling of students or collisions) produces it. The quantity that matters is the test–retest correlation r, and the closing share is 1 − r (DERIVED, FACTS_T6a §00-C).
- [✗] ★ Flight instructors: the BMJ retelling says trainee pilots "invariably made a subsequent poor landing" after praise (Morton & Torgerson 2003, p. 1083); Kahneman's own telling is "in general when they try it again, they do worse" (Nobel statement) — a tendency, not a law — and the whole account is an anecdote with no data (see STUDY above).
- [✗] "Before–after improvement proves the treatment/policy worked" — the speed-camera numbers: the naive −62.4% KSI (fixed rural) is judged by the report's author to be 30–50% after RTM allowance; the 2019 causal analysis halves the naive effect (−33.7% → −14.4%) yet still finds a real effect. The correct version: the naive number is an upper bound.
- [✗] "So RTM means the treatment/camera does nothing" — wrong in both directions: Allsop, Graham 2019 and the Cochrane review all find a real remaining effect; and the osteoporosis trials show the reverse mistake (stopping an effective drug because a year-1 reading dropped; 83% gained in year 2). RTM tells you what to compare against, not what the answer is.
- [✗] "The 75–90% fall in meningitis C cases after vaccination was mostly RTM" and "the placebo effect is simply RTM" — both are overreach in Morton & Torgerson 2003 (see health-care bullets); do not repeat them.
- [✗] "A 'worst week' improvement after a self-experiment is evidence the change worked" — people start experiments when they feel worst; with weekly mood r = .6 about 40% of the gap closes by chance alone (DERIVED, FACTS_T6a §00-C); the r is a parameter, not a measured constant (see Widget constants).
- Better than its reputation: the Allsop analysis is a model of intellectual honesty — it keeps both the naive table and the corrected tables, states "Allowance for RTM is important but hard to estimate", and still concludes that cameras prevented roughly 800–1,300 serious/fatal casualties in the year to March 2004 — the opposite of "statistics can prove anything".
- Better than its reputation: Kahneman's flight-school anecdote is told by Kahneman himself as the reverse of a triumph: the coin-toss demonstration changed nobody's mind ("I knew that this demonstration would not undo the effects of lifelong exposure to a perverse contingency") — a point about why the artefact is hard to see, not a claim that it was solved.

### Boundary
- RTM needs two things at once: an imperfect correlation r < 1 between the two measurements and selection on the first one; with r near 1 (a well-built exam: SAT section reliability .91–.93) the artefact is small, and with r near 0 (a 45-at-bat average) it is almost total.
- It is a statement about group averages, not a forecast for individuals (Morton & Torgerson 2003): individuals in an extreme group may move further out.
- It does not mean "nothing is real": selection-corrected analyses still find real effects (speed cameras ≈ −15%; osteoporosis drugs; the active arm of the hypertension trial). RTM sets the baseline to beat; it does not decide the answer.
- Corrections are fragile and sometimes costly: Allsop on estimating the "appropriate mean"; Fontanari & Santos 2026 on unreliable corrections; Goldenholz et al. 2026 simulation where reducing RTM cost 6–18% more participants. The robust defences are design ones: concurrent controls, duplicate baselines on a different occasion, covariate adjustment.
- Real change mixes with it: untreated depression falls by about 10–15% in weeks and about half remit within a year in primary-care samples (Posternak & Miller; Whiteford) — natural course, expectation and regression cannot be separated from these abstracts, and the numbers do not apply to severe depression.
- Not the same as other "illusions of pattern": the hot-hand estimator bias (Miller & Sanjurjo; FACTS_T1 §06) comes from how streaks are counted inside a finite sequence, not from selecting an extreme first measurement; the winner's curse (exaggerated significant effects in underpowered studies; FACTS_T1 §02, Szucs & Ioannidis, Button et al.) is selection on a significant result — related (both are selection on a noisy extreme) but with different fixes; Simpson's paradox is a reversal when groups are pooled (Pearl 2014, *Am Stat* 68(1):8–13; not opened) and needs no second measurement at all.

- [def] ★ Bias vs noise, the identity (DERIVED, my derivation; the same split as FACTS_T6a §03-D): let judgments x₁…x_n of ONE case with true value T, error e_i = x_i − T. Mean squared error MSE = (1/n)Σe_i² = (ē)² + (1/n)Σ(e_i − ē)² = bias² + noise², where bias = ē (the average error) and noise² = the variance of the judgments (scatter around their own mean). Nothing about the truth is needed to measure noise: it is the spread of judgments of the SAME case, which is why "noise audits" can run without ground truth. Worked numbers: bias 5 and noise SD 10 → MSE = 25 + 100 = 125 (RMSE ≈ 11.2)
  - remove all bias → 100; remove all noise → 25. Averaging n judges with independent noise gives MSE = b² + σ²/n: b = 5, σ = 10 → 125 (n = 1), 50 (n = 4), 31.25 (n = 16) [CORRECTION (lead, 2026-10-14): 25 + 100/16 = 31.25, not 30.6], → 25 (n → ∞): averaging cuts noise but never the bias. If the judges' errors are correlated ρ, the variance of the average is σ²[ρ + (1−ρ)/n], so shared (non-independent) error does not average away (DERIVED; standard result).
- [def] ★ Three flavours, as named by Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein 2021 (*Noise*, Little, Brown) and as reported in a review of the book: level noise = differences between judges in their average level (e.g. harsh vs lenient); pattern noise = a judge's idiosyncratic reaction to particular cases (judge × case interaction); occasion noise = the same judge's variability on different occasions (e.g. a radiologist reading the same mammogram differently on different days)
  - system noise = the total, (level² + pattern²) with occasion noise inside pattern noise. The book is not peer-reviewed; its statistics below are quoted through a review, not checked against the book. — Kinni, "How noisy is your company?" (book review, strategy+business, page opened: https://www.strategy-business.com/article/How-noisy-is-your-company)
- [~] Numbers from the book as reported in that review (NOT checked in the book; treat as "the authors report"): 828 senior executives' median expected variation in expert judgments was 10%; in the authors' noise audit of an insurance company the median difference between two underwriters' prices for the same policy was 55% and between two claims adjusters' payouts for the same claim 43%
  - the review's list of other book claims: heroin-dealer sentences "from one to 10 years", bank robbers "from five to 18"; mammogram false-negative rates by radiologist "from zero to more than 50 percent"; "70 to 80 percent" of the variance in performance appraisals unrelated to performance; "62 to 65 percent" probability that two interviewers give similar ratings after meeting the same candidate. The 5–18-year and 1–10-year figures trace to the Second Circuit study below, where they are single paper cases (see Myths)
  - the Scullen 2000 study below gives the real source for the appraisal numbers (53–62% idiosyncratic rater effects, not 70–80%).

### Myths and corrections
- [✗] ★ "Judges give 1 year or 10 years for the same heroin sale, 5 or 18 for the same bank robbery, depending on the judge" — these are the ranges for single, edited paper cases in a 1974 experiment (Case 3, a heroin sale by a defendant with seven prior convictions: 1–10 years; Case 2, a bank robbery: 5–18 years; Second Circuit study, Table 1), and the study's own text says "it would be erroneous to conclude that the range of sentences for bank robbery… is 5 years to 18 years". Stated sentences also overstate time served (Case 1: parole eligibility 1 year–6⅔ years against a 3–20 year range). In real federal cases the spread between judges' AVERAGE sentences was about 4.9 months (17%) before the Guidelines (Anderson, Kling & Stith 1999).
- [✗] "Noise can only be found if you know the right answer" — the identity MSE = bias² + noise² and every study above (identical cases, same experts later) show noise as spread among judgments of the same case with no ground truth.
- [✗] "The insurance company's 55% / 43% differences are a scientific finding" — they come from the authors' own audit of one firm, reported in a book; I did not find a peer-reviewed paper with those numbers, and the reviews give them second-hand.
- [✗] "Judges are harsher when it is hot / hungry / their team lost" — graded D (temperature: Spamann's re-analysis), C in FACTS_T6a and D in FACTS_T3a (hungry judge; both files cite a simulation showing the original pattern can arise from case ordering), C (football). The robust, replicated phenomenon is smaller and more boring: judges disagree with each other on identical cases.
- [✗] "Fingerprints/pathology/radiology are objective, so experts agree" — same-expert-later agreement is 89–90% for fingerprint decisions (Ulery 2012) but 35–64% for the middle classes of melanocytic lesions (Elmore 2017), and 48% concordance with the reference for breast atypia (Elmore 2015).
- [✗] "So experts are worthless / fingerprints are junk science" — the opposite error: the largest black-box study found a 0.1% false-positive rate and that independent verification caught all false positives (Ulery 2011); the finding is variability on borderline cases and susceptibility to context, not uselessness.
- [✗] "Formulas beat experts, always" — meta-analyses give "somewhat greater accuracy" (Aegisdóttir: 13% in the strictest sample), formulas can be no better than laypeople when the data are thin (Dressel & Farid: 65.2% vs crowd 67.0%), and the best prediction of life outcomes was "not very accurate" (Salganik). The robust claim is "equal or better, and more consistent".
- [✗] "People hate algorithms" — Logg 2019: lay people gave algorithms MORE weight than people; experts less. Aversion is context-dependent (Dietvorst 2015 vs Logg 2019; HICSS 2025 meta-analysis of interventions).
- [✗] "Unstructured interviews have no validity" — mean operational validity .19 after the 2022 correction (observed .13–.21), low rather than zero; structured .42. The gap depends on corrections, and the 2022 authors had to revise earlier values downward by .10–.20.
- [✗] "Averaging a group always beats the best individual" — it removes noise, never shared bias; in Kurvers 2016 it beats the best doctor only when the doctors' accuracy is similar; independence matters (Lorenz 2011: social information narrowed diversity without improving the error), though the effect of social influence is not uniformly harmful (Becker 2017, FACTS_T7d) and FACTS_T7d cites Davis-Stober 2014 for averaging being robust even with biased, correlated judges.
- [✗] "Decision hygiene is proven" — see the STUDY: individual ingredients are supported, the package is not tested; a criteria-checklist for EEG experts did not change reliability (Yuan 2025).
- Better than its reputation: Kahneman's army story is a sober account of how a first structured interview schedule, and then a global rating produced AFTER the structured ratings, raised validity from about .10 to about .30 — in the author's recollection — and the same recipe is what the modern structured-interview meta-analysis ranks first (.42).
- Better than its reputation: Dawes's "improper linear models" are not a gimmick: on the Oregon admissions data the equal-weighted composite (.48) beat the cross-validated regression (.38) and the committee (.19, on 23 students), and "a model of the judge" beat the judge in every study he tabulates.

### Bet candidates
- BET 1 ★ (B): "At a major US wine competition the same expert judges were given three samples poured from the same bottle in one session. About what share of judges gave the same medal level to all three?" Truth: about 10% replicated their score within a single medal group; roughly another 10% on occasion rated the same wine anywhere from Bronze to Gold (Hodgson 2008, 2005–2008 panels, 65–70 judges a year). Tolerance: accept 5–15%. Not sensitive. Grade B.
- BET 2 (B): "In two big 360-degree rating data sets of managers (n = 2,350 and 2,142; seven raters each), what share of the variance in the ratings was the rater's own idiosyncrasy, and what share was the manager's actual performance?" Truth: idiosyncratic rater effects 62% and 53%; ratee performance (general + dimension) 21% and 25%; random error 11% and 18% (Scullen, Mount & Goff 2000). Tolerance: rater effect 50–65%, ratee 20–25%. Grade B.
- BET 3 (A): "Experienced pathologists re-read the same melanocytic skin-biopsy slides at least 8 months later: how often did they give the same diagnostic class for the 'moderately atypical' cases (class II)?" Truth: 35.2% (and 76.7% for plainly benign class I, 82.6% for invasive melanoma class V) (Elmore et al. 2017, 187 pathologists). Tolerance: accept 25–45%. Not contested; medical but not advice. Grade A for the direction, numbers are from a test set enriched for hard cases (say so).
- Reserve BET (A/B): "Fingerprint examiners re-tested after about 7 months: share of individualization decisions they repeated?" Truth: 89.1% (exclusions 90.1%; 72 examiners; Ulery et al. 2012). Tolerance 85–93%.
- Reserve BET (B): "Second Circuit judges, 20 representative cases: in how many did the judges not even agree on whether any prison at all was appropriate?" Truth: 16 of 20 (Partridge & Eldridge 1974). Tolerance: ±2.

### Widget constants
- Model (deterministic toy): judgment_jc = T_c + b + L_j + P_jc (+ O_jco if the same judge repeats), with L_j ~ N(0, σ_L²), P_jc ~ N(0, σ_P²), O ~ N(0, σ_O²) independent; then bias² = b², noise² = σ_L² + σ_P² + σ_O² (the book's level/pattern/occasion split), MSE = b² + σ_L² + σ_P² + σ_O² (DERIVED, standard variance-components algebra); averaging k INDEPENDENT judges: MSE = b² + σ_L²/k + σ_P²/k + σ_O²/k
  - with k independent repeats of the SAME judge only the occasion part shrinks: MSE = b² + σ_L² + σ_P² + σ_O²/k (DERIVED — this is why asking one expert twice helps less than asking different experts). Correlated errors: var of the average = σ²[ρ + (1−ρ)/k] (DERIVED).
- Published anchors the widget may print next to the sliders (not as the model's inputs): variance shares in 360-degree ratings 62%/53% idiosyncratic rater, 21%/25% ratee, 11%/18% error [V, Scullen 2000]; Second Circuit Case 1 sentences 3–20 years (median 10), Case 2 5–18 (n = 45, 48 ranked) [V, FJC]; real-case judge-to-judge spread of average sentence 17% (4.9 months) → 11% (3.9 months) [V, AKS 1999]
  - same fingerprint decision repeated after ≈ 7 months 89.1–90.1% [V]; same melanocytic class repeated after ≥ 8 months 35.2–82.6% by class [V]; clinic breast-screening order rate 63.7% (8 am) → 48.7% (11 am) → 56.2% (noon) → 47.8% (5 pm) [V, Hsiang 2019]; same wine, same bottle: ≈ 10% of judges within one medal group [V, Hodgson 2008]; mean validity .42 vs .19 [V, Sackett 2022].

### Boundary
- Noise is measurable only where the cases are comparable: identical presentence reports, identical slides, identical prints. For real sentences, case mix, plea bargaining and parole enter (Partridge & Eldridge; Gabrielson/ProPublica; AKS).
- Not every disagreement is error: the Second Circuit report defines disparity as dissimilar treatment of similarly situated defendants, and legitimate discretion, local norms and judges' differing weights on aims are not noise in the engineering sense; a rule that removes the spread can also remove wanted flexibility (the "broken leg", [~] Meehl 1954).
- Formulas inherit bias and have ceilings: proxy labels (Obermeyer: cost for illness), thin features (Dressel & Farid: two features ≈ COMPAS's 137 and ≈ lay-crowd accuracy), limits of predictability (Salganik).
- The "irrelevant influence" literature is mixed: Danziger (C in FACTS_T6a, D in FACTS_T3a), temperature (D), football (C), sequence effects (C), while time-of-day effects in clinics (B) are observational; the safest statement is "decisions vary with things that should not matter, by an amount that is often small and sometimes unestablished".
- Independence is the hinge of aggregation: second opinions after a prior diagnosis are not independent (Elmore 2022, Kerr 2026), social information narrows diversity and in Lorenz 2011 did not improve the collective error (but improved accuracy in decentralised networks in Becker 2017, FACTS_T7d), and averaging does not remove bias shared by all judges.
- The evidence base for noise in medicine is thin: a 2025 scoping review found 14 qualifying studies from 2,082 hits (Dlugos et al.); percentages from enriched test sets are not clinical error rates.
