## 15 · 经历与记忆：你记得的，不是你经历的

### Core evidence
#### 15-A · The cold-pressor experiment: "when more pain is preferred to less" (Kahneman 1993)
- ★[V] Design (read in the full-text PDF of the JSTOR copy): 32 male University of California students, age 19–39 (median 22.5), paid $10 for a 1-hour session; each did TWO immersions, one hand each, counterbalanced for order and for dominant/non-dominant hand. SHORT trial: hand in water at 14 °C for 60 s. LONG trial: 60 s at 14 °C, then 30 s more while the water was slowly warmed (mean 14.1 °C → 15.2 °C over the last 30 s), "still painful but distinctly less so for most subjects". Subjects expected a third trial and were asked to choose which of the two to repeat. 7 min wait between trials (doing a personality inventory); 2-min room-temperature baseline before each; real-time discomfort on a 15-LED "discomfort meter" (values 0–14; potentiometer sampled 5×/s, 1-s means recorded); the water warmed by 1.1 °C (±0.3) in 30 s by switching circulation pumps. The choice question: "Suppose we paid you to come back tomorrow to repeat just one of the two cold-water trials". EXCLUSIONS: 3 participants were replaced (1 for temperature-control problems, 2 because they did not give a consistent preference between the trials) — so 22/32 is among consistent choosers. — Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier 1993, *Psychol Sci* 4(6):401–405, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x (abstract via Crossref; method/results from the JSTOR PDF copy, https://www.jstor.org/stable/40062570)
- ★[V] Result: 22 of 32 (69%) chose to repeat the LONG trial (z = 2.15, p < .05 by sign test) — "this proportion would be zero if subjects acted to minimize their exposure to pain". Among the 21 subjects whose discomfort fell by ≥ 2 points in the last 30 s, 17 (81%) chose the long trial; the 11 subjects with little or no decrement split 6:5 in favour of the SHORT trial. The preference correlated with the drop in discomfort in the last 30 s (biserial r = .38, p < .05). — same paper, Results p. 403
- ★[V] In-paper replication WITHOUT the real-time discomfort meter: 24 of 37 participants (65%) chose the long trial. — same paper, General Discussion p. 404
- [V] Memory vs choice: choice correlated .80 (biserial) with the post-choice comparison of overall discomfort but only .16 with the comparison of durations; most participants could correctly report the relative durations (mean duration-difference judgment correct, t[31] = 3.27) but did not use it — "an attentional phenomenon", not an inability. Six participants said the long trial was actually shorter. — same paper, pp. 403–404
- [V] ★ The authors state the boundary themselves: "We do not propose that duration is always neglected" — it likely matters for episodes "either very much longer or very much shorter than expected"; nor are peak and end "the only relevant predictors"; hope-related positive affect may not count if the episode ends in disappointment. They also say they do not believe participants would actually prefer 90 s of slowly warming water over a dry towel after 60 s. — same paper, p. 404 (paraphrase; no more than one short quotation used)
- [V] Prior studies the 1993 paper builds on: Varey & Kahneman 1992 (JBDM; global evaluations of someone else's discomfort series: an unweighted combination of peak and END discomfort accounted for 94% of the variance; adding duration raised R² by only a further 3%), Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993 (film clips, below), Redelmeier & Kahneman 1993/1996 (colonoscopy; procedure length 4–69 min did not significantly affect retrospective ratings). — same paper, p. 401 (the 94%/3% figures are the 1993 paper's description of Varey & Kahneman 1992, whose own abstract was not opened [~])
- ★[✗] "In the cold-water experiment people preferred more pain to less" — CORRECTED: true only for the 30 s of milder (still painful) water: the long trial = the short trial PLUS a final stretch of slightly less pain; only 69% (22/32, all male students) chose it, and those who felt no real relief at the end were split 6:5 for the short trial. The participants' own chosen trial was not "pure extra pain with no relief".
- ★[✗] "Kahneman's cold-water study shows duration doesn't matter" — CORRECTED: the 1993 design adds duration AND a better end together; it does not isolate duration. Duration neglect rests on the film-clip and colonoscopy studies and on the meta-analysis (15-C).
- STUDY Cold-pressor "better end" choice (Kahneman et al. 1993) — original: N = 32 male students; short (60 s at 14 °C) vs long (same + 30 s warming to ≈15 °C); 22/32 = 69% chose to repeat the long trial; in-paper replication 24/37 = 65% (no real-time ratings). Later evidence: the replication within the same lab; the conceptual replications in 15-B (aversive sounds, colonoscopy RCT); no independent pre-registered replication of the exact cold-pressor design found.
  GRADE B: the effect sits inside a well-replicated retrospective-evaluation pattern (peak-end/duration neglect, meta-analytic r large) and a real clinical RCT shows the same direction, but the original choice result is a small single-lab sample of one sex, the proportion is modest (69%), and it vanishes for people who feel no relief at the end. — Kahneman 1993; Redelmeier 2003; Alaybek 2022

#### 15-B · Real episodes: colonoscopy, lithotripsy, film clips, aversive sounds
- ★[V] Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996: real-time pain every 60 s in colonoscopy (n = 154) and lithotripsy (n = 133); patients' retrospective judgments of total pain were "strongly correlated" with the PEAK pain (P < 0.005) and with the pain in the LAST 3 min (P < 0.005); "despite substantial variation in the duration of the experience, lengthy procedures were not remembered as particularly aversive". — *Pain* 66(1):3–8, PMID 8857625, doi:10.1016/0304-3959(96)02994-6 (abstract opened on PubMed; no erratum/retraction flag in the record). The individual correlation coefficients and the peak/end weights are NOT in the abstract (full text paywalled) — [not opened].
- ★[V] Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman 2003 RCT: 682 consecutive colonoscopy outpatients, randomised; in the "extended" group a short interval was added at the end during which the tip of the colonoscope stayed in the rectum (less painful than the main procedure). Results: pain in the final moments 1.7 vs 2.5 on a 10-point scale (P < 0.001); overall unpleasantness 4.4 vs 4.9 on a 10-cm VAS (P = 0.006); ranked against seven other unpleasant experiences 4.1 vs 4.6, 8 = worst (P = 0.002). Return for a repeat colonoscopy (median follow-up 5.3 years) averaged 50.4% overall; OR = 1.41 (P = 0.038) for the longer procedure, controlling for prior colonoscopy, indications and abnormal findings. — *Pain* 104(1–2):187–194, PMID 12855328, doi:10.1016/s0304-3959(03)00003-4 (abstract opened on PubMed; no erratum flag)
- ★[✗] "Patients whose colonoscopy was prolonged by a gentle minute were far more likely to come back" — CORRECTED: the retrospective ratings improved only slightly (a 0.5-point VAS difference on a 10-cm scale: 4.4 vs 4.9) and the return effect is a modest adjusted odds ratio of 1.41 (P = 0.038) from a 50.4% overall return rate — not a large behavioural change; the odds ratio is covariate-adjusted, and the abstract does not say whether return was the trial's pre-specified primary outcome. [The raw return percentages per arm are not in the abstract — not opened.]
- [~] The extra interval in the 2003 trial is commonly described as "about one minute"; the abstract says only "a short interval" (full text not opened) — do not state "60 s" for the colonoscopy trial in a lesson without the full text.
- ★[V] Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993: Study 1, 32 participants viewed aversive and pleasant film clips differing in duration and intensity, giving real-time affect ratings and global evaluations; Study 2, 96 participants ranked the clips from memory (controls were told in advance and encouraged to evaluate on-line). Effects of film duration on retrospective evaluation were "small, entirely explained by changes in real-time affect and further reduced when made from memory"; evaluations track "a weighted average of 'snapshots'". — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 65(1):45–55, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.65.1.45 (abstract via OpenAlex; no retraction flag)
- [V] Redelmeier's colonoscopy ratings were also given independently by the physician and nurse; their judgments were likewise dominated by the worst and final moments and unrelated to duration (Redelmeier & Kahneman 1993 as summarised in Kahneman et al. 1993, p. 401–402). [~] second-hand.
- STUDY Peak-end in clinical procedures (Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996; Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman 2003) — original: 154 colonoscopy + 133 lithotripsy patients (real-time pain vs retrospective total); RCT n = 682 (final interval milder vs not). Later evidence: Alaybek et al. 2022 meta-analysis pooling 174 effect sizes (15-C); no independent replication of the colonoscopy RCT found.
  GRADE B: the clinical studies are real, one is randomised with n = 682 and a median 5.3-year follow-up, but they come from the originating lab, the memory effects are small in absolute rating points (0.5/10 cm) and the return-rate effect is modest (OR 1.41, P = .038). — Redelmeier 1996; Redelmeier 2003

#### 15-C · The meta-analysis (decides the grades of the headline claims)
- ★[V] Alaybek, Dalal, Fyffe, Aitken, Zhou, Qu, Roman & Baines 2022, "All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect", *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 170:104149, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149 — CORRECTED authorship/venue (task file had "Sander, McCloy?" — wrong; authors are the eight above; venue OBHDP, not JBDM). Abstract (read on RePEc/EconPapers, reproducing the Elsevier abstract): "We meta-analyzed 174 effect sizes and found strong support for the peak-end rule." The peak-end effect on retrospective summary evaluations was (1) large (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487–0.661); (2) robust across boundary conditions; (3) "comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score"; (4) stronger than trend and variability across all episodes, and stronger than the first (beginning) and lowest-intensity (trough) episodes; (5) stronger than the effect of duration, "which was essentially nil", supporting duration neglect.
- ★[V] The author's 2020 dissertation (George Mason University, advisor R. Dalal) reports effect sizes from 85 studies on the peak effect and 95 on the end effect and a peak-end effect "r = .57", with the same qualitative conclusions (abstract page https://io.gmu.edu/defenses/1259). The published r = 0.581 is the figure to use; the dissertation number is a slightly different earlier analysis. [~] second-hand abstract.
- ★[V] ERRATUM EXISTS: Corrigendum to the article, OBHDP 180:104278 (2024), doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2023.104278 (Crossref flags the original as "updated-by: erratum", 1 Jan 2024). I could NOT retrieve the text (ScienceDirect returns 403 to fetchers, no open copy found) — so whether any number in the abstract (r = 0.581; 95% CI; the comparison with the mean) was changed is UNKNOWN. Writers: use the numbers only as "r ≈ .58, large" and say "as reported in 2022; a corrigendum exists" — do not quote the CI to three decimals.
- ★[✗] "Peak-end beats the average: the peak-end rule predicts memory better than the mean of the whole experience" — CORRECTED: the meta-analysis says the peak-end effect is "comparable" to the mean score's effect; it is better than trend, variability, beginning and trough, and better than duration. So the defensible statement is "memory tracks a summary of the moments (peak, end, and also the average), not the length".
- ★[✗] "Duration doesn't matter at all" — overstated: the meta-analytic duration effect is "essentially nil" for retrospective summary evaluations in the pooled studies, but Kahneman et al. 1993 themselves say duration may matter for episodes much longer or shorter than expected; and duration is available to the person (cold-pressor participants could report which trial was longer). The right reading: duration is under-weighted in retrospective evaluation, not absent from experience.
- [V] The lead sentence of the abstract defines the rule: people retrospectively evaluate an experience by relying more heavily on the episode with peak intensity and on the final episode than on other episodes (attributed there to Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993). — Alaybek 2022 abstract
- STUDY Peak-end rule (retrospective evaluation) — original: Varey & Kahneman 1992; Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993 (32 and 96 film-clip viewers); Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996 (n = 154 + 133). Later evidence: Alaybek et al. 2022 meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes — r = 0.581 (95% CI 0.487–0.661), robust across boundary conditions in the studied moderators, but "comparable" to the mean of all moments; corrigendum issued 2024 (text not read).
  GRADE A: a large meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes from many teams finds a large peak-end association with retrospective summary evaluation, in the same direction as the original studies; the caveat that the mean is about as good a predictor is carried in the sentence, not the grade. — Alaybek 2022
- STUDY Duration neglect in retrospective evaluation — original: Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993 (film clips: duration effects small, explained by real-time affect); Redelmeier & Kahneman 1996 (4–69 min colonoscopies not remembered as worse). Later evidence: Alaybek et al. 2022 (duration effect "essentially nil" across the pooled effect sizes).
  GRADE B: the pooled effect of duration on retrospective summary evaluations is essentially nil, but the original authors limit it to episodes of ordinary length, the studies measure ratings not behaviour, and duration neglect has to be read from non-significant effects whose upper bounds depend on how the duration manipulation was scaled (the corrigendum text was not read). — Alaybek 2022; Kahneman 1993 p. 404

#### 15-D · Outside the lab: longer, multi-episode and everyday experiences (where peak-end gets weaker or disappears)
- ★[V] Schreiber & Kahneman 2000: unpleasant sounds of variable loudness and duration; Exp 1 continuous annoyance reports tracked intensity; Exp 3 confirmed that "the effects of sound duration and intensity are additive in multitrial experiments"; Exp 4 confirmed "a robust preference for aversive episodes that are 'improved' by adding a period of reduced aversiveness". — *J Exp Psychol Gen* 129(1):27–42, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.129.1.27 (abstract via OpenAlex; N per experiment not in the abstract; no retraction flag). NOTE: the additivity result means duration DOES enter evaluations in some designs (multitrial) — a boundary of "duration neglect".
- ★[V] Stone, Broderick, Kaell, DelesPaul & Porter 2000: 32 patients with rheumatoid arthritis gave momentary pain ratings over 7 days and recalled the week's pain on day 8; "a combination of peak and recent pain was a better predictor of recalled patient pain than was a simple average of all momentary pain reports". — *J Pain* 1(3):212–217, PMID 14622620, doi:10.1054/jpai.2000.7568 (abstract opened)
- ★[V] Kemp, Burt & Furneaux 2008: 49 students on vacation (average 7 days) texted daily happiness for the previous 24 h; afterwards they judged overall happiness. "The duration of the vacation had no effect"; participants could not recall day-to-day detail; "a number of summary measures provided reasonable prediction"; "the peak-end rule was not an outstandingly good predictor"; the results "indicate much reconstruction of the affective states". — *Mem Cognit* 36(1):132–138, PMID 18323069, doi:10.3758/mc.36.1.132 (abstract opened)
- ★[V] Miron-Shatz 2009: large samples from the US, France and Denmark (N = 810, 820, 805) reported their feelings in each episode of the previous day (Day Reconstruction Method). "Contrary to the predictions of the peak-end rule, the average was the best predictor of retrospective evaluations of the day"; evaluations "rely on the averaged ratings of emotions, ignore ends, and also consider the presence of lows, and occasionally peaks, as subjectively defined by those experiencing them"; peaks and lows matter more for comparative than absolute evaluations. — *Emotion* 9(2):206–213, doi:10.1037/a0015295 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- ★[V] Scharbert, Utesch, Reiter, ter Horst, van Zalk & Back 2024/25: four experience-sampling samples, overall N = 1,889, 131,575 measurements; retrospective well-being judgments were "disproportionately influenced by the peak and end experiences", but "these effects depended on the item framing of the retrospective judgment (global vs. more specific framings) and broad versus narrow conceptualizations of peaks and ends (states, days, and weeks)". — *Eur J Pers* 39(1):55–69 (online 2024), doi:10.1177/08902070241235969 (abstract via OpenAlex; most recent everyday-life test found, as of Oct 2026)
- ★[V] Rode, Rozin & Durlach 2007: liking of dishes during a meal vs recalled liking, two studies with actual meals and one imagined; "consistent evidence for duration neglect" (more of the favourite dish did not change the memory) but "in all three studies, there was no evidence for peak, primacy or recency effects" and only weak evidence that rising profiles are preferred. — *Appetite* 49(1):18–29, PMID 17459522, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2006.09.006 (abstract opened). [Consequence stated by the authors: a small portion of a much-liked dish has about the same memorial weight as a large one.]
- [V] Do, Rupert & Wolford 2008: applied the peak-end idea to material goods ("strong evidence that the peak-end rule applies to both material goods and pain"; sample sizes not in the abstract). — *Psychon Bull Rev* 15(1):96–98, PMID 18605486, doi:10.3758/pbr.15.1.96 (abstract opened; N not read). Single paper, pleasant domain.
- [V] Diener, Wirtz & Oishi 2001, "the James Dean effect": in three studies of rated life quality "participants neglected duration"; a wonderful life ending abruptly was rated better than one with additional mildly pleasant years; a terrible life with extra moderately bad years was rated MORE desirable than one ending abruptly; adding years in the MIDDLE did not produce effects as strong as adding them at the END. — *Psychol Sci* 12(2):124–128, doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00321 (abstract via OpenAlex; vignette ratings, not lived experience).
- [V] Chajut, Caspi, Chen, Hod & Ariely 2014: labour pain recalled 2 days and 2 months after delivery was "biased toward the average of the peak pain and the end pain"; duration of delivery had "a relatively negligible effect"; mothers whose labour ended with vs without epidural showed an end-pain effect; "having given birth before weakened the effect 2 months after delivery". — *Psychol Sci* 25(12):2266–2271, PMID 25298293, doi:10.1177/0956797614551004 (abstract opened; N not in the abstract). CAUTION: D. Ariely is the last author — status of THIS paper: no retraction/erratum flag in PubMed or Crossref (checked 2026-10-14); not used as the sole source of any claim.
- ★[V] Wirtz, Kruger, Scollon & Diener 2003: students' predicted, on-line and remembered spring-break experiences; "predicted and remembered experiences were both more positive — and, paradoxically, more negative — than on-line experiences"; path analysis: "remembered experience, but neither on-line nor anticipated experience, directly predicted the desire to repeat the experience". — *Psychol Sci* 14(5):520–524, doi:10.1111/1467-9280.03455 (abstract via OpenAlex; N NOT verified — the paper was not accessible; do not state N). Interpretation by the authors: on-line measures approximate objective experience best, retrospective measures predict choice better.
- [V] Day Reconstruction Method (DRM): Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz & Stone 2004 — participants "systematically reconstruct their activities and experiences of the preceding day"; the paper reports "close correspondences between the DRM reports of 909 employed women and established results from experience sampling". — *Science* 306(5702):1776–1780, doi:10.1126/science.1103572 (abstract via OpenAlex; single-lab sample of 909 employed women [the "Texas" origin is from second-hand accounts — not used]). Kahneman & Krueger 2006 propose the U-index = "the proportion of time that people spend in an unpleasant state" (*J Econ Perspect* 20(1):3–24, doi:10.1257/089533006776526030, abstract via OpenAlex). The activity-level U-index figures widely quoted (e.g. a 29% morning commute) were NOT verified in a primary source — banned.
- [V] Later RCTs of a "better end" by independent teams: (1) Hetou et al. 2021 — flexible cystoscopy, 85 randomised (82 analysed: 45 standard vs 37 modified), a 2-min un-manipulated period with the scope left in the bladder at the end; mean pain 23.20 vs 11.97 (P = .039) and anxiety 2.09 vs 0.88 (P = .013) on the trial's scales (scale units not in the abstract) — *Urology* 154:33–39, PMID 33716036, doi:10.1016/j.urology.2021.02.033; (2) Yen et al. 2023 — Pap smear, women 30–70 y, ITT n = 266 (133 per arm), a nonpainful step added at the end: 5-min recalled pain 1.50 vs 2.02 (1–5 scale) and 2.12 vs 3.12 (0–10 VAS), both P < 0.001; 1-year willingness-to-receive-future-Pap-tests grade adjusted β = 2.11 (SE 0.27, P < 0.001) — *Pain* 164(8):1709–1717, PMID 37043729, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000002897. NOTE: (1) is a single-centre trial with an unequal split (45 vs 37) and whether clinicians could be blinded to arm is not stated in the abstract; (2) measures stated willingness to return, not actual return.
- [V] Review: Horwitz, McCarthy & Sen 2024 — the peak-end rule "has been understudied in mental health contexts"; intensive-longitudinal data now allow tests in retrospective recall of symptoms; "additional research is needed to better understand the contexts in which, and persons for whom, the peak-end rule presents a greater risk of bias". — *Curr Opin Psychol* 58:101845, PMID 39018885 (abstract opened).
- STUDY Better-end RCTs by independent teams (cystoscopy 2021; Pap smear 2023) — original: Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman 2003 (n = 682); Later evidence: Hetou 2021 (n = 82 analysed; pain 23.20 vs 11.97, P = .039), Yen 2023 (n = 266; recalled pain lower, P < 0.001; stated willingness higher).
  GRADE B: two further randomised trials from different teams point the same way for recalled pain, but samples are modest, one outcome is stated willingness not behaviour, one is single-centre, and no registered multi-site replication was found. — Hetou 2021; Yen 2023
- STUDY END and PEAK weights in long multi-episode everyday evaluations (days, weeks, trips) — original: Stone 2000 (32 RA patients, 7 days: peak+recent beat the average); Chajut 2014 (labour pain); Later evidence: Kemp 2008 (N = 49 vacation: "not an outstandingly good predictor"); Miron-Shatz 2009 (N ≈ 2,435 across three countries: the average was the best predictor, ends ignored); Scharbert 2024 (N = 1,889 ESM: peak/end effects present but depend on item framing and the time unit).
  GRADE C: mixed — supportive and contradicting studies of similar quality, strong dependence on the measure; the large meta-analysis finds the peak-end association "robust" but also "comparable" to the plain average, so for long everyday events the specific claim "the ending outweighs the rest" is not settled. — Miron-Shatz 2009; Scharbert 2024; Kemp 2008; Alaybek 2022
- STUDY Remembered (not on-line) experience predicts wanting to repeat (Wirtz et al. 2003) — original: Wirtz, Kruger, Scollon & Diener 2003 (spring-break trips; path analysis). Later evidence: Redelmeier 2003 return rates (OR 1.41) and Yen 2023 stated willingness are indirect support; no direct independent replication of the spring-break design found.
  GRADE C: one small single-lab study (N not verified) with a path-analytic claim, supported indirectly by clinical return-rate data of small size. — Wirtz 2003; Redelmeier 2003

#### 15-E · Experienced vs decision vs remembered utility; the "two selves"
- ★[def] Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin 1997: two core meanings of "utility": **decision utility** 决策效用 = "the weight of an outcome in a decision"; **experienced utility** 体验效用 = "hedonic quality, as in Bentham's usage", which can be reported in real time (**instant utility** 即时效用) or in retrospective evaluations of past episodes (**remembered utility** 记忆效用). "Psychological research has documented systematic errors in retrospective evaluations, which can induce a preference for dominated options." They propose a formal normative theory of the total experienced utility of temporally extended outcomes. — *Q J Econ* 112(2):375–406, doi:10.1162/003355397555235 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] Dolan & Kahneman (2008; issue date on Crossref 2007): decision utility is "unlikely to generate meaningful data on the utility associated with different experiences", so economists "should look towards developing measures that focus more directly on experienced utility" (the case argued for health valuation). — *Econ J* 118(525):215–234, doi:10.1111/j.1468-0297.2007.02110.x (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] Critique: Read 2007/2006 reviews the history of "experience utility" and examines three normative claims made for it — that it avoids dependent utilities, that it can be measured from an invariant zero point, and that it allows intrapersonal comparison — i.e., they are open to question. — *Think Reason* 13(1):45–61, doi:10.1080/13546780600872627 (abstract via OpenAlex; the abstract states the claims were "examined"; the verdict is not in the abstract — do not say Read rejected them)
- [~] "Two selves" 两个自我 (the experiencing self vs the remembering self): popularised in Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) and developed in Kahneman & Riis 2005, "Living, and thinking about it: two perspectives on life", in *The Science of Well-Being* (OUP), pp. 284–305, doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198567523.003.0011 (chapter not opened — Crossref record only). Say "Kahneman's distinction", not a measured quantity.
- ★[V] The ledger of the lesson in one sentence of evidence: in the cold-pressor design choices tracked the remembered comparison (r = .80) not the duration comparison (r = .16) — Kahneman et al. 1993; in the spring-break study the remembered but not on-line experience predicted the wish to repeat — Wirtz 2003; the colonoscopy RCT shows the same direction in a real procedure with a small effect (OR 1.41) — Redelmeier 2003.
- [~] Boundary of "remembered utility is a mistake": Kahneman et al. 1993 call the choice "a mistake" and "a violation of temporal monotonicity" in the cold-pressor design where the longer trial contains all of the short trial's pain — a normative claim about that design, not a general claim that remembered experience is wrong (they also write that "evaluated memories are the only available guide for many decisions"). — Kahneman 1993 p. 404 (paraphrase)

#### 15-F · Affective forecasting: the third ledger (what you PREDICT)
- ★[def] Affective forecasting 情感预测 (Wilson & Gilbert 2005, review, *Curr Dir Psychol Sci* 14(3):131–134, doi:10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x): predicting how one will feel about future events; **impact bias** 影响偏差 = overestimating the intensity and duration of reactions; **durability bias** 持久性偏差 = overestimating duration; **focalism** 聚焦错觉 = focusing on the event and neglecting other events that will also shape feelings; **immune neglect** 免疫忽视 = not anticipating the "psychological immune system" of coping and sense-making. (Review; already summarised in FACTS_T5 §19-C — definitions only repeated for the lesson writer.)
- ★[V] Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg & Wheatley 1998 (immune neglect): six studies in which participants overestimated the duration of their reactions to the end of a romantic relationship, failing to get tenure, an electoral defeat, negative personality feedback, an account of a child's death, and rejection by a prospective employer; participants "failed to distinguish between situations in which their psychological immune systems would and would not be likely to operate". — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 75(3):617–638, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.3.617 (abstract via OpenAlex; N per study and the tenure-study means were NOT accessible — do not state them). CORRECTED vs task file: the study list in the abstract is relationship, tenure, election, personality feedback, child's death story, employer rejection.

### Myths and corrections
- ★[✗] "People preferred more pain to less" — the long trial = the short trial + a final stretch of slightly milder (still painful) water; 69% (22/32) of male students chose it, 6:5 for the short trial among the 11 who felt no relief (15-A).
- ★[✗] "Peak-end beats the average" — the 2022 meta-analysis (174 effect sizes) calls the peak-end effect "comparable" to the mean-score effect; it beats trend, variability, beginning, trough and duration (15-C).
- ★[✗] "Duration doesn't matter at all" — pooled duration effect on retrospective summary ratings "essentially nil", but the originators bound it to episodes of ordinary length; in multitrial sound experiments duration and intensity were additive (Schreiber & Kahneman 2000); duration is still available to the person (15-A, 15-D).
- ★[✗] "A short gentler ending made colonoscopy patients far more likely to return" — the adjusted odds ratio was 1.41 (P = .038), overall return 50.4%; retrospective unpleasantness fell by only 0.5 on a 10-cm scale (4.4 vs 4.9) (15-B).
- [✗] "Peak-end is Kahneman's proven law / applies to every experience" — it is a description of retrospective summaries with a large pooled association (A), absent for meals (Rode 2007), not outstanding for a week-long vacation (Kemp 2008, N = 49) and beaten by the average in day-evaluations (Miron-Shatz 2009, N ≈ 2,435) (15-D).
- [✗] "A bad ending ruins the whole memory" — the effect is on the retrospective RATING (a weighted summary), small in absolute rating points in the clinical trial; the real-time record is unchanged; and even in the cold-pressor study 10 of 32 participants (31%, my arithmetic) chose the short trial.
- [✗] "People are terrible at forecasting their feelings" — see 15-F: overestimation of intensity has a pooled g ≈ .55 but ranges from ≈ 0 to .80 by question type (disputed), and relative accuracy r ≈ .28 (second-hand).
- [✗] "The experiencing self and the remembering self are two systems in the brain" — a metaphor for the distinction between real-time experience and retrospective evaluation, not a measured brain division; cite only as Kahneman's framing ([~] Kahneman & Riis 2005; book not opened).
- BETTER THAN ITS REPUTATION: the cold-pressor result replicated within the paper (24/37 without the real-time meter), and the colonoscopy study IS a randomised trial (n = 682), not just a lab curiosity; two independent RCTs (cystoscopy 2021, Pap smear 2023) point the same way for recalled pain.

### Boundary
(Feeds 「这个模型的边界」.)
- Peak-end describes retrospective RATINGS of an episode (and, in three RCTs, stated willingness or modest return-rate changes); it is not a theory of decisions in general, and the lab effect on choice (69%) is a single-lab sample of male students.
- It is weaker or absent for pleasant meals (Rode 2007: no peak/end/primacy), for a week-long vacation (Kemp 2008, N = 49: "not an outstandingly good predictor") and for evaluating a whole day (Miron-Shatz 2009: average best predictor, ends ignored); the large meta-analysis says the average is about as good as peak-end.
- Duration neglect is bounded by the authors themselves: duration may matter for episodes far longer or shorter than expected, and it is additive in multitrial sound experiments (Schreiber & Kahneman 2000); participants in the cold-pressor study could report which trial was longer.
- "Experienced utility" is itself hard to measure: real-time ratings are reports too (Kahneman et al. 1993 worried that real-time rating may raise the salience of peak and end and re-ran without it: 24/37); the Day Reconstruction Method approximates it with recall (909 employed women, single lab).
- The forecast ledger: the intensity bias is real in aggregate (g ≈ .55, 84 studies) but its size depends on the question asked and on how the studies are classed (Levine 2012 vs Wilson & Gilbert 2013); relative forecasts are partly accurate (r ≈ .28, second-hand).
- Adaptation (lesson 13 §七; FACTS_T3b §G) and the strong "no change at all" versions of it are not part of this lesson; the clinical RCTs are small-to-moderate and from few teams.

### Core evidence
#### 18-A · The spotlight effect (聚光灯效应)
- ★[V] Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000, "The spotlight effect in social judgment: an egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 78(2):211–222, PMID 10707330, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211 — full text read (PDF copy). Five studies; the effect is defined as people overestimating "the extent to which their actions and appearance are noted by others".
- ★[V] Study 1 (Barry Manilow shirt): 109 Cornell undergraduates = 15 targets (8 women, 7 men) + 64 observers + 30 controls. Each target donned a T-shirt with a 21 × 24 cm picture of the head of singer Barry Manilow ("not terribly popular among college students"; pretest interviews supported that most would be embarrassed), walked into a room of 2–6 seated observers (seating chairs on one side; a questionnaire task) and was then asked in the hall how many of them could say who is on the shirt. Targets' mean estimate was 46% of observers; observers' actual accuracy 23% ("exactly twice as high"); mean per-target discrepancy 23 percentage points, 95% CI 9–38 (27, CI 11–43, dropping the two smallest sessions). Pretest embarrassment rating 9.5 on a 1–13 scale appears in secondary sources only [~]. — Gilovich et al. 2000, Study 1 and footnote 3
- ★[V] Study 2 (shirts people liked): 79 students; 15 targets (6 women, 9 men) + 64 observers; each target chose a shirt picture (Jerry Seinfeld ×4, Martin Luther King Jr. ×5, Bob Marley ×6); targets rated the shirt as pleasant (mean 6.4). Targets' mean estimate 48% of observers vs actual 8% ("six times as great"); discrepancy 40 points, 95% CI 21–59. — same paper, Study 2 and footnote 3
- [V] Study 3: 193 Cornell undergraduates in 42 discussion groups of 3–7; afterwards each estimated how the group would rank them on six dimensions (advancing the discussion, number of speech errors, comments that might offend, comments that might be judged critically, share of talking time, most remarkable comments); participants thought others would rank them higher than others actually did on ALL six (t(41) from 2.27 to 7.07); estimates were nevertheless strongly correlated with the actual rankings within groups (people were not simply wrong about everything). Self-awareness manipulations (videotaping, mirror) had no effect. — same paper, Study 3
- [V] Study 4 (44 Northwestern undergraduates, Vanilla Ice shirt, close replication of the Study 1 procedure): average estimate 48%; 32 of 44 (73%) said they had considered another number first; of the 30 who gave one number higher/lower, 23 (77%) cited a HIGHER one (binomial z = 2.74, p < .01) — read as "start high, adjust down". Study 5 (30 Northwestern students): participants who met the observers IMMEDIATELY after donning the shirt estimated 51% of observers would identify Manilow; those who first acclimated (delay) estimated 37% (t(28) = 2.26, p < .05). — same paper, Studies 4–5
- ★[V] The authors themselves state the boundary: people "do not always overestimate"; the anchoring model predicts a REVERSE spotlight effect when people are not conscious of their own behaviour yet others notice it (their examples: smokers; habitual behaviour) — a hypothesis in the discussion, not a tested result. — same paper, General Discussion
- [V] Related corollary, reported in the General Discussion (cited there as Gilovich, Kruger, Medvec & Savitsky 1999; I did not open that source): students predicted 24% more variability in others' ratings of their appearance across five occasions than others actually showed ("bad hair day" fear). [~] second-hand description inside the 2000 paper.
- [V] Independent-lab evidence: Macrae, Mitchell, McNamara, Golubickis, Andreou, Møller, Peytcheva, Falben & Christian 2016 (3 experiments): spotlighting was lower when participants imagined an event in the far vs near future, and lower, with less embarrassment, when viewing an impending event from a third-person vs first-person vantage — *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 42(7):855–863, PMID 27149876, doi:10.1177/0146167216644961 (abstract opened; N not in abstract; this is a self-report future-oriented variant, not a re-run of the T-shirt design).
- [V] Brown & Stopa 2007: a memory task under low vs high social-evaluative conditions; in the high condition participants reported more "spotlight" and evaluated their performance more negatively; levels of the illusion of transparency did not differ between conditions; "the spotlight effect may be specific to social-evaluative concerns". — *J Anxiety Disord* 21(6):804–819, PMID 17166695, doi:10.1016/j.janxdis.2006.11.006 (abstract opened; N not in abstract). One pointer only → lesson 25 (social anxiety); no clinical content.

Do not write "replicated in Many Labs".
- [~] Pop-science rounding: the shirt result is often told as "50% vs 25%" or "twice as many"; the paper gives 46% vs 23% (Study 1) and 48% vs 8% (Study 2). Use the paper's numbers.
- STUDY Spotlight effect (Barry Manilow T-shirt; group-discussion rankings) — original: Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000: Study 1: 15 wearers (46% estimated vs 23% observed); Study 2: 15 wearers (48% vs 8%); Study 3: 193 students in 42 groups; Studies 4–5: 44 and 30 more wearers. #### 18-B · The illusion of transparency (透明度错觉) and overestimating the harshness of others' judgments
- ★[V] Gilovich, Savitsky & Medvec 1998: "three sets of studies" — liars overestimate how detectable their lies are (Studies 1a–1c); people believe their feelings of disgust (while tasting drinks, keeping a neutral face) are more apparent than they are (Studies 2a–2b); and the illusion helps explain reluctance to intervene in emergencies (Studies 3a–3b); the authors attribute it to insufficient adjustment from the anchor of one's own experience. — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 75(2):332–346, PMID 9731312, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.2.332 (abstract opened; N and the estimated vs actual detection percentages were NOT accessible)

#### 18-C · The liking gap (喜欢差距) and conversation
- ★[V] Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark 2018, *Psychol Sci* 29(11):1742–1756, doi:10.1177/0956797618783714 (abstract opened; numbers from the accepted manuscript in the Essex repository): "people systematically underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them and enjoyed their company, an illusion we call the liking gap" — in the lab with strangers, among first-year college students getting to know dorm mates, and among members of the public at a personal-development workshop; it "persisted in conversations of varying lengths and even lasted for several months".
- ★[V] Study 1a: 36 people (72.2% female, mean age 23.25) recruited with fliers near Yale, 5-minute conversations in same-sex pairs with ice-breaker questions, $10; after one pair was excluded (close friends): actual liking of the partner M = 5.82 (95% CI 5.49–6.14) vs perceived partner's liking M = 5.17 (4.85–5.49) on 7-point scales, b = −0.65, SE 0.11, t(34) = −5.83, p < .001. Study 2: 84 Yale students/recent graduates, free-topic talk: b = −0.57 (SE 0.07), p < .001; the indirect effect through the negativity of one's post-conversation thoughts b = −0.14 (95% CI −0.22 to −0.07) (participants' thoughts about how others viewed them were more negative than their thoughts about how they viewed others). — same manuscript
- ★[V] Study 3: 102 people from the Harvard Decision Science Lab pool (students and public), conversations 2–45 min (mean 22.97, SD 14.47): b = −0.38 (SE 0.10), p < .001; the gap was significant after medium conversations (estimated difference 0.37, p < .01), NOT significant after short ones (0.39, p = .12) and only marginal after long ones (0.42, p = .056) — i.e. similar in size but with small subgroups. Study 4: UK "How to Talk to Strangers" workshops, 118 attended, 100 analysed, 5-point scale: after ≈ 5 min, participants found their partner more interesting (M = 4.30) than they thought the partner had found them (3.61), difference 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.86; before talking, the gap was already present (3.59 vs 3.22, 0.37). Study 5: 102 first-year Yale students (suitemates), five surveys over the academic year: b = −0.36 (SE 0.06); the gap was significant at times 1–4 and NOT at time 5 (p = .87); paying participants $100 for accuracy did not change estimates. — same manuscript
- ★[V] Boundary inside Study 1a: shyness moderated the gap — the shyer participants were, the larger the gap (interaction b = 0.03, p = .003); high-shyness participants showed a large gap, average-shyness participants a significant one (difference 0.55, p = .01), and low-shyness participants no significant gap (p = .21); self-esteem, rejection sensitivity and narcissism did not moderate it. Study 1b: outside video coders could see that partners signalled liking, which predicted the partners' actual liking but not the participants' perceived liking ("neglected-signal" account; 15 usable videos). — same manuscript. Treat shyness as a sample statistic (n ≈ 10 per tertile), not a diagnosis.
- [V] Compiled-sample check: Sprecher 2025 pooled several getting-acquainted studies of stranger pairs from one investigator's lab: the liking gap was "found in the compiled sample and was robust across the individual studies and different conditions"; it also examined how common a perceived gap is versus no gap or the opposite error (the share is not in the abstract). — *J Soc Psychol* 166(5):650–655, doi:10.1080/00224545.2025.2576765 (abstract opened; a search snippet says ">1,800 young adults", [~] not verified). Same-lab but a different author from the original team.
- [~] Mastroianni, Cooney, Boothby & Reece 2021, "The liking gap in groups and teams", *Organ Behav Hum Decis Process* 162:109–122, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.10.013 (abstract not retrievable; a search summary says group members also liked others more than they thought they were liked and the gap persisted somewhat over the group's tenure) — second-hand, do not cite numbers.
- [V] Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson 2021 (ADDED), "Do conversations end when people want them to?": in two studies of 932 conversations, conversants "almost never" ended when both wanted to and "rarely" when even one did; the average gap between desired and actual duration was "roughly half the duration of the conversation"; people "had little idea when their partners wanted to end". — *PNAS* 118(10), doi:10.1073/pnas.2011809118 (abstract opened). [~] News coverage (Harvard Gazette via search) reports about 2% of conversations ending when both wanted and about 30% when at least one wanted; Study 1 = 806 recalled conversations, Study 2 = 252 strangers (126 conversations), which add to 932 — consistent but second-hand; do not use the percentages without opening the paper.
- ★[V] Kardas, Kumar & Epley 2022 (a related miscalibration): conversations between strangers "felt less awkward, and created more connectedness and happiness, than the participants themselves expected" (Experiments 1a–5); deep conversations were expected to be more awkward than they were (Experiments 2–5), and participants felt MORE connected to deep than shallow partners after trying both (6a–b); expectations were more accurate with close friends/family/partners; more calibrated expectations encouraged deeper topics (7a–b). — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 122(3):367–398, doi:10.1037/pspa0000281 (abstract via OpenAlex; per-experiment N not read)
- STUDY Liking gap — original: Boothby et al. 2018 (five studies; N = 36, 84, 102, 100 analysed, 102; mean gap 0.36–0.65 scale points on 7-point scales in Studies 1a, 2, 3, 5). Later evidence: Sprecher 2025 compiled sample (gap robust across studies, one lab); Mastroianni et al. 2021 liking gap in groups (abstract not read).
  GRADE B: the underestimation of a partner's liking appears in all five original studies and a pooled later sample, but the gap is small (≈ 0.4–0.7 scale points), depends on shyness (absent for low-shy people in Study 1a), is not significant in every subgroup (short conversations) and disappeared at the last time point of the year; no pre-registered or independent cross-cultural replication found. — Boothby 2018; Sprecher 2025
- STUDY Conversations end at the wrong time (Mastroianni et al. 2021, ADDED) — original: two studies, 932 conversations (806 recalled + 126 lab pairs by my arithmetic) — average discrepancy ≈ half the conversation; Later evidence: none found.
  GRADE C: large and well-measured but a single research team, partly recalled conversations, and no independent replication found; the headline percentages were not read in the paper. — Mastroianni 2021 abstract

#### 18-D · Underestimating gratitude, kindness, compliments, and willingness to help (the "undersociality" family)
- ★[V] Kumar & Epley 2018: three experiments in which participants wrote gratitude letters, predicted how "surprised, happy, and awkward" recipients would feel, and recipients reported actual feelings; "expressers significantly underestimated how surprised recipients would be about why expressers were grateful, overestimated how awkward recipients would feel, and underestimated how positive recipients would feel"; expected awkwardness and mood correlated with willingness to express gratitude. — *Psychol Sci* 29(9):1423–1435, doi:10.1177/0956797618772506 (abstract via OpenAlex; N and d not accessible — do not state them)
- ★[V] Kumar & Epley 2023 (JEP:G 152(1):236–252, doi:10.1037/xge0001271; read in the authors' in-press PsyArXiv version): kind acts in the field and lab, "pre-registrations" at OSF. Exp 1a: 106 MBA students did 192 random acts of kindness, 66 acts with complete recipient data: givers rated their act as less "big" than recipients did (d = 1.20) and recipients felt more positive than givers expected (d = 0.83); Exp 2a (hot chocolate given in a skating-rink park; N for the exchange not read): givers' act-size rating M = 3.76 vs recipients' 7.00 on 0–10 (d = 1.16), recipients' positive feeling above the givers' expectation (M = 3.52 vs 2.73 relative to normal, d = 0.48); Exp 2b lab gift: 102 people, 51 pairs. Givers' expectations were less sensitive to the warmth of the act (Exp 3). — as stated; effect sizes are for performer-vs-recipient paired comparisons.
- ★[V] Zhao & Epley 2021 compliments: nine experiments; expressers "consistently underestimated how positive recipients would feel but overestimated how awkward recipients would feel"; the gap is driven partly by underestimating how competent (and to a lesser extent how warm) the compliment will seem; focusing on warmth increased interest in expressing compliments. — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 121(2):239–256, doi:10.1037/pspa0000277 (abstract via OpenAlex; per-experiment N not read)
- ★[V] Independent team: Boothby & Bohns 2021 (five studies): compliment givers "underestimated how positively the person receiving their compliment would feel", overestimated how bothered and uncomfortable the recipient would feel, "even in hindsight"; givers' own anxiety and concern about their competence drove the misprediction, whereas third-party forecasters were accurate; despite their anxiety, givers felt better afterwards. — *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 47(5):826–840 (online 2020), PMID 32856538, doi:10.1177/0146167220949003 (abstract via OpenAlex; N not in abstract)
- ★[V] Flynn & Lake 2008 (read from the Cornell eCommons PDF copy; the author is also cited as Lake/Bohns): Study 1: 52 Columbia students enrolled, 42 completed; participants had to get 5 strangers to fill in a 5–10 minute questionnaire; those asked to predict (the other half were not asked, to check that predicting did not alter behaviour) expected to need to approach M = 20.5 people (SD 12.3) but needed only M = 10.5 (SD 3.9), paired t(22) = 3.99, p = .001 — the prediction group (M = 10.5) did not differ from the no-prediction group (M = 11.2, SD 10.2). Study 2: 48 students: asking strangers to borrow a cell phone (3 yeses needed) predicted 10.1 vs actual 6.2 people; asking for an escort to a gym (1 yes needed) predicted 7.2 vs actual 2.3; 23 of 29 participants (79%) over-predicted. Study 3 (fundraising for a charity): predicted M = 210.3 people to approach vs actual 122.2, paired t(87) = 3.13; predicted average contribution $48.33 vs actual $63.80 (24% more). Study 4 (63 Columbia students, hypothetical scenarios): people in the potential-helper role estimated that 49.6% would comply vs 34.3% in the help-seeker role; Study 5 (83 participants: 57 students + 26 financial-services employees): same pattern for direct requests (63.8% vs 50.8%). Study 6 (79 completers of 99): help seekers attend to the cost of saying yes and underappreciate the social cost of saying no. — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 95(1):128–143, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 (abstract + full text)
- ★[V] DERIVED from Flynn & Lake means (ratio-of-means, so approximate): Study 1 implied compliance ≈ 5/20.5 = 24% predicted vs 5/10.5 = 48% observed; cell phone 3/10.1 = 30% vs 3/6.2 = 48%; escort 1/7.2 = 14% vs 1/2.3 = 43%. Say "roughly twice as many strangers agreed as asked-for-help students expected", not "x% complied".
- ★[V] Review: Bohns 2016, "(Mis)Understanding our influence over others", *Curr Dir Psychol Sci* 25(2):119–123, doi:10.1177/0963721415628011 (abstract opened; the title in the task file, "Underestimating others' willingness to help / compliance", is not the actual title): "participants in our studies have asked more than 14,000 strangers a variety of requests"; "on average, requesters underestimate compliance by 48%"; the error "is robust (it persists across various samples and requests) and substantial", is explained by requesters' "failure to appreciate the awkwardness of saying 'no'", and "some factors … strengthen, attenuate, and reverse the effect".
- [V] CORRECTION of task-file attributions (lesson 18): the compliments papers are Zhao & Epley 2021 (JPSP) and Boothby & Bohns 2021 (PSPB); Kumar & Epley 2021 (JEP:G 150(3):595–607, doi:10.1037/xge0000962) is "It's surprisingly nice to hear you" (voice vs text) — not used; the kindness paper is Kumar & Epley 2023 in JEP:G (not JPSP); the review is Bohns 2016 "(Mis)Understanding our influence over others", not "Underestimating others' willingness to help".

#### 18-E · Pluralistic ignorance, meta-accuracy and the self-other knowledge asymmetry
- ★[V] Prentice & Miller 1993: four studies; "students believed that they were more uncomfortable with campus alcohol practices than was the average student" (Study 1), also relative to their friends (Study 2); in Study 3 male students shifted their attitudes over a semester toward what they mistakenly believed was the norm while women did not; perceived deviance correlated with campus alienation (Study 4). — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 64(2):243–256, PMID 8433272, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243 (abstract opened; N and the estimate numbers not in the abstract — do not state them). Earlier concept: Miller & McFarland 1987, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 53(2):298–305, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.53.2.298 (Crossref record only).
- ★[V] Borsari & Carey 2003 meta-analysis: 23 studies, 102 tests, 53,825 participants; students systematically perceive peers as drinking more and approving of drinking more than they do themselves (self-other discrepancy, SOD); SODs were larger for injunctive (approval) norms, for women's estimates, for distal reference groups, for non-specific questions and on smaller campuses. — *J Stud Alcohol* 64(3):331–341, PMID 12817821, doi:10.15288/jsa.2003.64.331 (abstract opened)
- ★[V] Interventions: Foxcroft, Moreira, Almeida Santimano & Smith 2015 Cochrane review "Social norms information for alcohol misuse in university and college students": 70 studies (44,958 participants), 63 in meta-analyses; evidence "moderate or low quality"; at ≥ 4 months: drinking quantity SMD −0.08 (95% CI −0.12 to −0.04; 32 studies; ≈ 0.9 fewer drinks/week from a baseline of 13.7), binge drinking SMD −0.06 (−0.11 to −0.02; ≈ 2.7% fewer binge drinkers if prevalence is 43.9%), alcohol problems with individual face-to-face feedback SMD −0.14 (−0.24 to −0.04; 11 studies; ≈ 1.28 points on a 69-point scale); no effects for web or mailed feedback on problems, nor for group feedback or marketing campaigns on drinking frequency. — *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2015(12):CD006748, PMID 26711838, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD006748.pub4 (abstract opened; PubMed lists it as an update of an earlier version)
- ★[V] Kenny & DePaulo 1993: meta-accuracy ("the extent to which people know how others see them"); eight social-relations-model studies, 569 subjects; people judge how others view them "not from the feedback that they receive from others but from their own self-perceptions"; they overestimate the consistency of how different targets view them and are better at understanding how others GENERALLY view them than how they are UNIQUELY viewed by specific individuals. — *Psychol Bull* 114(1):145–161, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.114.1.145 (abstract via OpenAlex; correlations not read)
- [V] Carlson & Furr 2009: with a contextually differentiated design (parents, hometown friends, college friends) "people can indeed detect the relative impressions they make on others". — *Psychol Sci* 20(8):1033–1039, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02409.x (abstract via OpenAlex). Carlson, Vazire & Furr 2011 (3 studies): "people can make valid distinctions between how they see themselves and how others see them" — "genuine insight into their reputation" — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 101(4):831–846, doi:10.1037/a0024297 (abstract via OpenAlex).
- [V] Vazire 2010 SOKA model: 165 participants rated by themselves, 4 friends and up to 4 strangers plus behavioural criteria; the self was the best judge of neuroticism-related traits (low observability), friends the best judges of intellect-related traits (high evaluativeness), and all perspectives were about equally good for extraversion — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 98(2):281–300, doi:10.1037/a0017908 (abstract via OpenAlex). Caution: the original models test which source predicts criteria, not a general "others see you more accurately".
- STUDY Pluralistic ignorance about campus drinking norms (self–other discrepancy) — original: Prentice & Miller 1993 (four studies). Later evidence: Borsari & Carey 2003 meta-analysis (23 studies, 102 tests, 53,825 participants; SODs consistently present, moderated by norm type, gender, reference group, question specificity, campus size).
  GRADE A: a four-study original plus a large meta-analysis of independent studies show the same self-other discrepancy in perceived norms; the grade covers the DESCRIPTIVE pattern only (mostly US campuses, self-report). — Prentice & Miller 1993; Borsari & Carey 2003
- STUDY Correcting norm misperceptions changes drinking (social-norms feedback) — original: social-norms approach built on pluralistic-ignorance findings (Borsari & Carey 2003 framing). - STUDY Meta-accuracy: people know how others generally see them but little about specific others' views — original: Kenny & DePaulo 1993 (8 SRM studies, 569 subjects).   GRADE B: the broad pattern (accuracy mostly via assuming others see us as we see ourselves, with some genuine insight) is supported by several teams, but sizes differ across designs and the original numbers (correlations) were not read. — Kenny 1993; Carlson 2011

#### 18-F · Mechanism: egocentric anchoring, perspective-taking and the "different information" account
- [V] Epley, Keysar, Van Boven & Gilovich 2004: five studies — estimates of others' perceptions were consistent with one's own and differed "in a manner consistent with serial adjustment"; biases increased under time pressure and decreased with accuracy incentives; adjustments stop "once a plausible estimate is reached". — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 87(3):327–339, PMID 15382983, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.327 (abstract opened; N not in abstract)
- [V] Keysar, Barr, Balin & Brauner 2000: eye-tracking in two experiments — "people do not restrict the search for referents to mutually known objects": addressees considered objects the speaker could not see as potential referents; "people occasionally use an egocentric heuristic when they comprehend". — *Psychol Sci* 11(1):32–38, doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00211 (abstract via OpenAlex; N not in abstract)
- [V] Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng 2005 (ADDED): five experiments — people "believe that they can communicate over e-mail more effectively than they actually can"; Studies 4–5 link the overconfidence to egocentrism (the writer "hears" the intended tone). — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 89(6):925–936, PMID 16393025, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.89.6.925 (abstract opened; the sarcasm accuracy percentages are NOT in the abstract — not used)
- [V] Gilovich 1998/2000 Study 4–5 evidence for anchor-and-adjust (above) is process evidence from small samples (44 and 30); Savitsky & Gilovich 2003 reportedly found an anomaly (speakers rated themselves as MORE nervous-looking than they felt) that the anchoring account does not predict (second-hand snippet [~]).
- [def] The "different information" account (a framing, not a tested model in these sources): from the inside we have loud private evidence (our heartbeat, our slips); from the outside others have only the quiet public evidence. This is the lesson's own explanatory frame; Epley et al. 2022 propose asymmetric learning/differential construal/uncertain responsiveness as mechanisms (a review), Gilovich 2000 propose anchoring-and-adjustment (graded below).
- SEE FACTS_T1 §B1 for anchoring (explicit anchors strong; incidental anchors D; Lieder et al. 2018 resource-rational account) — do not re-grade.
- STUDY Egocentric anchoring-and-adjustment as THE mechanism of spotlight / transparency effects — original: Gilovich 2000 Studies 4–5 (n = 44 and 30; 23/30 cited a higher alternative number; 51% vs 37% after acclimation); Gilovich 1998; Epley et al. 2004 (five studies, adjustment under time pressure/incentives). Later evidence: Savitsky & Gilovich 2003 anomaly (second-hand); no study opened that pits anchoring-and-adjustment against the information-asymmetry account.
  GRADE C: the descriptive effects are better supported than this explanation: the mechanism evidence is thin (small samples, verbal self-reports of process, same research group), alternative accounts were not tested head-to-head, and the broader anchoring literature is itself mixed (FACTS_T1). — Gilovich 2000; Epley 2004
- STUDY Egocentric comprehension and communication errors — original: Keysar et al. 2000 (eye movements, 2 experiments); Kruger et al. 2005 (five experiments on e-mail tone). Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE B: two lines of experiments from different labs agree that people use their own perspective as a default, but sizes and generality (everyday speech vs lab tasks) were not checked in the full texts. — Keysar 2000; Kruger 2005

### Myths and corrections
- ★[✗] "Everyone is watching you / notices every slip" — in the T-shirt studies wearers expected about 46% of observers to identify Barry Manilow; 23% did (Study 1); for shirts they liked 48% expected vs 8% did (Study 2).
- ★[✗] "Nobody notices anything — you are invisible" — the over-correction: a quarter of observers DID identify the embarrassing shirt (23%), and in the group study people's estimates of how they ranked tracked reality (within-group correlations were high); the claim is about the size of the overestimate, not that others notice nothing.
- ★[✗] "The spotlight effect is an established, replicated 'law'" — grade B here: four same-lab shirt samples (15, 15, 44, 30 wearers), no independent re-run of the original design found, NOT tested in Many Labs 2, plus independent conceptual support in other paradigms.
- ★[✗] "People think about you as much as you think about yourself" — a slogan; the data are about estimates of observers noticing a specific feature or action, not about overall attention.
- ★[✗] "Being open about nerves always makes them obvious / never shows" — the evidence base says people usually believe more of their inner state shows than does (illusion of transparency), but the headline numbers for lying/disgust/speaking were not accessible (grade C); do not state that "anxiety is invisible".
- ★[✗] "People like you more than you think, so just talk to anyone" — an average over pairs in cooperative 5-minute-to-45-minute conversations in mostly student samples (Yale, Harvard, a UK workshop); the gap is ≈ 0.4–0.7 scale points, larger for shyer people, absent for low-shyness people in Study 1a, and gone at the last measurement of the dorm study. It is not advice and not "everyone likes you".
- [✗] "Strangers will say yes twice as often as you think" — the paper's figures are numbers of people ASKED to reach a fixed number of yeses (20.5 predicted vs 10.5 actual in Study 1); the review's "underestimate compliance by 48% on average" is an average over requests; the effect can attenuate or reverse (Bohns 2016).
- [✗] "Gratitude/kindness/compliments always land better than expected" — direction replicates (two research networks) but with small, mostly US/online samples and predicted-vs-reported feelings; no large pre-registered multi-lab test.
- [✗] "Telling people about the illusion cures speech anxiety" — only a second-hand description of a 2003 study; effect size not verified (banned).
- BETTER THAN ITS REPUTATION: the underestimation-of-compliance finding is the best-replicated item of the lesson (Flynn & Lake 2008; Bohns 2016: >14,000 strangers; Zhao & Epley 2022: six pre-registered experiments incl. a live-helping experiment with 107 pairs); and the liking gap shows up in all five original studies including a year-long dorm study.

- BET-A (spotlight): Cornell students wore a Barry Manilow T-shirt they thought embarrassing and walked into a room with several seated strangers. What share of the people in the room did the wearer expect to be able to say who was on the shirt — and what share actually could? TRUTH: expected 46% (mean), actual 23% — exactly twice (Study 1: 15 wearers, 64 observers). Tolerance: accept an estimate of 35–60% and an actual of 15–35%; the surprise is that a quarter noticed, not zero. Grade B (Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000, *JPSP* 78:211–222).
- BET-B (asking for help): Students had to collect 5 completed questionnaires from strangers on campus. Before starting, they predicted how many people they would have to ask. TRUTH: predicted about 20.5 people (SD 12.3); actually needed about 10.5 (SD 3.9). Pick: "half as many as predicted". Tolerance: accept 8–13 for the actual. Grade B (Flynn & Lake 2008, *JPSP* 95:128–143, Study 1; N = 42).
- BET-C (liking gap): After a first 5-minute conversation with a stranger, did people think their partner liked them MORE or LESS than the partner said? TRUTH: LESS (M perceived 5.17 vs actual 5.82 on a 7-point scale, N = 36 in Study 1a; b = −0.65; later studies −0.36 to −0.57). Pick only; do not ask for the number. Grade B (Boothby et al. 2018, *Psychol Sci* 29:1742–1756).
- (Spare: fundraising — students predicted their supporters would give $48.33 on average; the average actual gift was $63.80 (24% more), Flynn & Lake 2008 Study 3.)

### Boundary
(Feeds 「这个模型的边界」.)
- Samples: Cornell/Northwestern undergraduates (spotlight), Yale/Harvard students and a UK workshop (liking gap), online US adults (Epley-lab studies) — all WEIRD (lesson 22); no cross-cultural replication of the spotlight or liking-gap studies was found.
- The spotlight effect is a statement about averages and conspicuous-to-oneself features; the originators predict a reverse effect for routinised behaviour the person does not notice (smokers — a stated hypothesis) and found it shrinks with acclimation (51% → 37%). Very conspicuous traits that others genuinely notice are not tested in these sources.
- The liking gap is an average over pairs, small (≈ 0.4–0.7 on 7-point scales), larger for shy people, absent for low-shy people in Study 1a, not significant after short conversations in Study 3 and gone at the final dorm wave; liking is self-reported and measured in friendly, structured settings.
- Underestimation of compliance/kindness is about predicted vs reported feelings or compliance with low-cost asks; Bohns 2016 lists factors that attenuate or reverse it; costly requests, distrust or real danger are not what these studies tested.
- Mechanism claims (anchoring-and-adjustment; asymmetric learning) are proposals; the descriptive effects are better established than any single explanation (graded C above).
- Social anxiety is a separate clinical area (lesson 25): Brown & Stopa 2007 show the spotlight measure rises under high social evaluation; nothing here is a treatment claim.
