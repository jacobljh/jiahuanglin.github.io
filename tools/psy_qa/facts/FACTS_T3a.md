## 07 · value and emotion
### A. Value comes first (homeostasis, allostasis, interoception, core affect, somatic markers)
#### A1. Homeostasis → allostasis (the body's ledger)
- [V] ★ Cannon's definition (Cannon 1929, "Organization for physiological homeostasis", *Physiol Rev* 9:399–431, p. 400): homeostasis = "the coordinated physiological processes which maintain most of the steady states in the organism" (quotation as given in the chapter "From Homeostasis to Allodynamic Regulation", doi:10.1017/9781107415782.018). The 1932 book *The Wisdom of the Body* popularised the term; Cannon was inspired by Claude Bernard's "milieu intérieur" (constancy of the internal environment as the condition of free, independent life). 稳态 / 内环境
- [~] ADDED: Historical account of Bernard → Cannon: Cooper 2008, *Appetite* 51(3):419–427, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2008.06.005 (abstract not retrievable; not opened). A 2024 *Philosophy of Science* paper (doi:10.1017/psa.2024.72) argues that Bernard's and Cannon's own homeostasis was broader than the later cybernetic "set-point" reading, i.e. critics of homeostasis (heterostasis, rheostasis, allostasis) often attack a narrower version.
- [V] ★ Sterling 2012 ("Allostasis: a model of predictive regulation", *Physiol Behav* 106(1):5–15, doi:10.1016/j.physbeh.2011.06.004): the standard model's premise — regulation = preserving constancy by error-correcting feedback — "is flawed"; allostasis = efficient regulation by anticipating needs before they arise, which requires a dedicated organ (the brain) that tracks many variables, predicts needs and sets priorities; appetites funnel into a "stick" (broadly anxiety, non-adapting) and a "carrot" (broadly pleasure, brief and fast-adapting). 异稳态 — PMID 21684297
- [~] Term coined by Sterling & Eyer 1988 ("Allostasis: a new paradigm to explain arousal pathology", in Fisher & Reason (eds.), *Handbook of Life Stress, Cognition and Health*, Wiley, pp. 629–649, as cited by Sterling 2012). Whether allostasis is a new principle or an extension of homeostasis is disputed among physiologists (see the 2024 paper above).
- [V] McEwen 1998 ("Protective and damaging effects of stress mediators", *NEJM* 338(3):171–179, doi:10.1056/NEJM199801153380307): defines allostasis as "the ability to achieve stability through change" and allostatic load as the long-term cost of the physiological stress response. 异稳态负荷
- STUDY allostasis/allostatic load as the organising idea of bodily regulation — original: Sterling & Eyer 1988; McEwen 1998 (review). Later evidence: broad support for anticipatory regulation in physiology, but "allostatic load" as a single summed biomarker score is a measurement construct with mixed predictive value (not researched in detail here).
  GRADE: no letter grade (conceptual framework, not a single finding); [def]-level.

#### A2. Interoception (what is established vs what is theory)
- [def] Interoception = sensing, central processing and mental representation of signals from inside the body (heartbeat, breathing, gut, temperature, pain/itch, "visceral" state). 内感受 — Critchley & Garfinkel 2017
- [V] ★ ESTABLISHED (anatomy/physiology): Craig 2002 (*Nat Rev Neurosci* 3(8):655–666, doi:10.1038/nrn894): an afferent system in primates and humans represents all aspects of the physiological condition of the body ("the material me"); proposed as a foundation for subjective feelings, emotion and self-awareness (the last part is a proposal). — PMID 12154366
- [V] ADDED: ESTABLISHED-ISH (network): Kleckner et al. 2017 (*Nat Hum Behav* 1:0069, doi:10.1038/s41562-017-0069): macaque tract-tracing + two resting fMRI samples (N = 280, N = 270) identify an intrinsic allostatic/interoceptive brain system; in a third sample (N = 41) stronger hub connectivity went with better performance on an implicit index of interoceptive ability tied to autonomic fluctuations.
- [V] Critchley & Garfinkel 2017 ("Interoception and emotion", *Curr Opin Psychol* 17:7–14, doi:10.1016/j.copsyc.2017.04.020): interoception has dissociable psychological dimensions (accuracy vs belief vs awareness); short-term cardiac effects on neural/mental processes incl. fear-specific effects; predictive-coding models of interoception explain emotion and selfhood.
- [V] ★ THEORY (proposal, evidence indirect): Barrett & Simmons 2015 ("Interoceptive predictions in the brain", *Nat Rev Neurosci* 16(7):419–429, doi:10.1038/nrn3950) — an Opinion article proposing the EPIC model: interoceptive experience may largely reflect limbic predictions about the expected state of the body, constrained by ascending visceral sensations. Seth 2013 ("Interoceptive inference, emotion, and the embodied self", *Trends Cogn Sci* 17(11):565–573, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2013.09.007): emotions as actively inferred generative models of the causes of interoceptive signals. Both are theoretical frameworks, not settled findings. 预测性内感受
- [~] ADDED: Measurement caveat: heartbeat-counting scores (the usual "interoceptive accuracy" measure) are disputed — Zamariola et al. 2018 (*Biol Psychol* 137:12–17) listed four problems; Zimprich et al. 2020 (*Biol Psychol* 152:107868) replied they are statistical artefacts of ratio scores and "not as serious". Teach interoceptive-accuracy individual-difference claims cautiously.
- [~] Panksepp 2011 (*Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 35:1791–1804, doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2011.08.003): "primal affects are intrinsic brain value systems that unconditionally and automatically inform animals how they are faring in survival" (abstract wording, paraphrased) — the affective-neuroscience version of the "value comes first" story.

#### A3. Core affect
- [~] Russell 1980 ("A circumplex model of affect", *JPSP* 39:1161–1178, doi:10.1037/h0077714): self-reported affect words fall around a circle defined by two bipolar dimensions, pleasure–displeasure (valence) and arousal–sleepiness. 环状模型 (abstract not accessible; standard description). ★
- [V] Russell 2003 ("Core affect and the psychological construction of emotion", *Psychol Rev* 110(1):145–172, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145): states "experienced as simply feeling good or bad, energized or enervated" are core affect; they influence reflexes, perception, cognition and behaviour, people have no direct access to their causes; core affect can be free-floating (mood) or attributed to a cause, which begins an emotional episode. 核心情感 ★
- [~] Barrett & Bliss-Moreau 2009 ("Affect as a psychological primitive", *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 41:167–218): argues valence and arousal are properties of consciousness/basic ingredients of mind rather than emotion-specific; abstract not retrieved.

#### A4. Somatic markers and the Iowa Gambling Task
- [V] ★ Bechara, Damasio, Damasio & Anderson 1994 (*Cognition* 50:7–15) introduced the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT); Bechara, Damasio, Tranel & Damasio 1997 (*Science* 275(5304):1293–1295, doi:10.1126/science.275.5304.1293): normal participants began to choose advantageously before they could state which strategy worked, and generated anticipatory skin-conductance responses before risky choices; prefrontal (VMPFC) patients kept choosing disadvantageously even after they knew the correct strategy and never generated anticipatory SCRs. Authors' reading: nonconscious biases guide behaviour before conscious knowledge does. 躯体标记假说 (Damasio 1994, *Descartes' Error*)
- [V] ★ Critique 1: Maia & McClelland 2004 (*PNAS* 101(45):16075–16080, doi:10.1073/pnas.0406666101): with more sensitive questionnaires participants had much more reportable knowledge than Bechara assumed — they report the advantageous strategy more reliably than they behave advantageously, and when they behave advantageously their reports show quantitative knowledge of deck outcomes — so "no need to appeal to nonconscious somatic markers".
- [V] Critique 2: Dunn, Dalgleish & Lawrence 2006 (*Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 30(2):239–271, doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2005.07.001): the IGT findings are undermined by the cognitive penetrability of the reward/punishment schedule, ambiguity about the SCR data and lack of causal evidence that peripheral feedback drives performance; parsimonious alternatives exist; the SMH "requires additional empirical support to remain tenable".
- [V] ADDED: Critique 3: Steingroever et al. 2013 (*Psychol Assess* 25(1):180–193, doi:10.1037/a0029929): across literature reviews and 8 data sets, healthy participants often prefer decks with infrequent losses (not the "good" decks), show idiosyncratic choice patterns and do not show the assumed explore-then-exploit pattern — questioning the IGT's standard interpretation.
- STUDY Somatic-marker hypothesis / IGT — original: Bechara 1994/1997 (small lesion + control groups; N not verified here). Later evidence: Maia & McClelland 2004; Dunn et al. 2006; Steingroever et al. 2013.
  GRADE C: VMPFC damage impairs real-life and IGT decisions (a lesion finding), but "nonconscious bodily markers guide choice" has strong alternative explanations and the task's own validity is doubtful. — Maia & McClelland 2004; Dunn 2006; Steingroever 2013

### B. The four-generation story (James-Lange → Cannon-Bard → Schachter-Singer → Dutton-Aron), facial feedback, power poses
#### B1. James–Lange and Cannon–Bard (the first two generations)
- [V] ★ James 1884 "What is an emotion?", *Mind* 9(34):188–205. Thesis (pp. 189–190): "the bodily changes follow directly the PERCEPTION of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion"; also p. 190: "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble". (Quoted exactly from the text at psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm; S&S 1962 p. 379 quote the same thesis from James 1890 *Principles* p. 449.) 詹姆斯-兰格理论
- [~] Lange 1885 (Danish monograph *Om Sindsbevægelser*) gave a similar vascular/visceral account, hence "James–Lange" — date/title from standard histories; not opened. 兰格
- [~] Cannon 1927 (*Am J Psychol* 39:106–124, "The James–Lange theory of emotions: a critical examination and an alternative theory") and Bard 1928 (*Am J Physiol* 84:490–515) → 坎农-巴德丘脑理论. Five standard objections per secondary summaries: (1) cutting viscera off from the CNS does not abolish emotional behaviour; (2) the same visceral changes occur in different emotions and in non-emotional states (fever, cold); (3) viscera are relatively insensitive; (4) visceral changes are too slow; (5) artificially induced visceral change (adrenaline) does not produce true emotion. Objection (2) is verified in S&S 1962 p. 379, which quotes Cannon 1929 p. 351: "the same visceral changes occur in very different emotional states and in non-emotional states".
- [V] ADDED: Marañón 1924 (cited in S&S 1962 p. 380): adrenaline injected into 210 patients; 71% reported only bodily symptoms with no emotional overtones, 29% responded in an apparently emotional way, most of them "as if" (cold) emotions ("I feel as if I were afraid"); very few reported genuine emotion, and in those cases Marañón had supplied an emotional memory/cue. This is the empirical seed of S&S's design. — Schachter & Singer 1962, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046234

#### B2. Schachter & Singer 1962 (read in full: scanned copy of the paper, *Psychological Review* 69(5):379–399, doi:10.1037/h0046234)
- [V] ★ Cover story: a study of how the vitamin compound "Suproxin" affects vision; every participant consented to an injection. Epinephrine groups got ½ cc of a 1:1000 solution of epinephrine bitartrate (Winthrop's "Suprarenin") subcutaneously, i.e. 0.5 mg (my arithmetic from the stated 1:1000 dilution; the paper gives only the dilution); placebo group ½ cc saline. Expected symptoms: palpitation, tremor, flushing, faster breathing, starting ~3–5 min after injection and lasting 10 min to an hour.
- [V] ★ Participants: all male introductory-psychology students at the University of Minnesota (volunteers for extra exam credit). 185 approached, "all but 1" agreed to the injection (so the textbook "184" = 185 − 1; my inference); 11 discarded as too suspicious; Table 1 totals N = 174 (105 euphoria + 69 anger); 5 more (4 euphoria, 1 anger) excluded because epinephrine produced no symptoms → analysed N = 169 (101 euphoria: 25/25/25/26; 68 anger: 22/23/23). The paper's own counts are slightly inconsistent (185 − 1 − 11 = 173, Table 1 sums to 174) — do not quote a sample size more precisely than "about 170–185".
- [V] ★ Seven cells: Euphoria × {Epi Informed, Epi Ignorant, Epi Misinformed, Placebo}; Anger × {Epi Informed, Epi Ignorant, Placebo}. Informed = told the true effects (hands shake, heart pounds, face warm/flushed); Ignorant = told the injection was mild with no side effects; Misinformed = told false effects (numb feet, itching, headache); Placebo = saline + the Ignorant script. Epi Mis was not run in the anger series (it was conceived as a control for the Informed instructions).
- [V] Social manipulation: the "other participant" was a confederate ("stooge"). Euphoria = a 15-step routine in a deliberately messy room (doodling a fish, paper basketball, paper airplanes, slingshot, hula hoops). Anger = a five-page questionnaire growing insulting ("How many times each week do you have sexual intercourse?"; "With how many men (other than your father) has your mother had extramarital relationships?"), the confederate ends by tearing it up and stamping out.
- [V] Measures: (a) observer behind a one-way mirror (euphoria: activity index weighting hula-hooping 5 … doodling 1, plus number of self-initiated acts; anger: agree +2 / disagree −2 / ignore −1 etc., summed); coders agreed completely on 71% of units and differed by ≤1 point on 88%. (b) self-report scales: irritated/angry 0–4 and happy/good 0–4; index = happiness minus irritation. The authors say anger self-reports are weak because students would not admit irritation to the experimenter (p. 391).
- [V] Manipulation check (Table 1): pulse rose in every epinephrine cell and fell in placebo: anger Epi Inf 85.9 → 92.4, Epi Ign 85.0 → 96.8, Placebo 84.5 → 79.6; euphoria Epi Inf 85.7 → 88.6, Epi Ign 84.6 → 85.6, Epi Mis 82.9 → 86.0, Placebo 80.4 → 77.1 (bpm). Self-rated palpitation/tremor higher than placebo in all epinephrine cells (p < .001). All p-values in the paper are two-tailed.
- [V] ★ Euphoria, self-report (Table 2, mean of happiness − irritation): Epi Inf 0.98 (n = 25) · Epi Ign 1.78 (25) · Epi Mis 1.90 (25) · Placebo 1.61 (26). Epi Inf vs Epi Mis p < .01; Epi Inf vs Epi Ign p = .02; Placebo vs any epinephrine cell: ns.
- [V] ★ Euphoria, behaviour (Table 3): activity index Epi Inf 12.72 · Epi Ign 18.28 · Epi Mis 22.56 · Placebo 16.00; acts initiated 0.20 · 0.56 · 0.84 · 0.54. Epi Inf vs Epi Mis p = .05 (activity) / .03 (initiated); Epi Inf vs Epi Ign ns / .08; Placebo vs the three epinephrine cells: ns / ns.
- [V] ★ Anger, self-report (Table 4): Epi Inf 1.91 (n = 22) · Epi Ign 1.39 (23) · Placebo 1.63 (23); Inf vs Ign p = .08; placebo comparisons ns.
- [V] ★ Anger, behaviour (Table 5, anger index): Epi Inf −0.18 · Epi Ign +2.28 · Placebo +0.79; Inf vs Ign p < .01; Ign vs Placebo p < .05; Placebo vs Inf ns. This is the ONLY epinephrine-vs-placebo contrast in the main tables that reaches p < .05 without post-hoc exclusions.
- [V] ★ The placebo anomaly, in the authors' own words (p. 393): placebo subjects fall "between" Epi Ign and Epi Inf — "a particularly troubling pattern ... raises serious questions about our entire theoretical structure" (quoted in part). Placebo-saline subjects also showed arousal-like variation (some pulses rose), so the "no arousal" control was not clean.
- [V] Post-hoc rescue: after removing "self-informed" subjects (those whose open-ended answers attributed their state to the injection): Epi Ign euphoria 8 of 25 self-informed (activity 11.63 vs 21.14 for the other 17; p = .05); Epi Mis 5 of 25 (12.40 vs 25.10; p = .10); anger Epi Ign 3 of 23 (−1.67 vs +2.88 for the other 20; p = .05); with them removed, Epi Ign + Epi Mis vs Placebo in euphoria p = .03 and Epi Ign vs Placebo in anger p = .01. A second internal analysis split placebo subjects by whether pulse rose/stayed (euphoria activity 23.17, n = 12 vs 10.67, n = 14, p = .02; anger +1.69, n = 8 vs +0.15, n = 13, p = .01). The authors concede these internal analyses make their conclusions "somewhat tentative" (p. 396).
- [V] What the data DO support (as S&S frame it): appropriate explanation matters — informed subjects were least affected (Epi Inf lowest euphoria/anger; Epi Mis/Epi Ign highest). Epi Inf vs Epi Mis is the cleanest contrast. They did NOT show that epinephrine alone raises emotion relative to placebo in the euphoria arm (all ns).
- [V] Reisenzein's 1983 summary of the whole literature (abstract, *Psychol Bull* 94(2):239–264, PMID 6356197): of three deductions from the theory, only one — that misattributed arousal from an extraneous source intensifies emotional reactions — is adequately supported; little support for "arousal reduction reduces emotion"; "equivocal" for "misattributing emotional arousal to a neutral source reduces emotion"; "no convincing evidence" that arousal is necessary for emotion nor that emotion can result from labelling unexplained arousal; at best "a rather attenuated version" survives. ★ — https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.94.2.239 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [~] Marshall & Zimbardo 1979 (*JPSP* 37:970–988, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.37.6.970) tried to reproduce the euphoria arm; per secondary summaries the euphoric confederate had little effect and epinephrine subjects were not more susceptible than placebo subjects. Maslach 1979 (*JPSP* 37:953–969) used hypnotically induced arousal instead of a drug: unexplained arousal produced negative states whatever the confederate did. Abstracts not accessible (paywalled); details below in COULD NOT VERIFY.
- [V] ADDED: Mezzacappa et al. 1999 (*Cognition & Emotion* 13:181–199, doi:10.1080/026999399379320): 26 male students, titrated epinephrine vs saline, misinformed about symptoms, six film clips. Epinephrine subjects showed more physiological arousal and more intense emotion (self-report and facial) than controls; more expressed fear to fear films; but NO difference in expressed anger (anger films) or amusement (amusement films); non-valenced arousal rated more intense. A modern, careful test: epinephrine intensifies, it does not by itself select the label.
- [V] ADDED: Reisenzein 2017, *Emotion Review* 9(1):17–26 (doi:10.1177/1754073916639665): "arousal may in fact not be necessary for emotions"; the revised family of "cognition–feeling" theories replaces arousal with intrinsically affective feelings.
- STUDY Schachter & Singer 1962 (the epinephrine experiment) — original: ≈170 male students, 7 cells, 1 injection of 0.5 mg epinephrine or saline; main effects rest on Epi Inf vs Epi Mis/Ign contrasts, with epinephrine-vs-placebo ns in 5 of 6 comparisons and rescued post hoc. Later evidence: failed/mixed replications (Marshall & Zimbardo 1979, Maslach 1979), Reisenzein 1983 review (no convincing evidence for "labelling of unexplained arousal"), Mezzacappa 1999 (intensity yes, label no).
  GRADE D (as a demonstration of "arousal + context label = emotion"): never cleanly replicated, authors' own placebo problem, post-hoc exclusions; teach as the textbook story that is much weaker than its fame. — S&S 1962; Reisenzein 1983
- STUDY the general idea "residual/misattributed arousal can intensify a later emotional judgement (excitation transfer)" — original: Zillmann 1971 (*J Exp Soc Psychol* 7:419–434, doi:10.1016/0022-1031(71)90075-8) and Zillmann, Katcher & Milavsky 1972 (*JESP* 8:247–259). Later evidence: Reisenzein 1983 (the one deduction judged adequately supported); Foster, Witcher, Campbell & Green 1998 (*JPSP* 74:86–101, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.74.1.86): meta-analysis + review + experiment — arousal affects attraction even when the source is fairly unambiguous, more strongly when ambiguous; they propose an automatic effect that people can correct when the source is obvious (judgement-and-adjustment, cognitive busyness blocks correction).
  GRADE B: direction reliable across labs, size and conditions vary; "mislabelling" is only one of several accounts (Allen et al. 1989: response facilitation). — Foster et al. 1998; Allen et al. 1989

#### B3. Dutton & Aron 1974 (the suspension-bridge study)
Caveat on sourcing: I could not open the 1974 PDF (paywalled). Numbers below are the ones reproduced and QUOTED (with page numbers) in the critical reanalysis by Szczucka 2012, *Polish Psychological Bulletin* 43(3):191–198, doi:10.2478/v10059-012-0021-6 (which I read in full), cross-checked against several secondary descriptions that agree on the headline counts. Treat as [~] until checked against the original: Dutton & Aron 1974, *JPSP* 30(4):510–517, doi:10.1037/h0037031.
- [~] ★ Field study 1 design: male passers-by, 18–35, unaccompanied, were approached on either the Capilano Canyon Suspension Bridge (North Vancouver; as quoted: 5 ft wide, 450 ft long, 230 ft above the river) or a low, solid wooden bridge (about 10 ft high) by an attractive female interviewer (or, in a comparison arm, a male interviewer). Each completed a questionnaire including a TAT picture whose story was scored by judges for sexual imagery (1–5); then the interviewer wrote down her name and phone number and, as Szczucka quotes the original (p. 512), invited each man to call "if he wanted to talk further". — Dutton & Aron 1974 via Szczucka 2012
- [~] ★ Female-interviewer results (as reported): TAT sexual-imagery mean 2.47 (suspension, n = 20) vs 1.41 (control, n = 18), t(36) = 3.19, p < .01. Took the phone number: 18 of 23 vs 16 of 22 (reanalysis: ns, p > .6). Called the interviewer: 9 of 18 (50%) vs 2 of 16 (12.5%) (Szczucka's chi-square 5.44, p < .02, odds ratio about 7). Male-interviewer arm: no difference in sexual imagery (t = 0.36, ns) and tiny call counts (2 of 7 vs 1 of 6, reanalysis). The female-interviewer approach counts: 66 men accosted, 45 agreed to complete the questionnaire (23/33 and 22/33), 7 questionnaires unusable.
- [~] ★ Manipulation check was only a separate pre-test: 15 men on each bridge rated how fearful the average person would be (79 vs 18 on a 0–100 scale) and how fearful they themselves were (65 vs 3). No arousal was measured in the main sample; participants were not randomly assigned (self-selected into crossing the bridges), so the field study is quasi-experimental. — Szczucka 2012 quoting the original, p. 512
- [~] Field study 2 (only a female interviewer; all participants were men who had crossed the Capilano bridge; the control group was approached at least 10 min after crossing, the experimental group after an unspecified, shorter time — Szczucka notes the timing is not reported): sexual-imagery means as quoted 2.99 (experimental) vs 1.92 (control); took the number 20 of 25 vs 19 of 23; calls "13 out of 20" vs "7 out of 23" (chi-square 5.89, p < .02). Szczucka argues the control denominator should be 7 of 19 (those who took the number), which gives p = .08 (ns); and that the proportion taking the number (20/25 vs 19/23 in the original; 80% vs 76% reanalysed) did not differ. Single-critic reanalysis, so [~]. — Szczucka 2012
- [~] Laboratory study 3 (anticipated electric shock, strong vs weak; female confederate present or absent; the paper says 80 participants but Szczucka counts 5 cells × n = 20 = 100): anxiety higher under strong-shock expectation; attraction to the female confederate depended on the shock level (main effect p < .001 per the reanalysis) but the TAT result was an interaction only (F = 4.73, p < .05; main effect p = .07). Authors' claim "strong emotion per se increases sexual attraction" is broader than the design.
- [V] ADDED: Kenrick, Cialdini & Linder 1979 (*PSPB* 5(3):329–334, doi:10.1177/014616727900500312; I read the paper): four LABORATORY studies (male students, n = 16–38 per cell; Exp. 3 an "exact replication" attempt of D&A's lab study) — high expected shock produced no more liking/desire to date/desire to kiss the confederate (e.g. Exp. 3 liking 3.2 high vs 3.5 low fear; desire to date 3.4 vs 3.2), and participants attributed their excitement to the shock, not to the confederate. They argue for negative reinforcement (relief) and say misattribution was the textbook explanation with "little direct evidence". They did not retest the bridge itself.
- [V] ADDED: Allen, Kenrick, Linder & McCall 1989 (*JPSP* 57:261–270, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.57.2.261): Study 1 (D&A-style fear arousal) found enhanced attraction; directing attention to the true arousal source did not remove it; Study 2 (exercise) too — arousal facilitated sexual attraction even when the source was salient. Best explained by simple response facilitation, not mislabelling.
- [V] Meston & Frohlich 2003 (*Arch Sex Behav* 32(6):537–544, doi:10.1023/A:1026037527455, PMID 14574097): amusement-park field study, N ≈ 300 (165 men, 135 women) approached before or just after a roller-coaster ride and rated a photo of an average-attractive opposite-sex person; for people riding with a NON-romantic partner, ratings of attractiveness/dating desirability were higher after the ride; no before/after difference for those with a romantic partner (partner salience moderates). Direction supports excitation transfer; effect small, one study.
- [~] White, Fishbein & Rutstein 1981 (*JPSP* 41:56–62; exercise before rating a confederate) is the usual "extension"; its abstract is not accessible — details in COULD NOT VERIFY.
- STUDY Dutton & Aron 1974 bridge — original: field study 1 (about 38 usable questionnaires; calls 9/18 vs 2/16) + 2 + lab study. Later evidence: no independent close replication of the bridge design found; Kenrick et al. 1979 lab failures; Allen et al. 1989 conceptual replication with a response-facilitation reading; Meston & Frohlich 2003 field study (N ≈ 300) partly consistent; Foster et al. 1998 meta-analysis supports the general arousal→attraction effect; Szczucka 2012 reanalysis: effects on taking the phone number and (study 2) calling are not robust.
  GRADE C: pretty story, tiny cells, quasi-experimental, no arousal measure, no direct replication; the broader "arousal can boost attraction" is B (Foster 1998) but "fear is mislabelled as love" is only one of several explanations. — Szczucka 2012; Kenrick 1979; Allen 1989; Foster 1998

#### B4. Facial feedback
- [V] Strack, Martin & Stepper 1988 (*JPSP* 54(5):768–777, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.54.5.768): pen held in teeth ("smile") vs lips ("pout") while rating cartoon funniness; cartoons rated funnier under teeth. The Registered Replication Report states the original difference was 0.82 units on a 10-point scale. ★ — abstract via OpenAlex; RRR abstract
- [V] ★ Wagenmakers et al. 2016 RRR (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(6):917–928, doi:10.1177/1745691616674458): 17 independent labs ran the same vetted protocol (total N ≈ 1,900 — not verified from the abstract); meta-analytic smile−pout rating difference 0.03 units, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.16 (vs original 0.82). Pen-in-mouth cartoon effect not reproduced.
- [V] ★ ADDED: Coles, Larsen & Lench 2019 (*Psychol Bull* 145(6):610–651, doi:10.1037/bul0000194, PMID 30973236): meta-analysis of 286 effect sizes from 138 studies manipulating facial feedback and measuring emotion self-report: overall effect "significant but small", heterogeneous; larger for affective judgments (with publication-bias evidence) than for felt emotion (no bias evidence by 3 methods). (A thesis version gave d ≈ .20; the published point estimate not verified here.)
- [V] ★ Coles et al. 2022, Many Smiles Collaboration (*Nat Hum Behav* 6:1731–1742, doi:10.1038/s41562-022-01458-9; I read the accepted manuscript): preregistered adversarial collaboration; 3,878 participants collected (26 research groups, 19 countries; mean age 26.6); primary analyses on 1,504 after exclusions (46% were excluded for showing awareness of the hypothesis, 17% failed an attention check). Happiness (scale of about 6 points) after posing happy vs neutral: Mdiff = 0.31, 95% CI [0.21, 0.40] (5.17% of the scale range; BF10 ≈ 61–103). By task: facial mimicry 0.49 [0.36, 0.61] (8.2% of range); voluntary facial action 0.40 [0.23, 0.56] (6.7%); pen-in-mouth 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15] (0.7%; BF10 0.11–0.17 = moderate support for the null). Pose effect was larger among participants more aware of the hypothesis (β = 0.08), so demand characteristics cannot be excluded.
- STUDY Facial feedback (pen-in-mouth version) — original: Strack 1988, N = 92 (not verified here), 0.82-point difference. Later evidence: RRR 2016, 17 labs, difference 0.03 [−0.11, 0.16]; Many Smiles 2022 pen-in-mouth Mdiff 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15].
  GRADE D for the famous pen-in-mouth cartoon result. — Wagenmakers 2016; Coles 2022
- STUDY Facial feedback (general: posing/mimicking a smile nudges reported happiness slightly) — original: many studies; Later evidence: Coles 2019 meta-analysis (small, heterogeneous); Many Smiles 2022 (mimicry/voluntary action +0.4–0.5 points on a ~6-point scale, i.e. 7–8% of the range, with awareness moderating).
  GRADE B: small positive effect for overt/voluntary smiling, big boundary conditions (pen task fails; demand effects plausible); never a "make yourself happy" technique. — Coles 2019; Coles 2022

#### B5. Power poses
- [V] ★ Carney, Cuddy & Yap 2010 (*Psychol Sci* 21(10):1363–1368, doi:10.1177/0956797610383437): high-power vs low-power poses (two poses, 1 min each) → higher testosterone, lower cortisol, more felt power and risk tolerance. Final N = 42 after 5 exclusions (Carney 2016 statement). Carney's later statement lists problems: tiny sample, data checked in chunks (about 25, then 10, 7, 5 participants), risk-taking chi-square reported with the likelihood-ratio p (.05) rather than Pearson (.052), outlier handling, experimenters aware of the hypothesis, winning-confounds for testosterone, p-hacked self-report items.
- [V] ★ Ranehill et al. 2015 (*Psychol Sci* 26(5):653–656, doi:10.1177/0956797614553946; I read the paper): N = 200 (98 women, 102 men), experimenter blind to condition, power > 95% for the original effect size; poses held 3 min instead of 1 min each. Result: significant effect on self-reported feelings of power, NO significant effect on testosterone, cortisol or any of three behavioural tasks (gain-domain risk, loss-domain risk, willingness to compete).
- [V] ★ Carney's own 2016 statement ("My position on 'Power Poses'", faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney): "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real." Also: "The data are real" but "flimsy"; she no longer studies or teaches power poses.
- [V] ADDED: Simmons & Simonsohn 2017 (*Psychol Sci* 28(5):687–693, doi:10.1177/0956797616658563): p-curve of the 33 studies Carney et al. 2015 cited — p-value distribution "indistinguishable" from zero true effect plus selective reporting.
- [V] ADDED: Gronau et al. 2017 (*Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology*, doi:10.1080/23743603.2017.1326760): Bayesian meta-analysis of six preregistered studies on FELT power: very strong evidence for an effect on felt power, but only moderate when restricted to participants unfamiliar with the effect.
- [V] ADDED: Elkjær et al. 2022 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 17(1):276–304, doi:10.1177/1745691620919358): meta-analysis, 73 studies; robust affective and behavioural differences between expansive and contractive displays, driven by the ABSENCE of contractive postures (contractive vs neutral g = 0.45) rather than expansive postures (expansive vs neutral g = 0.06).
- STUDY Power posing (hormones and risk-taking) — original: Carney 2010, N = 42. Later evidence: Ranehill 2015 (N = 200, null on hormones and behaviour), Simmons & Simonsohn 2017 p-curve (no evidential value), Carney's retraction-style statement 2016.
  GRADE D for testosterone/cortisol/risk-taking. For FELT power (self-report): B/C — replicated in Ranehill and in Gronau's six preregistered studies but plausibly demand-driven (moderate only for naïve participants), and Elkjær shows it is slumping that lowers mood, not expanding that lifts it. — Ranehill 2015; Gronau 2017; Elkjær 2022
### C. The debate: basic vs constructed emotion
#### C1. The basic-emotion (BET) case
- [V] ★ Ekman & Friesen 1971 ("Constants across cultures in the face and emotion", *JPSP* 17(2):124–129, doi:10.1037/h0030377; I read the scanned paper): Fore of the South East Highlands of New Guinea, screened for minimal contact (no movies, no English/Pidgin, never worked for a Caucasian); 189 adults + 130 children (≈3% of the culture). Task: a story, then 3 photographs (2 for children), pick the face that fits the story. Adults, % choosing the intended face (chance ≈ 33%): happiness 86–100; anger 82–87; sadness 69–87; disgust 77–80; surprise 65–71; fear 64–87 — BUT fear vs surprise failed: fear chosen over surprise only 48%, 52% and 28% (the last significantly BELOW chance; surprise chosen 67%). Children (chance 50%): 76–100%. Authors: fear–surprise confusion fits a culture where frightening events are almost always also surprising.
- [V] Methodological debate in the pair of 1994 *Psychol Bull* papers: Russell 1994 (doi:10.1037/0033-2909.115.1.102) — forced-choice format, within-subject design, preselected posed photographs are each problematic; "when they are altered, less supportive or nonsupportive results occur"; association "may vary with culture and is loose". Ekman 1994 (doi:10.1037/0033-2909.115.2.268) — Russell "misrepresents what universality means"; new data answer the forced-choice worry; evidence "overwhelming in support of universals" (abstract paraphrase).
- [V] ★ Elfenbein & Ambady 2002 (*Psychol Bull* 128(2):203–235, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.2.203) meta-analysis: emotions were recognised across cultures at better-than-chance levels; accuracy higher when expresser and perceiver share a national/ethnic/regional group (in-group advantage), smaller with more cross-group contact; majority members were worse at judging minority members than the reverse. As described by Barrett et al. 2019: 87 experiments, >22,000 participants from >20 cultures, 95% used posed faces, nearly all used choice-from-array. (Some summaries say 97 studies — the abstract gives no count; do not quote a number.) Matsumoto 2002 argued the in-group-advantage conclusion is unwarranted.
- [V] Sauter, Eisner, Ekman & Scott 2010 (*PNAS* 107(6):2408–2412, doi:10.1073/pnas.0908239106): Himba of Namibia vs UK/Western listeners; vocalizations of the "basic" emotions (anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, surprise) were recognised in both directions; additional emotions (e.g. most positive ones) only within culture.
- [V] ★ Cowen & Keltner 2017 (*PNAS* 114(38):E7900–E7909, doi:10.1073/pnas.1702247114): 2,185 short videos; self-reports reliably captured 27 distinct categories of experience; categorical labels (e.g. amusement) captured reports better than valence/arousal dimensions, but boundaries between categories are "fuzzy rather than discrete" (gradients, e.g. anxiety → fear → horror → disgust). (The PubMed DOI field in some tools is wrong; DOI above is the PNAS one.)
- [V] Cowen, Laukka, Elfenbein, Liu & Keltner 2019 (*Nat Hum Behav* 3(4):369–382, doi:10.1038/s41562-019-0533-6): US and Indian raters, 2,519 speech samples from 100 actors in 5 cultures: at least 12 distinct emotions preserved across the 2 cultures; categories drive recognition more than valence; categories bridged by gradients.
- [V] ★ Cowen, Keltner, Schroff, Jou, Adam & Prasad 2021 (*Nature* 589(7841):251–257, doi:10.1038/s41586-020-3037-7): deep-net analysis of 6 million videos from 144 countries: 16 facial expressions each had distinct associations with a set of contexts (weddings, sports...) that were 70% preserved across 12 world regions; regions varied in how often each expression occurred.
- [V] Cordaro, Sun, Keltner, Kamble, Huddar & McNeil 2018 (*Emotion* 18(1):75–93, doi:10.1037/emo0000302): >2,600 free-response facial/bodily displays of 22 emotions in China, India, Japan, Korea, US (FACS-coded): cross-cultural "core patterns" per emotion plus systematic cultural variants; a gradient of universality across the 22 emotions.
- [~] Panksepp's affective neuroscience: mammals share subcortical primary-process emotional systems (listed in Panksepp 1998 as SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, PLAY; capitalised names are his convention). Panksepp 2007 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 2(3):281–296, doi:10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00045.x) and 2011 (above) argue these ancient systems are the "primary-process" source of affect. Panksepp & Burgdorf 2003 (*Physiol Behav* 79(3):533–547, doi:10.1016/s0031-9384(03)00159-8): ≈50-kHz ultrasonic chirps in rats during play/tickling resemble primitive laughter; "a dozen reasons" for the working hypothesis that they index positive affect with evolutionary relation to human joy — a hypothesis, not a settled finding.
- [V] ADDED: Ekman 2016 survey ("What scientists who study emotion agree about", *Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(1):31–34, doi:10.1177/1745691615596992; I read it): emailed to 248 scientists (selection vetted by J. A. Russell), 60% responded. Endorsed: compelling evidence for universals in some aspect of emotion 88%; universal signals (face or voice) 80%; universal triggering events 66%, physiology 51%, appraisal mechanisms 44%. Orientation: 49% "discrete emotions combining biological and social influences", 11% "emotions as constructed", 30% both. "What is most basic": 18% dimensions, 16% discrete packages, 55% both. Emotions judged empirically established (among the 74% asked): anger 91%, fear 90%, disgust 86%, sadness 80%, happiness 76%; shame, surprise, embarrassment 40–50%. Caveats: designed by Ekman, quantitative researchers only, closed-ended items; treat as a snapshot of 2014 opinion, not evidence.
- [V] ADDED: Keltner, Tracy, Sauter & Cowen 2019 (*J Nonverbal Behav* 43(2):195–201, doi:10.1007/s10919-019-00298-y): basic-emotion proponents' reply to Crivelli & Fridlund 2019: BET is a "multifaceted" theory, critics focus narrowly on faces of six emotions and on single "critical tests".

#### C2. The constructed-emotion case
- [V] Barrett 2006 ("Are emotions natural kinds?", *Perspect Psychol Sci* 1(1):28–58, doi:10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00003.x): reviews evidence "inconsistent with the view that there are kinds of emotion with boundaries that are carved in nature". Barrett 2017 (*SCAN* 12(1):1–23, doi:10.1093/scan/nsw154) "theory of constructed emotion": a brain-based computational account in which emotions are built from interoceptive predictions, concepts and context; popular version *How Emotions Are Made* (2017).
- [V] ★ Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019 ("Emotional expressions reconsidered", *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 20(1):1–68, doi:10.1177/1529100619832930; I read the full text): "people do sometimes smile when happy, frown when sad, scowl when angry, and so on ... more than what would be expected by chance" yet expressions "vary substantially across cultures, situations, and even across people within a single situation", similar configurations express more than one category, and a scowl often communicates something other than an emotional state. Criteria borrowed from Haidt & Keltner 1999: reliability/specificity of 70–90% = strong evidence, 40–69% moderate, 20–39% weak, <20% no support.
- [V] ★ The review's headline numbers (Duran, Reisenzein & Fernández-Dols 2017 meta-analysis of spontaneous facial movements, 37 articles, mostly lab): average proportion of emotional episodes in which the predicted configuration appeared, by emotion (the figure's N label in brackets, i.e. a handful of studies per cell): anger .28 (3), disgust .32 (5), fear .34 (3), happiness .12 (1), sadness .21 (2), surprise .09 (16); correlations between emotion intensity and predicted configuration: anger .22 (6), disgust .24 (4), fear .11 (1), happiness .27 (12), sadness .41 (5), surprise .24 (3) (values read from the review's Figure 6; several cells rest on a single study). Expanded version (preprint numbers quoted in the review): 131 effect sizes, 76 studies, 4,487 participants, mean r = .31 (per-emotion .06–.45), mean proportion .22 (.11–.35). So "people scowl when angry about 28–30% of the time" is roughly what the meta-analysis shows (k = 3), but it is a lab-induced-emotion estimate.
- [V] ★ ADDED: Published version: Durán & Fernández-Dols 2021 (*Emotion* 21(7):1550–1569, doi:10.1037/emo0001015, PMID 34780241): co-occurrence effect size .13 for full FACS expressions, .23 including partial expressions, and r = .30 between intensity of reported emotion and intensity of the predicted expression; greatest for disgust, least for surprise; conclusion that the six classic emotions "do not reliably co-occur with their predicted facial signal"; heterogeneity between samples. Use these published values rather than the preprint's .31/.22.
- [V] Posed expressions and perception (Barrett 2019 summary): same-culture perceivers match posed configurations strongly; cross-culture moderate; in the only study of expression production in a remote culture (Ekman 1972, Fore, sample size not reported) US students judged 9 Fore posers: smile → happiness 73%, frown → sadness 68%, scowl → anger 51%, nose-wrinkle → disgust 46%, but surprise 27% and fear 18%.
- [V] ★ Gendron, Roberson, van der Vyver & Barrett 2014 ("Perceptions of emotion from facial expressions are not culturally universal", *Emotion* 14(2):251–262, doi:10.1037/a0036052; I read the full text via PMC): Himba (Kunene, NW Namibia) 65 recruited → 54 analysed after exclusions (11 excluded for non-completion, failure to sort by emotion, feasibility); US community sample 68 recruited (Boston Museum of Science). 36 cards, 6 posed expression types × 6 African-American identities. FREE sort (no emotion words): US participants named their piles with discrete emotion words far more (M = 4.45 vs Himba 0.81 per participant) while Himba used action descriptors ("laughing", "looking at something") more (2.38 vs 0.84). Cluster analysis: Himba grouped smiling faces and wide-eyed faces but did not form separate clusters for scowl (anger), pout (sadness), nose-wrinkle (disgust); US grouped smiling, scowling, wide-eyed and neutral, not separate pout/nose-wrinkle clusters. With emotion-word anchors both groups moved toward the "universal" pattern but substantial cultural variation persisted (Himba still only smiling and wide-eyed clusters; the authors note these could reflect valence and arousal). NOTE for writers: this is a SORTING task, so there is no "% of Himba choices matching the Western label" in this paper.
- [V] ADDED: Gendron et al. 2014b ("Cultural relativity in perceiving emotion from vocalizations", *Psychol Sci* 25(4):911–920, doi:10.1177/0956797613517239): Himba did not freely label Western vocalizations with the expected emotion terms (they perceived valence and to some extent arousal); in a concept-embedded 2-choice task they were above chance only when target and foil differed in valence only. Contrasts with Sauter et al. 2010 — different tasks, different conclusions.
- [V] ★ Crivelli, Jarillo, Russell & Fernández-Dols 2016 ("Reading emotions from faces in two indigenous societies", *J Exp Psychol Gen* 145(7):830–843, doi:10.1037/xge0000172): Trobriand Islands children/adolescents N = 68 (6–16 y) + Spanish control N = 113; Matemo Island (Mozambique) N = 36. Participants pointed to the face of the person feeling a named emotion. Spanish controls matched 83–100% of trials; indigenous groups matched smiles to happiness on 58% and 56% of trials, other emotions 7–46% (Trobriand) and 22–53% (Matemo); robust across age, gender, static vs dynamic, between/within design.
- [V] Crivelli, Russell, Jarillo & Fernández-Dols 2016 ("The fear gasping face as a threat display in a Melanesian society", *PNAS* 113(44):12403–12407, doi:10.1073/pnas.1611622113): Trobriand adolescents matched the Western "fear" gasping face mainly to anger and threat; when asked to select the threatening face, Trobrianders chose the gasping "fear" face while Spaniards chose the scowl.
- [V] ADDED: Siegel et al. 2018 ("Emotion fingerprints or emotion populations?", *Psychol Bull* 144(4):343–393, doi:10.1037/bul0000128): meta-analysis of 202 studies of autonomic (ANS) reactivity in lab emotion inductions; mean effect sizes increased for 59.4% of ANS variables across emotion categories but the pattern did not clearly distinguish one category from another; heterogeneity moderate-to-substantial (I² ≥ 30%) in 54% of effect sizes; publication-bias correction shrank effects further. Supports "emotion populations" over "fingerprints".
- [V] ADDED: Lindquist, Wager, Kober, Bliss-Moreau & Barrett 2012 ("The brain basis of emotion: a meta-analytic review", *Behav Brain Sci* 35(3):121–143, doi:10.1017/S0140525X11000446): "little evidence that discrete emotion categories can be consistently and specifically localized to distinct brain regions"; instead general networks active across categories. (Counter-evidence from pattern-classification studies exists and was not reviewed here.)
- [V] Context effects: Aviezer et al. 2008 ("Angry, disgusted, or afraid?", *Psychol Sci* 19(7):724–732, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02148.x; I read the paper): identical facial configurations convey different emotions depending on context, modulated by similarity between face and context; eye-fixation patterns shift with context from the first fixation. Details in Widget constants. Barrett, Mesquita & Gendron 2011 (*Curr Dir Psychol Sci* 20(5):286–290, doi:10.1177/0963721411422522): visual scenes, voices, bodies, other faces, cultural orientation and words shape how emotion is perceived in a face; context incorporation is routine, efficient and to some degree automatic.

#### C3. What both camps accept, and the grades
- [V] AGREED (each point appears in both Barrett et al. 2019 and in the BET-side papers above): (1) facial movements carry some information about emotion or social intent, above chance (Barrett 2019 abstract; Elfenbein & Ambady 2002); (2) the mapping varies with culture, situation and person (Barrett 2019; Cordaro 2018 "cultural variations"; the in-group advantage); (3) emotion categories are not sealed boxes — BET-side authors report "fuzzy" boundaries and gradients (Cowen & Keltner 2017, 2019), constructionists speak of populations of variable instances; (4) both sides call for larger, more naturalistic data (Barrett 2019's recommendations; Cowen 2021's 6-million-video study). DISPUTED: whether anger/fear/etc. are biologically distinct "natural kinds" each with a dedicated signal, autonomic pattern and brain circuit (BET: yes at some level; constructionists: no — instances are variable populations assembled from core affect, concepts and context).
- STUDY "Each basic emotion has its own fixed, universal fingerprint (face / body / brain)" — original: Ekman & Friesen 1971 (189 adults, 69–100% agreement vs 33% chance, fear/surprise failure) and Ekman's programme. Later evidence: Elfenbein & Ambady 2002 (above-chance everywhere, in-group advantage), Crivelli 2016 (7–46% in Trobrianders), Gendron 2014 (Himba sorting), Durán & Fernández-Dols 2021 (co-occurrence .13–.30), Siegel 2018 (ANS fingerprints not found), Lindquist 2012 (no consistent brain localisation); versus Cowen 2019/2021, Cordaro 2018, Sauter 2010 (cross-cultural structure).
  GRADE C: strong, credible evidence on BOTH sides with different methods (forced-choice/posed vs free-sort/spontaneous/naturalistic); no consensus. — Barrett 2019; Durán 2021; Cowen 2021; Siegel 2018
- STUDY "The same face/body state is read as different emotions depending on context" — original: Aviezer 2008 (N = 16 per experiment); Later evidence: Barrett, Mesquita & Gendron 2011 review; Gendron 2014; many conceptual replications of context effects; size depends on how similar the face is to the context's typical expression.
  GRADE B: direction robust across labs and stimuli; effect size depends on face–context similarity and task. — Aviezer 2008; Barrett 2011

#### C4. Concept learning, granularity, body state and judgment (hungry judges, hangry)
- [~] ★ Widen & Russell 2008 ("Children acquire emotion categories gradually", *Cognitive Development* 23(2):291–312): 168 children aged 2–5 labelled and sorted faces for fear, happiness, sadness, anger; categories start broad (organised by valence) and narrow gradually, so a child who says "angry" at first includes other negative faces. Abstract not retrieved; details from secondary summaries — treat numbers (168; ages 2–5) as [~].
- [~] Barrett, Gross, Christensen & Benvenuto 2001 (*Cogn Emot* 15(6):713–724, "Knowing what you're feeling and knowing what to do about it") introduced emotional granularity/differentiation → regulation; Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight 2015 (*Curr Dir Psychol Sci* 24(1):10–16) review. Not opened.
- [V] ADDED: Kalokerinos, Erbas, Ceulemans & Kuppens 2019 (*Psychol Sci* 30(6):863–879, doi:10.1177/0956797619838763): two experience-sampling studies (N = 200 and 101; 34,660 and 6,282 measurements): low negative-emotion differentiation was NOT linked to choosing worse strategies, but to using strategies less effectively (all strategies in Study 1, four of six in Study 2 were more strongly tied to increased negative emotion among low differentiators).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Seah & Coifman 2022 (*Emotion* 22(7):1686–1697, doi:10.1037/emo0000968, PMID 34264705): meta-analysis of 17 studies: negative emotion differentiation was associated with fewer maladaptive behaviours (binge drinking, NSSI, treatment non-compliance...), r = −.15, and r = −.09 after controlling negative affect; same in clinical (n = 7; −.15) and non-clinical (n = 10; −.16) samples.
  GRADE B (granularity ↔ better regulation): replicated associations, small, mostly correlational. — Seah & Coifman 2022; Kalokerinos 2019
- [V] ★ Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso 2011 ("Extraneous factors in judicial decisions", *PNAS* 108(17):6889–6892, doi:10.1073/pnas.1018033108): Israeli parole boards, 1,112 rulings (count per Glöckner 2016), two food breaks segmenting the day into three sessions: favourable rulings fall gradually from ≈65% to nearly zero within a session and jump back to ≈65% after a break.
- [V] ★ Rebuttals: Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011 (*PNAS* 108(42):E833, doi:10.1073/pnas.1110910108; letter paywalled — contents as described by Glöckner 2016): case order is not random; prisoners without a lawyer tend to be scheduled last in a session and are much less likely to win parole. Danziger et al.'s reply (PNAS 2011) says the pattern persists when representation is controlled. Glöckner 2016 (*Judgm Decis Mak* 11(6):601–610, doi:10.1017/s1930297500004812): favourable rulings take longer (M = 7.37 min, SD 5.11) than unfavourable (M = 5.21, SD 4.97); a simulated rational judge who ends the session instead of starting a long case just before a break reproduces a decline "comparable" to the observed one (base rate of favourable rulings 36%); censoring the last 5% of cases per session adds about 15 points of artefact; the effect is "overestimated" and "at best small in magnitude (if existing at all)". Chatziathanasiou 2022 (*German Law J* 23(4):452–464, doi:10.1017/glj.2022.32): the validity of the "hungry judge" effect "is doubtful".
- [V] ADDED: The depletion/glucose mechanism the story relied on: Hagger et al. 2016 multilab RRR of ego depletion (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(4):546–573, doi:10.1177/1745691616652873): 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]; Vohs et al. 2021 (*Psychol Sci* 32(10):1566–1581, doi:10.1177/0956797621989733): 36 labs, N = 3,531, d = 0.06 (confirmatory), Bayes factor ≈ 4:1 for the null.
- STUDY Hungry judges (Danziger 2011) — original: 1,112 parole rulings, ≈65% → ≈0% favourable within sessions. Later evidence: non-random case ordering (Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011), simulation artefact (Glöckner 2016), implausible effect size, mechanism (ego depletion) failed in 2 large multilab tests.
  GRADE D: do not teach "judges rule harshly when hungry" as a finding; teach the pattern as a scheduling artefact case study. — Glöckner 2016; Hagger 2016; Vohs 2021
- [V] Hangry (everyday hunger → irritability): Bushman, DeWall, Pond & Hanus 2014 (*PNAS* 111(17):6254–6257, doi:10.1073/pnas.1400619111): 107 married couples, 21 days; lower blood glucose ↔ more pins in a voodoo doll representing the spouse (0–51 pins) and higher intensity/longer duration of noise blasts set for the spouse (correlational). Swami, Hochstöger, Kargl & Stieger 2022 (*PLoS ONE* 17(7):e0269629, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0269629): 64 participants, 21-day experience sampling, 9,142 responses: more hunger ↔ more anger and irritability, less pleasure (robust to sex, age, BMI, diet, trait anger); no association with arousal. Ackermans, Jonker, Bennik & de Jong 2022 (*Appetite* 168:105746, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2021.105746): women randomised to 14 h fast (n = 53) vs breakfast (n = 55): fasting women reported more tension, anger, fatigue and confusion and less vigour. (The task file's "Ackermann 2023" is probably this Ackermans 2022 paper; I found no 2023 paper under that name.)
  GRADE B for "hunger goes with more negative/irritable self-reported mood" (3 independent designs, small samples, mostly self-report; glucose as mechanism untested/contested). — Swami 2022; Ackermans 2022; Bushman 2014

### Widget constants (07)
- [V] ★ Same face, different context: Aviezer et al. 2008, Experiment 1 (N = 16 undergraduates, University of Toronto; 10 Ekman–Friesen disgust faces placed on bodies/contexts; six-label forced choice, no time limit). Isolated disgust faces were labelled "disgust" 65.6% of the time (chance 16.7%); isolated context bodies were categorised at 92–96%. When the SAME disgust face sat on a body conveying: disgust → labelled disgust 91%; fear context → 59% disgust, 13% labelled fear; sadness context → 35% disgust, 29% labelled sadness; anger context → 11% disgust, 87% labelled anger (values read from the paper's Figure 2; the disgust-face-on-anger-body result was as likely to be labelled with the context's emotion as the disgust-face-on-disgust-body, p > .7). Accuracy fell with the perceptual similarity between the disgust face and the face typically associated with the context. — doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02148.x
- [V] Experiment 2 (same paper; N = 16, Hebrew University): context also shifts dimensional ratings (valence/arousal) of faces when the face resembles the context's typical expression; Experiment 3: eye-movement patterns on the same face change with context from the first fixation. Use only qualitatively.
- [V] Barrett, Mesquita & Gendron 2011: context effects shown for scenes, voices, bodies, other faces, cultural orientation and words. Use as a qualitative second source; no single number.
- [V] Ekman & Friesen 1971 Fore adult data (above) is the "no context" baseline for the contrasting view: with a story-context and a 3-way choice, 69–100% matched.
- Widget guidance: for the reader's own labels of one body state in 4–5 situations, compare their label spread against Aviezer's pattern (the SAME disgust face on an anger-conveying body was labelled "anger" in 87% of trials and "disgust" in only 11%) and Gendron's free-sort result (without emotion words Himba sorted by valence/arousal and action, not by Western emotion categories).

### A. Counts, energy, connectomes
#### A1. How many neurons, glia, synapses?

#### A2. The energy budget (why "only a few neurons at a time")
- [V] ★ Lennie 2003 ("The cost of cortical computation", *Curr Biol* 13(6):493–497, doi:10.1016/S0960-9822(03)00135-0; PDF read): "In the young human adult, the brain accounts for 20% of the total [body energy]; in children, the fraction is nearer 50%" (citing Clarke & Sokoloff); the awake brain's consumption "does not change with normal variations in mental activity, though it is reduced by sleep and various pathological states". Whole-brain glucose use, median in young adults: 77 mg/min; neocortex = 44% of it (0.40 µmol glucose/g/min × 475 cm³ cortex ≈ 3.4 × 10^21 ATP/min). 大脑约占全身能量的 20%（儿童近 50%）
- [~] Raichle & Gusnard 2002 ("Appraising the brain's energy budget", *PNAS* 99(16):10237–10239, doi:10.1073/pnas.172399499): opening says the brain is about 2% of body weight but about 20% of the body's oxygen and calories, and that this high metabolic rate is remarkably constant despite varying mental and motor activity — I could not open the commentary text (PNAS page returned 403, PMC behind a cookie wall); wording taken from search-engine excerpts, so use Lennie for the 20% and treat the "2%" as a standard textbook value [~].
- [~] "≈ 20 W": my own conversion of Lennie's 77 mg glucose/min (≈ 416 kcal/day ≈ 20 W; also ≈ 20% of a ~100 W resting body) — derived, not stated in either paper. Say "of the order of 20 watts" and label as a back-of-envelope figure.
- [V] ★ Attwell & Laughlin 2001 ("An energy budget for signaling in the grey matter of the brain", *J Cereb Blood Flow Metab* 21(10):1133–1145, doi:10.1097/00004647-200110000-00001): for **rodent** grey matter, action potentials ≈ 47% of the signalling energy, postsynaptic glutamate effects 34%, resting potential 13%, glutamate recycling 3%; "an increase in activity of 1 action potential/cortical neuron/s" raises O2 use by 145 mL/100 g grey matter/h; the budget favours distributed codes with ≤ 15% of neurons simultaneously active.
- [V] ★ Cost of one spike: in a human neocortical pyramidal cell a spike costs about 2.4 × 10^9 ATP molecules (Lennie Fig. 1A; 52% EPSPs evoked at postsynaptic sites, 33% axon propagation, 6% dendrites; the sustainable-rate calculation in the text uses 2.2 × 10^9 for ion-flux reversal); Lennie scales the rat analysis of Attwell & Laughlin — a human spike costs 3.3× a rat spike; spiking-related processes ≈ 13% of cortical energy (resting potentials ≈ 1.3 × 10^21 of 3.4 × 10^21 ATP/min).
- [V] ★ Sparse activity: the cortex's energy supports an average of only **0.16 spikes/s/neuron** (1.9 × 10^10 cortical neurons); "fewer than 1%" of neurons can be substantially active at once (abstract); a "strong" signal (10 spikes in 200 ms) is affordable in 1 in 312 cortical neurons; sustaining 1.8 spikes/s/neuron in all cortical neurons would use more energy than the whole brain normally does, 13 spikes/s more than the whole body. Energetically optimal active fraction ≈ 3% (Levy & Baxter's model, as applied by Lennie) or 1–4% for a group representing 100–1,000 distinct entities (Attwell–Laughlin-type model); if 3% of neurons fired, "less than 10% of cortex could be active at all". These are model estimates (human values scaled from rat), not direct measurements. 稀疏编码；一次只有很小比例的神经元在放电
- [V] Local nuance (Lennie): in visual cortex, glucose use measured by PET/MRS rises by only about 50% from darkness to rich flicker (≈ 1 neuron in 63 firing at 50 spikes/s), while whole-brain use is essentially constant — the energy budget is reallocated among regions, not raised; the author links this to attention as a limited-capacity allocator.
- STUDY "The brain's energy budget forces sparse activity" — original: Attwell & Laughlin 2001 (rodent budget); Lennie 2003 (human scaling). Later evidence: not re-checked beyond these two; the 2002–03 estimates are model-based and use rat parameters scaled to human. GRADE B: direction (spikes are expensive, activity is sparse) is robust; the exact fractions (<1%, 1 in 312) are order-of-magnitude. — Attwell & Laughlin 2001; Lennie 2003
### B. The neuron: spikes, firing rates, doctrine, synapse, transmission
#### B1. The spike: all-or-none, rate code, ionic mechanism, integrate-and-fire
- [V] ★ Adrian, Nobel lecture "The Activity of the Nerve Fibres" (12 Dec 1932; nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1932/adrian/lecture/, read): single sensory fibres (frog muscle spindle; recorded with Zotterman) carry "waves of constant size and duration"; discharge starts at about 10 per second and rises to 50 per second or more as stretch increases; the nerve fibre "can only transmit a succession of brief explosive waves" and the message varies only by their frequency and total number (the same holds for Limulus eye, per Hartline). 全或无 + 频率编码 (all-or-none + rate code). Adrian's own 1914 paper: *J Physiol* 47(6):460–474, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1914.sp001637 ("The all-or-none principle in nerve"; no abstract on PubMed, not opened).
- [V] Adrian (same lecture) credits the all-or-nothing idea to Gotch (equal duration of the potential wave for weak and strong stimuli) and Keith Lucas (first direct evidence of the "ungraded character" of the wave in excitable tissue other than heart); Nobel 1932 to Sherrington and Adrian "for their discoveries regarding the functions of neurons".
- [V] ★ Hodgkin & Huxley 1952 ("A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve", *J Physiol* 117(4):500–544, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1952.sp004764; PMID 12991237; abstract not on PubMed, content taken from the 1963 Nobel lectures, read): squid giant axon; "voltage clamp" measurements → equations for membrane current → computed action potentials "showed a surprisingly good agreement" with the real axon. Nobel 1963 to Eccles, Hodgkin and Huxley "for their discoveries concerning the ionic mechanisms involved in excitation and inhibition in the peripheral and central portions of the nerve cell membrane". 霍奇金-赫胥黎模型
- [V] ★ Huxley's Nobel lecture (11 Dec 1963): the 1951 computations were "done by hand" — "a membrane action took a matter of days to compute, and a propagated action potential took a matter of weeks". (A hand-computed neuron: good for the "model cost" theme of the series.)
- [V] Hodgkin's Nobel lecture (1963): in most excitable cells the action potential exceeds the resting potential, "often by 40 or 50 mV" (the inside goes positive) — incompatible with Bernstein's "membrane breakdown" idea. Measured examples: squid axon 1939 action potential 86 mV on a resting potential 45 mV (Hodgkin & Huxley), average 108 mV on 51 mV with a d.c. amplifier (Curtis & Cole); amphibian myelinated fibre: action potential 120 mV, resting 70 mV (so peak ≈ +50 mV); mammalian nerve: "absolute values … are unknown, but they are probably not very different from those reported for frog" (1963 statement).
- [~] Temperature caveat: Hodgkin–Huxley's squid-axon kinetics were measured in the cold (Huxley's lecture figure: 8.5 °C; the HH model's standard temperature is 6.3 °C — from memory, HH 1952 not opened); mammalian neurons at 37 °C are faster, so HH time constants are not mammalian constants.
- [V] ADDED: Integrate-and-fire: Lapicque 1907 is the origin — Brunel & van Rossum 2007 ("Lapicque's 1907 paper: from frogs to integrate-and-fire", *Biol Cybern* 97(5–6):337–339, doi:10.1007/s00422-007-0190-0): "Exactly 100 years ago, Louis Lapicque published a paper on the excitability of nerves that is often cited in the context of integrate-and-fire neurons" (with a translation). 整合发放模型
- [V] Gerstner, Kistler, Naud & Paninski 2014 (*Neuronal Dynamics*, neuronaldynamics.epfl.ch, Ch. 1): spikes have "an amplitude of about 100 mV and typically a duration of 1-2 ms"; "The minimal distance between two spikes defines the absolute refractory period of the neuron"; for a typical neuron the membrane time constant "is in the range of 10 ms", long compared with a spike of about 1 ms.
- [V] ADDED: Firing rates in cortex (measured): Lennie 2003: sustainable average ≈ 0.16 spikes/s/neuron; in behaving monkeys, task-engaged neurons "can discharge action potentials at rates of up to 100/s, at least for a short time". Hromádka, DeWeese & Zador 2008 (*PLoS Biol* 6(1):e16, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.0060016): awake rat auditory cortex, cell-attached recordings: sounds elicited high rates (>20 spikes/s) in < 5% of neurons at any instant; some neurons fire < 0.01 spikes/s spontaneously, others > 50 spikes/s when driven; population rates fit a lognormal distribution. Barth & Poulet 2012 (*Trends Neurosci* 35(6):345–355, doi:10.1016/j.tins.2012.03.008): "a minority of neurons are responsible for the majority of spikes"; Buzsáki & Mizuseki 2014 (*Nat Rev Neurosci* 15:264–278, doi:10.1038/nrn3687): skewed (typically lognormal) distributions of firing rates and synaptic weights.

#### B2. Neuron doctrine, synapse, chemical transmission
- [V] ★ Nobel 1906 shared by Camillo Golgi and Santiago Ramón y Cajal "in recognition of their work on the structure of the nervous system" — yet the two lectures (Stockholm, 11 and 12 Dec 1906) argued opposite sides. Golgi (whose silver "black staining" made the anatomy visible): "I have always been opposed to the neuron theory", while "acknowledging that its starting-point is to be found in my own work"; he called the doctrine "generally recognized to be going out of favour" (hindsight: wrong). Cajal: "the nerve elements possess reciprocal relationships in contiguity but not in continuity"; he applied Golgi's method to embryos and young animals. The word "neuron" is Waldeyer's, from a lecture of 1891 (Golgi's lecture quotes Waldeyer, p. 52: independent "nerve units (neurons)"; Cajal's lecture credits Waldeyer too). 神经元学说 (neuron doctrine); 高尔基 vs 卡哈尔 — both read from the Nobel lecture PDFs (nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/golgi-lecture.pdf, cajal-lecture.pdf)
- [V] Cajal 1906 also states how a signal could cross the gap: "a sort of induction or influence from a distance" — i.e. in 1906 the mechanism of the gap was an open hypothesis (the mechanism was shown to be chemical in 1921, see below).
- [V] ★ "Synapse": Tansey 1997 ("Not committing barbarisms: Sherrington and the synapse, 1897", *Brain Res Bull* 44(3):211–212, doi:10.1016/s0361-9230(97)00312-2): the word first appeared in 1897 in the 7th edition of Michael Foster's *Textbook of Physiology*; Sherrington, who helped write the nervous-system volume, "can be credited with developing and advocating the physiological concept of a synapse"; the word itself was devised by the Cambridge classicist Arthur Verrall. 突触 (Sherrington 1897; not Cajal, not Golgi)
- [V] ★ Chemical transmission: Loewi, Nobel lecture 12 Dec 1936 ("The Chemical Transmission of Nerve Action", read): "Up until the year 1921 it was not known how the stimulation of a nerve influenced the effector organ"; in 1921 (*Pflügers Arch* 189:239) Loewi obtained "certain proof" that stimulating the nerves of a frog's heart releases substances that, passed on with the heart fluid to a test heart, make it react as nerve stimulation does — so nerves "do not act directly upon the heart". Nobel 1936 to Henry Dale and Otto Loewi "for their discoveries relating to chemical transmission of nerve impulses". 化学突触传递
- STUDY Neuron doctrine (neurons are separate cells joined by synapses) — original: Cajal's 1888–1906 anatomy (Golgi stain); Sherrington 1897 (function); Loewi 1921 (chemistry). Later evidence: electron microscopy and every connectome (C. elegans 1986 → fly 2024 → human 1 mm³ 2024) are built on the doctrine; gap-junction (electrical) synapses exist but do not revive the reticular theory (not separately sourced here). GRADE: no letter grade (history/foundation); [def]-level.
### C. Plasticity: Hebb, LTP, STDP, Aplysia, engrams, adult plasticity, neurogenesis, critical periods
#### C1. Hebb → LTP → STDP: how a synapse is rewritten
- [V] ★ Hebb's postulate, exactly (Hebb 1949, *The Organization of Behavior*, Wiley, p. 62 as usually cited; text as quoted in Markram, Gerstner & Sjöström 2011, "A history of spike-timing-dependent plasticity", *Front Synaptic Neurosci* 3:4, doi:10.3389/fnsyn.2011.00004, OA, read): "When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased." Hebb himself wrote that "The general idea is an old one" and the same review shows he was not the first to conjecture it. 赫布规则
- [✗] ★ "Neurons that fire together wire together" is NOT Hebb's wording. It is a later slogan: the 1992 Scientific American article by Carla Shatz ("The developing brain", *Sci Am* 267(3):60–67, doi:10.1038/scientificamerican0992-60) is the usual source (the line is quoted in secondary sources as "In a sense, then, cells that fire together wire together" — article text not opened [~]); Löwel & Singer 1992 (*Science* 255(5041):209–212, doi:10.1126/science.1372754) is the earlier experiment (kittens with induced strabismus: "the selection criterion is the correlation of activity"), and a near-identical phrase is sometimes credited to them (who first printed it is disputed [~]). Markram et al. 2011 [V]: the slogan "does not reflect all of what Hebb meant" because Hebb's rule is directional (A must take part in firing B), and "cells that fire together do not always wire together, because timing matters too".
- [V] ★ Bliss & Lømo 1973 ("Long-lasting potentiation of synaptic transmission in the dentate area of the anaesthetized rabbit following stimulation of the perforant path", *J Physiol* 232(2):331–356, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1973.sp010273): in 15 of 18 urethane-anaesthetised rabbits the population response of dentate granule cells was potentiated for 30 min to 10 h after conditioning trains (10–20/s for 10–15 s, or 100/s for 3–4 s); the authors proposed two mechanisms (more efficient transmission at perforant-path synapses; higher granule-cell excitability). 长时程增强 (LTP)
- [V] ADDED: Malinow 1991 (per Markram 2011) gave the first direct test of Hebb's prediction on a single connection (four monosynaptic CA3–CA1 pairs in slice, LTP by paired bursts); Markram, Lübke, Frotscher & Sakmann 1997 (*Science* 275(5297):213–215, doi:10.1126/science.275.5297.213): in dual recordings from neocortical pyramidal cells, EPSP amplitude was up- or down-regulated depending on the precise timing of the postsynaptic spike relative to the EPSP; per the 2011 history: LTP when the presynaptic input precedes the spike by ≈10 ms (causal), LTD when it follows it (acausal), no change at 100 ms offsets, and blockade of postsynaptic spiking or NMDA receptors abolished the LTP.
- [V] ★ Bi & Poo 1998 (*J Neurosci* 18(24):10464–10472, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998): in cultures of dissociated rat hippocampal neurons, postsynaptic spiking within 20 ms AFTER presynaptic activation → LTP; spiking within 20 ms BEFORE → LTD; significant LTP only at synapses with relatively low initial strength; both needed NMDA receptors and were absent when the postsynaptic cell was GABAergic; authors suggest Hebb's rule "may need to incorporate a quantitative consideration of spike timing". The name "STDP" came later (Song, Miller & Abbott 2000, per Markram et al. 2011). 脉冲时间依赖可塑性
- [V] Aplysia and Nobel 2000 (Carlsson, Greengard, Kandel "for their discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system"): per the Nobel press release (nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2000/press-release/, read) *Aplysia* has "around 20.000" nerve cells; a gill-withdrawal reflex strengthened for days to weeks was due to "an amplification of the synapse" between sensory and motor cells; weak stimuli → short-term memory (minutes–hours; ion-channel phosphorylation → more calcium entry → more transmitter release); stronger or repeated stimuli → long-term memory (weeks; cAMP/protein kinase A → nucleus → new proteins; "If this synthesis of new proteins is prevented, the long term memory will be blocked but not the short term memory"). Bailey & Chen 1989 (*J Neurobiol* 20(5):356–372, doi:10.1002/neu.480200508): sensory-neuron active zones were larger in sensitised and smaller in habituated animals vs controls, with a more dramatic parallel change in the number of presynaptic varicosities per neuron. 海兔 (Aplysia) 学习的突触基础
- [V] ADDED: Evidence that LTP is learning (not just a lab effect): Morris, Anderson, Lynch & Baudry 1986 (*Nature* 319:774–776, doi:10.1038/319774a0): chronic AP5 (NMDA-receptor antagonist) infusion blocked LTP in vivo and impaired rat place learning, but not visual-discrimination learning; Whitlock, Heynen, Shuler & Bear 2006 (*Science* 313(5790):1093–1097, doi:10.1126/science.1128134): one-trial inhibitory-avoidance learning in rats changed CA1 glutamate receptors as LTP does and the learning-induced potentiation occluded HFS-induced LTP, so "inhibitory avoidance training induces LTP in CA1".
- [V] ★ ADDED: The verdict of the standard review: Martin, Grimwood & Morris 2000 (*Annu Rev Neurosci* 23:649–711, doi:10.1146/annurev.neuro.23.1.649): "a wealth of data supports the notion that synaptic plasticity is necessary for learning and memory, but that little data currently supports the notion of sufficiency."
- STUDY LTP/STDP (activity-dependent, NMDA-dependent, timing-dependent strengthening and weakening of synapses) — original: Bliss & Lømo 1973 (15/18 rabbits); Markram 1997; Bi & Poo 1998. Later evidence: reproduced for >50 years across species, preparations and labs (see Morris 1986, Whitlock 2006, Malinow 1991 above); STDP windows vary with synapse type and firing rate (the 2011 history stresses frequency- and location-dependent timing windows). GRADE A for "synapse strengths change with activity, by an NMDA-dependent, timing-sensitive rule" (the phenomenon); GRADE B for "this is how memories are stored" (necessity supported, sufficiency not — Martin 2000). — Markram 2011; Martin 2000
- [V] ★ ADDED: LIVE DISPUTE — are memories stored as synaptic strengths? Abraham, Jones & Glanzman 2019 (*npj Sci Learn* 4:9, doi:10.1038/s41539-019-0048-y): the view that synapses are the principal site of storage "has received substantial support", yet "we are still without conclusive proof that memories reside at synapses" and non-synaptic substrates exist. Chen, Cai, Pearce, Sun, Roberts & Glanzman 2014 (*eLife* 3:e03896, doi:10.7554/eLife.03896): in *Aplysia* sensory–motor co-cultures, serotonin-induced growth of new varicosities was reversed by antimnemonic treatments, but the final synaptic structure was reached by an "apparently arbitrary retraction" of both new and original synapses, and the memory persisted covertly — "These results challenge the idea that stable synapses store long-term memories." Ryan, Roy, Pignatelli, Arons & Tonegawa 2015 (*Science* 348(6238):1007–1013, doi:10.1126/science.aaa5542): engram cells under protein-synthesis-inhibitor amnesia lacked the strengthened synapses and spine density yet optogenetic activation still retrieved the memory — authors propose a specific pattern of engram-cell connectivity may store the information, with strengthened synapses aiding retrieval. Yang, Pan & Gan 2009 (*Nature* 462:920–924, doi:10.1038/nature08577): in mouse cortex a small fraction of experience-induced new spines persist for the animal's life (a structural basis for lifelong memory).

#### C2. Engrams
- [V] ★ Josselyn & Tonegawa 2020 ("Memory engrams: Recalling the past and imagining the future", *Science* 367(6473):eaaw4325, doi:10.1126/science.aaw4325): Semon introduced "engram" in 1904 for the neural substrate of a memory; new tagging/manipulation technology has revived the field; "these findings are beginning to define an engram as the basic unit of memory" (abstract; hedged wording). 记忆痕迹/印迹 (engram)
- [V] ★ Liu et al. 2012 (*Nature* 484:381–385, doi:10.1038/nature11028): mice; dentate-gyrus neurons active during fear conditioning were tagged with ChR2; light re-activation in a different context elicited freezing (not in non-conditioned mice, nor in EYFP-labelled mice; not when cells labelled in a non-fear context were stimulated) — sufficiency of a sparse ensemble for recall. Ramirez et al. 2013 (*Science* 341:387–391, doi:10.1126/science.1239073): the same approach created a "false memory" (reactivating context-A cells during shock in context B made mice freeze in A).
- [V] ADDED: Independent-lab support: Garner et al. 2012 (Mayford lab; *Science* 335:1513–1516, doi:10.1126/science.1214985): DREADD-activated ensembles for context A during conditioning in context B produced a hybrid memory, and recall required reactivating the artificial network. Josselyn, Köhler & Frankland 2015 ("Finding the engram", *Nat Rev Neurosci* 16:521–534, doi:10.1038/nrn4000): before the "capture" studies engrams "remained largely elusive"; they propose four criteria for an engram.
- STUDY Engram cells (a sparse, identifiable ensemble that is necessary and sufficient for a specific memory) — original: Liu 2012 (mice, fear/context); Ramirez 2013. Later evidence: Garner 2012 (independent lab), Ryan 2015, review Josselyn & Tonegawa 2020. All are mouse studies, mostly fear/context memory in hippocampus/amygdala, using artificial stimulation; what counts as the engram (cells vs connections vs molecules) is disputed (see C1). GRADE B: direction replicated by several labs, but limited to mouse fear/context paradigms and to what artificial reactivation shows; no human evidence. — Garner 2012; Josselyn & Tonegawa 2020

#### C3. Adult plasticity (what the adult brain can and cannot do)
- [V] ★ Maguire et al. 2000 ("Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers", *PNAS* 97(8):4398–4403, doi:10.1073/pnas.070039597; PDF read from fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk): structural MRI, 16 licensed male right-handed London taxi drivers vs 50 healthy right-handed non-taxi-driving males (age-matched; ages 32–62 allowed): posterior hippocampi larger in taxi drivers, a more anterior region larger in controls — but the OVERALL hippocampal volume did not differ (volumetry in 16 age-matched controls: right hippocampus 4,300 ± 432 mm³ in drivers vs 4,255 ± 424 mm³ in controls, uncorrected), i.e. a regional redistribution, not a bigger hippocampus; right posterior hippocampal volume correlated with time as a taxi driver (r = 0.6, p < .05, VBM with age as covariate; r = 0.5, p < .06, pixel counting; anterior r = −0.6; one 42-year driver excluded from the correlations); training "The Knowledge" took on average 2 years (range 10 months–3.5 years). Cross-sectional, so it cannot separate cause from pre-existing difference.
- [V] ADDED: Maguire, Woollett & Spiers 2006 (*Hippocampus* 16(12):1091–1101, doi:10.1002/hipo.20233): taxi drivers vs London bus drivers (matched for driving and stress): taxi drivers still had more mid-posterior and less anterior hippocampal grey matter; years of experience correlated with volume only in taxi drivers; taxi drivers were worse at acquiring new visuo-spatial information (a possible cost).
- [V] ★ Woollett & Maguire 2011 ("Acquiring 'the Knowledge' of London's layout drives structural brain changes", *Curr Biol* 21(24):2109–2114, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2011.11.018, OA, read): longitudinal; 79 male trainees scanned at the start (T1) and 3–4 years later (T2) plus 31 male controls; at T2 39 trainees had qualified, 20 non-qualifiers returned (20 other trainees did not return; 2 of those had qualified), all 31 controls returned; no group differences at T1 (age, handedness, education, IQ); qualifiers showed a selective increase of posterior hippocampal grey-matter volume and changed memory profile; "no structural brain changes" in non-qualifiers or controls. 
- [V] ADDED: Critical review: Thomas & Baker 2013 ("Teaching an adult brain new tricks", *NeuroImage* 73:225–236, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.03.069): many reports of training-induced grey- or white-matter change in adult humans may reflect "limitations of experimental design, statistical methods, and methodological artifacts", "seriously undermining the evidence"; the most robust finding they identify is anterior hippocampal volume with exercise in elderly participants. (Use as a caution against generalising the taxi result to "brain training".)
- STUDY Adult human hippocampus structure follows spatial-navigation experience — original: Maguire 2000 (16 vs 50); Woollett & Maguire 2011 (longitudinal, 79 + 31). Later evidence: Maguire 2006 (bus-driver control); Thomas & Baker 2013 (general caution). Same research group for all three main studies; effects are regional grey-matter volume changes of unknown cellular basis. GRADE B: direction supported by cross-sectional, comparison-group and longitudinal designs; independent replication and cellular mechanism are lacking, and the wider "training changes adult brain structure" literature is graded C by Thomas & Baker's critique. — Woollett & Maguire 2011; Thomas & Baker 2013
- [✗] ★ "Adults cannot form new connections / the adult brain is fixed." Trachtenberg et al. 2002 (*Nature* 420:788–794, doi:10.1038/nature01273): repeated imaging of single pyramidal neurons in adult mouse barrel cortex over weeks: dendrites stable but spines come and go; "stable spines, about 50% of the population, persist for at least a month, whereas the remainder are present for a few days or less"; experience-dependent receptive-field plasticity came with increased synapse turnover. Yang et al. 2009 (above). (Mouse data; human adults show plasticity at the systems level — taxi drivers — but synapse-level evidence in humans is indirect.)

#### C4. Adult neurogenesis: the dispute and where it stands (as of October 2026)
- [V] ★ Sorrells et al. 2018 (*Nature* 555:377–381, doi:10.1038/nature25975): human dentate-gyrus progenitors and young neurons drop sharply in the first year of life; "only a few isolated young neurons" at 7 and 13 years; in healthy adults 18–77 y (n = 17 post-mortem) and epilepsy surgical samples (n = 12) "young neurons were not detected" — conclusion: neurogenesis "does not continue, or is extremely rare, in adult humans".
- [V] ★ Boldrini et al. 2018 (*Cell Stem Cell* 22(4):589–599, doi:10.1016/j.stem.2018.03.015; methods read at PMC5957089): whole autopsy hippocampi of 28 people (17 men, 11 women; 14–79 y; no neuropsychiatric disease or treatment; post-mortem interval ≤ 26 h): similar numbers of intermediate progenitors and "thousands of immature neurons" in the dentate gyrus across ages, but a smaller quiescent progenitor pool, less angiogenesis and neuroplasticity in older anterior-mid DG. Moreno-Jiménez et al. 2019 (*Nat Med* 25:554–560, doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0375-9): "thousands of immature neurons" in neurologically healthy subjects up to the ninth decade, declining progressively in Alzheimer's disease.
- [V] Kempermann et al. 2018 ("Human adult neurogenesis: evidence and remaining questions", *Cell Stem Cell* 23(1):25–30, doi:10.1016/j.stem.2018.04.004): there is "currently no reason to abandon the idea" that adult-generated neurons contribute to plasticity and cognition across the lifespan.
- [V] Single-nucleus RNA-seq era: Franjic et al. 2022 (*Neuron* 110(3):452–469, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2021.10.036): robust transcriptomic and histological neurogenesis signatures in adult mouse, pig and macaque "but not humans"; DCX "was detected in diverse human neurons, but it did not define immature neuron populations". Zhou et al. 2022 (*Nature* 607:527–533, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-04912-w): snRNA-seq plus a validated machine-learning classifier identified immature granule cells across the lifespan, "a substantial number … in the adult human hippocampus via low-frequency de novo generation", fewer in Alzheimer's disease; rare proliferating progenitors and neurogenesis in cultured surgical specimens. Dumitru et al. 2025 (*Science* 389(6755):58–63, doi:10.1126/science.adu9575): Ki67 antibodies plus machine learning found proliferating neural progenitors in adult hippocampus (dentate gyrus). Disouky et al. 2026 (*Nature* 652(8112):1264–1273, doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10169-4): 355,997 nuclei from young adults, cognitively normal elderly, SuperAgers, preclinical-AD and AD brains: neural stem cells, neuroblasts and immature granule neurons identified; altered in preclinical AD and AD; a distinct "resilience signature" in SuperAgers.
- STUDY Do adult humans make new hippocampal neurons? — original: Eriksson 1998 (not opened); Sorrells 2018 (none) vs Boldrini 2018 / Moreno-Jiménez 2019 (thousands). Later evidence: Franjic 2022 (none), Zhou 2022, Dumitru 2025, Disouky 2026 (rare cells present). Middle ground as of Oct 2026 (my synthesis of the five papers above): most recent single-cell studies find rare progenitors and immature neurons in some adults; how many survive to integrate, how they change with age or disease, and whether they matter for human cognition are unresolved; no study has shown it in living people. GRADE C: mixed replications with different methods, strong alternative explanations (marker specificity, tissue handling), and the functional question is open. Teach "rare and contested", not "none" and not "you grow thousands a day". — Sorrells 2018; Franjic 2022; Zhou 2022; Dumitru 2025; Disouky 2026

#### C5. Critical periods
- [V] ★ Hubel & Wiesel 1970 ("The period of susceptibility to the physiological effects of unilateral eye closure in kittens", *J Physiol* 206(2):419–436, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1970.sp009022): susceptibility to monocular lid suture "begins suddenly near the start of the fourth week, remains high until some time between the sixth and eighth weeks, and then declines, disappearing finally around the end of the third month"; "Monocular closure for over a year in an adult cat produces no detectable effects"; a 3–4-day closure in week 4–5 sharply cut the cells driven by both eyes; after 3 months' monocular deprivation from birth the proportion of cells the eye influenced fell "from 85 to about 7%"; reopening the eye for up to 5 y gave only very limited recovery of cortical physiology. 关键期 (critical period). Nobel 1981 (half to Roger Sperry, half jointly to David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel) "for their discoveries concerning information processing in the visual system".
- [V] ADDED: Plasticity can be re-opened in animals: Maya Vetencourt et al. 2008 (*Science* 320(5874):385–388, doi:10.1126/science.1150516): chronic fluoxetine reinstated ocular-dominance plasticity in adult rats and promoted recovery in adult amblyopic rats, with reduced intracortical inhibition and higher BDNF. (Rat data; I did not verify any human translation — treat as animal evidence only, not as advice.)
### D. Drugs act on synapses; chemical-imbalance caution; distributed storage; myths
#### D1. Drugs show that the synapse is a chemical interface (mechanism only; no medical advice)
- [V] ★ Caffeine = adenosine-receptor antagonist: Huang et al. 2005 (*Nat Neurosci* 8(7):858–859, doi:10.1038/nn1491): caffeine "binds to adenosine A1 and A2A receptors as an antagonist"; in mice, 5, 10 and 15 mg/kg increased wakefulness in wild-type and A1-knockout mice but not in A2A-knockout mice, so caffeine-induced wakefulness depends on A2A receptors. 咖啡因 — 腺苷受体拮抗剂 (the 1999 Fredholm et al. review, *Pharmacol Rev* 51:83–133, is the standard reference but PubMed has no abstract; not opened).
- [V] ★ Alcohol acts on several receptor systems, not one switch: Lovinger, White & Weight 1989 (*Science* 243(4899):1721–1724, doi:10.1126/science.2467382): ethanol (5–50 mM, "a range that also produces intoxication") inhibited NMDA-activated current in rat hippocampal neurons, by 61% at 50 mM, versus 18% (kainate) and 15% (quisqualate) responses at the same dose; Mihic et al. 1997 (*Nature* 389:385–389, doi:10.1038/38738): GABA-A and glycine receptor function is enhanced by alcohols and some anaesthetics, and two residues in transmembrane domains 2 and 3 are critical. Harris, Trudell & Mihic 2008 (*Sci Signal* 1(28):re7, doi:10.1126/scisignal.128re7): "exactly how it acts … is still poorly understood"; for most effects it is unknown whether ethanol binds the protein studied directly. 酒精：增强 GABA-A、抑制 NMDA（并非单一靶点）
- [V] ★ Dopamine as a transmitter: Nobel 2000 press release (read): Carlsson (late 1950s) showed dopamine is a transmitter in its own right (not only a noradrenaline precursor), concentrated in the basal ganglia; reserpine-treated animals lost spontaneous movement and L-dopa restored it; Parkinson's disease involves loss of dopamine-producing cells in the basal ganglia (L-dopa compensates); antipsychotic drugs "affect synaptic transmission by blocking dopamine receptors"; Carlsson also "contributed strongly to the development of selective serotonin uptake blockers" (SSRIs). 多巴胺是神经递质（卡尔松，2000 年诺贝尔奖）；SSRI = 选择性 5-羟色胺再摄取抑制剂
- [V] ★ "Chemical imbalance" caution (cross-reference: lesson 17/18 handle disorders and treatment; no advice here). Moncrieff, Cooper, Stockmann, Amendola, Hengartner & Horowitz 2022/2023 ("The serotonin theory of depression: a systematic umbrella review of the evidence", *Mol Psychiatry* 28(8):3243–3256, doi:10.1038/s41380-022-01661-0; online July 2022): 17 reviews/studies; "no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentrations" (largest samples: serotonin metabolite 5-HIAA n = 1,002, no association; SERT binding n = 1,845, "weak and inconsistent evidence of reduced binding in some areas" with prior antidepressant use not reliably excluded; SERT gene n = 115,257 and n = 43,165, no association or gene–stress interaction; tryptophan depletion n = 566, no effect in most healthy volunteers). Response: Jauhar, Cowen & Browning 2023 ("Fifty years on: Serotonin and depression", *J Psychopharmacol* 37(3):237–241, doi:10.1177/02698811231161813, OA, read): "there are some reliable abnormalities in serotonin mechanisms in depressed patients but their potential role in the causation of illness remains to be determined"; "diminished serotonin levels in the brain are neither necessary nor sufficient to cause clinical depression"; but the serotonin–depression link "is likely to outlive its recent obituaries". Harmer, Duman & Cowen 2017 (*Lancet Psychiatry* 4(5):409–418, doi:10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30015-9): a purely neurotransmitter-based account of antidepressant action is "challenged by the delayed clinical onset"; alternative/complementary accounts stress early changes in emotional processing and neural plasticity. AGREED by both sides: a simple "low serotonin" deficit is not established. DISPUTED: whether serotonin has any causal role and how SSRIs work.
- STUDY Serotonin-deficit ("chemical imbalance") theory of depression — original: serotonin hypothesis (proposed by the British psychiatrist Alec Coppen, "over 50 years" before 2023 per Jauhar et al.); Later evidence: Moncrieff 2022 umbrella review (no consistent association); Jauhar 2023 (even critics: deficit neither necessary nor sufficient). GRADE D for the simple deficit/imbalance claim as an explanation of depression (the drugs' pharmacology is real; the explanation is not). — Moncrieff 2022; Jauhar 2023

#### D2. Distributed storage and "the Jennifer Aniston neuron"
- [~] ★ Lashley 1950 ("In search of the engram", *Symp Soc Exp Biol* 4:454–482): after training rats on mazes and removing cortex in varying amounts, deficits tracked the amount of tissue removed more than its location ("mass action", "equipotentiality"). I could not open the paper or verify Lashley's famous quoted sentences, so no quotation is given. Current reading [V, abstracts]: engrams are real but sparse and identifiable (Josselyn 2015; Liu 2012), specific regions are essential for specific memories, and storage is distributed across a circuit — Lashley's strong equipotentiality is not supported; the modern evidence is graded in C2.
- [V] ★ Quiroga et al. 2005 ("Invariant visual representation by single neurons in the human brain", *Nature* 435:1102–1107, doi:10.1038/nature03687; abstract read): recordings in the human medial temporal lobe of patients with epilepsy (intracranial depth electrodes) found "a remarkable subset" of neurons selectively activated by "strikingly different pictures of given individuals, landmarks or objects and in some cases even by letter strings with their names" — suggesting "an invariant, sparse and explicit code". Quiroga's own account (Sci Am excerpt, 2013 [~]): the Aniston cell "responded to seven completely different images" of Jennifer Aniston "and to no other stimulus" among those tested; a second cell responded to four pictures of Halle Berry and not to 78 other pictures, five caricatures or seven other names; other neurons in the same patient responded to the Sydney Opera House and the Leaning Tower of Pisa. 概念细胞 (concept cells)
- [V] How to say it precisely: (1) it was a cell recorded among a tiny sample — the experimenters could test only a limited set of images, so "responds to X only" means "among those shown"; (2) Waydo, Kraskov, Quian Quiroga, Fried & Koch 2006 (*J Neurosci* 26(40):10232–10234, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2101-06.2006): a probabilistic analysis shows the data are "consistent with a sparse code in which neurons respond in a selective manner to a small fraction of stimuli" — not one cell per concept; (3) Quiroga 2012 ("Concept cells: the building blocks of declarative memory functions", *Nat Rev Neurosci* 13:587–597, doi:10.1038/nrn3251) argues these sparse, explicit, abstract MTL neurons matter for associations and episodic memory; (4) Bowers 2009 (*Psychol Rev* 116(1):220–251, doi:10.1037/a0014462) argues single-cell data fit localist coding better than distributed models, so "grandmother cell vs distributed" is itself still argued. The accurate summary: selective, invariant, sparse concept coding in human MTL neurons; not proof that one neuron stores one memory.
- STUDY Concept cells (sparse invariant MTL neurons) — original: Quiroga 2005 (epilepsy patients, MTL). Later evidence: Waydo 2006 (sparse-code consistency), Quiroga 2012 review; studied by one group in small clinical samples. GRADE B: selective, invariant responses are well documented, but sample sizes are small, stimuli limited, and the coding interpretation (sparse vs localist vs distributed) is disputed. — Waydo 2006; Quiroga 2012; Bowers 2009

#### D3. Myth list (08)
- [✗] ★ "We use only 10% of our brains." Not supported: no silent 90%; the real, different fact is energetic — at any instant only a small fraction of neurons can fire (Lennie 2003, abstract: the cost of a spike limits the number of neurons that can be substantially active at once "possibly to fewer than 1%") because spikes are expensive. I did not open a primary source for "no brain region is silent/unused" (Beyerstein 1999; Boyd 2008 in *Sci Am* — see COULD NOT VERIFY). Belief is common: Macdonald et al. 2017 (*Front Psychol* 8:1314, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01314; read): item "We only use 10% of our brain" answered incorrectly by 36% of the US general public (N = 3,045), 33% of educators (N = 598) and 14% of the high-neuroscience-exposure group (N = 234); Dekker et al. 2012's teachers answered incorrectly 48% (UK) and 46% (Netherlands) on the same item (as compared by Macdonald et al.). Origin story (Beyerstein 1999, in Della Sala (ed.), *Mind Myths*) not opened [~].
- [✗] ★ "Some people are left-brained, some right-brained." Nielsen, Zielinski, Ferguson, Lainhart & Anderson 2013 (*PLoS ONE* 8(8):e71275, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0071275): resting-state fMRI of 1,011 people aged 7–29; strongly lateralised connections cluster as 9 left- and 11 right-lateralised hubs (left: default-mode and language regions; right: attention-control regions), but lateralisation "appears to be a local rather than global property" and the data are "not consistent with a whole-brain phenotype of greater 'left-brained' or greater 'right-brained' network strength across individuals". (Hemispheres do specialise — language, attention — the myth is a personality-type claim.) Sperry's Nobel 1981 was for "his discoveries concerning the functional specialization of the cerebral hemispheres", not for personality types. 左脑/右脑人格论 = 神话
- [✗] "The brain stops developing at 25." There is no single age. Bethlehem et al. 2022 ("Brain charts for the human lifespan", *Nature* 604:525–533, doi:10.1038/s41586-022-04554-y; 123,984 MRI scans, 101,457 people, 115 days post-conception to 100 y; OA full text read): grey-matter volume peaks at 5.9 y (95% CI 5.8–6.1) then declines almost linearly; subcortical grey matter peaks at 14.4 y (14.0–14.7); total cerebrum volume at 12.5 y and cortical surface area at 11.0 y; cortical thickness at 1.7 y; white-matter volume at 28.7 y (28.1–29.2), declining faster after about 50. Mills et al. 2016 (*NeuroImage* 141:273–281, doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2016.07.044): four longitudinal samples (391 participants, 852 scans, ages 8–30): cortical grey-matter volume decreases through the second decade with deceleration in the third; white matter rises to mid-to-late adolescence before decelerating. (Where "about 25" comes from — I did not trace it [~].) 
- [✗] ADDED: "We lose thousands of neurons every day / most neurons die with age." Pakkenberg et al. 2003 (*Exp Gerontol* 38:95–99, doi:10.1016/s0531-5565(02)00151-1): stereology: the difference in total neocortical neuron number was "less than 10%" across ages 20–90; neocortical glia ~36 billion (mean age 89) vs 39 billion (mean age 26), not significantly different.
- [✗] ADDED: "Taxi drivers (or musicians) have bigger hippocampi" as a plain fact: Maguire 2000 found no difference in overall hippocampal volume — a posterior increase with an anterior decrease; and see Thomas & Baker 2013 (C3) for the generic caution.
- [✗] ADDED: "Neurogenesis is finished in childhood" and "you grow thousands of new neurons a day" are both overstated — see C4 (rare and contested).
- [✗] ADDED: "Glia outnumber neurons 10 to 1" — see A1 (≈ 1:1; 86 billion neurons vs ~85 billion nonneuronal cells in Azevedo 2009).
### Bet candidates (08)
- [V] Bet: neurons in a human brain? → 86.06 ± 8.12 billion (N = 4 men; range 78.8–95.4; Azevedo 2009); folklore says 100 billion. Surprise: even the "textbook" number was never measured.
- [V] ADDED: Bet: what share of the brain's neurons sits in the cerebellum? → 80% (69.03 of 86.06 billion) in 10% of brain mass; the cerebral cortex has 19% (Azevedo 2009).
- [V] ADDED: Bet: how many glial (non-neuronal) cells per neuron in the whole brain? → 0.99 (≈ 1:1), not 10 (Azevedo 2009; von Bartheld 2016). In the 1 mm³ cortical cube of Shapson-Coe 2024 it is 2:1 (32,315 glia vs 16,087 neurons).

- [V] ADDED: Bet: how long did it take Hodgkin and Huxley to compute ONE action potential by hand in 1951? → "a matter of days" for a membrane action potential, "a matter of weeks" for a propagated one (Huxley's Nobel lecture 1963).

### Widget constants (08): integrate-and-fire neuron
- [def] ADDED: Leaky integrate-and-fire (Lapicque 1907; Gerstner et al. 2014 Ch. 1): τm dV/dt = −(V − V_rest) + R·I(t); when V reaches the threshold ϑ a spike is emitted and V is reset to a value below threshold; after a spike the neuron is refractory for a short time. Constant input I gives an inter-spike interval T = t_ref + τm·ln[R·I / (R·I − (ϑ − V_rest))] for R·I > ϑ − V_rest (standard result for reset to rest; my own derivation — check by simulation); no spikes below R·I = ϑ − V_rest (rheobase). Worked example for the widget defaults below (V_rest −70, ϑ −55, τm 10 ms, t_ref 2 ms; derived): R·I = 15.5 mV → 27.5 Hz; 20 mV → 63 Hz; 30 mV → 112 Hz; 100 mV → 276 Hz; ceiling 1/t_ref = 500 Hz.
- [V] ★ Resting potential — default −70 mV. Sources (they differ; show a range): OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e, Ch. 12.4 (Betts et al. 2022, openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/12-4-the-action-potential): "−70 mV, the value described as the resting membrane potential"; Hodgkin's 1963 Nobel lecture: amphibian myelinated fibre resting potential 70 mV (squid axon 45–51 mV in the 1939 records); J. H. Byrne's open textbook *Neuroscience Online* (UTHealth McGovern Medical School; Section 1 Ch. 1 "Resting Potentials and Action Potentials", nba.uth.tmc.edu/neuroscience/m/s1/chapter01.html; page read 14 Oct 2026): "Here it is shown to be -60 mV, but can range between -80 mV and -40 mV, depending on the particular type of nerve cell"; Gerstner et al. 2014 Ch. 2: about −65 mV. OpenStax adds that "-70 mV is most commonly used as this value" and that the exact value varies between cells. Range to show: −80 to −40 mV; default −65 to −70.
- [V] ★ Threshold — default −55 mV. OpenStax: "Any depolarization that does not change the membrane potential to −55 mV or higher will not reach threshold" (i.e. a 15 mV depolarisation from −70); UTHealth *Neuroscience Online* Ch. 2 example: depolarisation "from −60 mV to −45 mV in order to reach threshold" (nba.uth.tmc.edu/neuroscience/m/s1/chapter02.html). Threshold is not a fixed constant of a neuron (depends on how fast V rises, recent spikes and cell type); range to show: −55 to −45 mV (fixed-threshold LIF is a simplification [def]).
- [V] ★ Spike peak — default +40 mV (+30 mV if matching OpenStax). OpenStax: "The membrane potential will reach +30 mV by the time sodium has entered the cell"; UTHealth Ch. 2: "approximately +40 mV", approaching but not reaching E_Na ≈ +55 mV; Hodgkin 1963: frog node action potential 120 mV on rest −70 mV → peak ≈ +50 mV; Gerstner: spike amplitude "about 100 mV". In a pure LIF model the spike is a reset event, so the peak is cosmetic — draw it at +30 to +40 mV.
- [V] Spike duration — 1–2 ms (Gerstner 2014: "typically a duration of 1-2 ms"; OpenStax: "approximately 2 milliseconds" for the whole event; UTHealth: "about one msec").
- [~] Absolute refractory period — default 2 ms (range 1–2 ms). Gerstner defines it ("minimal distance between two spikes") without a number on the pages read; PhysiologyWeb lecture notes (physiologyweb.com, secondary) give 1–2 ms with full recovery of sodium channels in about 3–4 ms; UTHealth says recovery from inactivation takes "time (several msec)". The 1/t_ref ceiling (500–1,000 spikes/s) is a derived upper bound; real cortical neurons rarely exceed ~100/s (Lennie 2003).
- [V] ★ Membrane time constant — default 10 ms. Gerstner 2014 Ch. 1: "For a typical neuron it is in the range of 10 ms" (long compared with a ~1 ms spike). The task-file range 10–20 ms is plausible but I could not source 20 ms from a primary text [~]. In an awake, busy cortex the effective constant is shorter: Destexhe & Paré 1999 (*J Neurophysiol* 81:1531–1547, doi:10.1152/jn.1999.81.4.1531) found synaptic activity gave "an approximately fivefold decrease in input resistance" in cat parietal pyramidal cells in vivo (so τm = R_in·C falls by roughly the same factor — derived, with membrane capacitance assumed fixed).
- [V] ADDED: Reset potential — not fixed by any source read; use V_rest or a few mV below (a modelling choice; say so). Input: a constant "R·I" in mV is the simplest control; one EPSP is a few mV at most (no source read — do not quote a number).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Spike timing window for a Hebbian-learning panel: ±20 ms (Bi & Poo 1998: post within 20 ms after pre → LTP, within 20 ms before → LTD); Markram 1997 as summarised by Markram et al. 2011: ≈10 ms offsets worked, 100 ms offsets gave no plasticity. STDP amplitudes (how much a weight changes per pairing) are modelling choices (Song, Miller & Abbott 2000 — not opened).
- [V] ADDED: Energy panel: 2.4 × 10^9 ATP per spike in a human pyramidal cell and 0.16 spikes/s/neuron sustainable (Lennie 2003); rodent signalling budget 47% action potentials / 34% postsynaptic effects / 13% resting potential / 3% glutamate recycling (Attwell & Laughlin 2001); "of the order of 20 W" for the whole brain is derived (see A2).
- [V] ADDED: Rate-code panel (Adrian 1932 Nobel lecture): constant spike size; frog muscle-spindle fibre fires ≈ 10/s at low stretch and ≥ 50/s at high stretch.

## 09 · learning
### A. Classical and instrumental foundations; Little Albert; latent learning
#### A1. Pavlov: what the 1904 prize was for, and the first words about "conditioned" reflexes
- [V] ★ CORRECTION: Pavlov's Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1904 was "in recognition of his work on the physiology of digestion, through which knowledge on vital aspects of the subject has been transformed and enlarged" (nobelprize.org summary page, Nobel Prize Outreach, fetched Oct 2026) — not for "classical conditioning". His Nobel Lecture (12 Dec 1904, "Physiology of Digestion", https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1904/pavlov/lecture/) is mostly about digestive glands; the conditioned reflex appears only in its last part, as a by-product of the digestion work. 巴甫洛夫诺贝尔奖(1904)是因消化生理学
- [V] ★ What the 1904 lecture says (English translation; exact fragments): the salivary reflex evoked from the mouth is "constant, unconditioned, while the new reflex continually fluctuates and, hence, is conditioned"; the distant properties of food (colour etc.) "appear here in some way as signals for the first"; repeated teasing with the sight of food makes the reaction "weaker and weaker" until it "drops to zero" (extinction) and it can be restored by letting the dog eat or by putting acid in its mouth. The lecture says "psychical stimulation" of the glands had been reported earlier, and that his team decided "to take an entirely objective point of view" and "ignored completely the mental state of the animal". (The popular phrase "psychic secretion" is NOT in this lecture; its literal source in Pavlov's own writing is unverified — see COULD NOT VERIFY.) 条件反射/无条件反射 (translation of the Russian "uslovnyi", conditional)
- [def] Terms (Craske et al. 2014): a neutral conditional stimulus (CS) followed by an unconditional stimulus (US) comes to elicit a conditional response (CR) because the CS predicts the US; exposure therapy is derived from extinction. In the 1904 lecture the examples of "signals" are the sight and smell of food ("from the eye or from the nose"), not a bell; later Pavlov-lab experiments used many CSs, e.g. the metronome and the black-square second-order experiment that Sutton & Barto 2018 (§14.2.1) describe ("In just ten trials" the dog salivated to the black square — second-hand [~]).

#### A2. Thorndike's law of effect and Skinner's operant
- [V] ★ Thorndike 1911, *Animal Intelligence: Experimental Studies*, New York: Macmillan, ch. V "Laws and hypotheses for behavior" (text read at psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm; the passage follows the page marker [p. 244]): the Law of Effect — of several responses made to the same situation, those "accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal" will, other things equal, be more firmly connected with the situation and more likely to recur; those followed by discomfort will have their connection weakened; "The greater the satisfaction or discomfort, the greater the strengthening or weakening of the bond" (exact). 效果律 The cat puzzle-box experiments behind it come from his 1898 monograph, *Psychological Review* Monograph Supplement 2(4, Whole No. 8), reprinted as ch. II of the 1911 book (Classics-site footnote).
- [def] Skinner 1938, *The Behavior of Organisms*, New York: Appleton-Century (book not opened): operant conditioning 操作性条件作用 = behaviour is shaped by its consequences (reinforcement raises, punishment lowers its future frequency); standard definition as in OpenStax Psychology 2e §6.3 (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett 2020, https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/6-3-operant-conditioning).

#### A3. Taste aversion: not every cue goes with every outcome (Garcia & Koelling 1966)
- [V] ★ Garcia & Koelling 1966, "Relation of cue to consequence in avoidance learning", *Psychonomic Science* 4(1):123–124, doi:10.3758/BF03342209 (2 pages; publisher PDF behind a bot challenge — not opened). As described in Eelen 2018 (open review, *Psychol Belg* 58(1):196–211, doi:10.5334/pb.451, CC BY): thirsty rats drank flavoured (saline) water while a light and noise accompanied each lick ("bright-noisy-tasty"); in groups made ill (X-rays, or lithium chloride) drinking later fell in the FLAVOUR test but not the audiovisual test, while in the group shocked 2 s after drinking the reverse occurred — "an interaction between the nature of the discriminative stimulus and the drinking consequences". 味觉厌恶/加西亚效应 Details of group sizes and numbers: not verified.
- [V] Eelen 2018 on status: the 1966 paper "was indeed rather weak at the methodological level" and had been rejected by a more renowned journal, but "sufficient subsequent independent replications" exist: "The phenomenon is real. The debate about its reach and interpretation, however, remains active today"; at least 600 follow-up articles; taste aversions can form with CS–US delays of up to 24 h ("records"), unlike most conditioning preparations; two families of explanation — biological specialisation of learning mechanisms vs the general laws of association with unusual stimulus properties.
- [V] ADDED: Humans: Bernstein 1978 ("Learned taste aversions in children receiving chemotherapy", *Science* 200(4347):1302–1303, doi:10.1126/science.663613): children with cancer were offered an unusual ice cream before drug treatment; those who experienced gastrointestinal toxicity were later less likely to choose that ice cream than controls (N not in abstract).
- STUDY Taste-aversion selectivity / "preparedness" (Garcia & Koelling 1966) — original: rats, X-ray/LiCl vs shock, flavour vs audiovisual cue. Later evidence: widely replicated flavour-illness learning with long delays in many species incl. humans (Bernstein 1978; Eelen 2018 review); the broader claim that learning laws are biologically specialised is still debated.
  GRADE B: the phenomenon (strong, fast, long-delay flavour–illness learning; cue–consequence selectivity) is robust; its interpretation and generality are disputed. — Eelen 2018; Bernstein 1978

#### A4. Watson & Rayner 1920, "Little Albert"
- [V] ★ Original: Watson & Rayner 1920, "Conditioned emotional reactions", *J Exp Psychol* 3(1):1–14 (text read: psychclassics.yorku.ca/Watson/emotion.htm). One infant, Albert B., described as "healthy from birth" and "stolid and unemotional"; at 11 months 3 days a white rat was presented and, as his hand touched it, a steel bar was struck with a hammer behind his head (2 pairings that day); a week later (11 months 10 days) 5 more pairings — "in all seven joint stimulations" — after which the rat alone made him cry and crawl away; fear was reported to transfer to a rabbit, a dog and other furry things; 31 days later reactions persisted "with a certain loss in the intensity"; Albert left the hospital before any "detachment" of the fear. The authors' claim that such responses "persist and modify personality throughout life" was speculation, untested. N = 1, no control child, no replication in infants.
- [V] ★ ADDED: The textbook version is wrong in places: Harris 1979 ("Whatever happened to Little Albert?", *Am Psychol* 34(2):151–160, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.34.2.151; abstract): reviews the actual procedures and the "distortion" of the study by Watson himself, textbook authors, behaviour therapists and a prominent learning theorist — "myth making". Griggs 2014 ("The continuing saga of Little Albert in introductory psychology textbooks", *Teach Psychol* 41(4):309–317, doi:10.1177/0098628314549702): of 23 current introductory texts, inaccuracies "especially concerning generalization" are still present in some and coverage of the recent identity/impairment debate is limited.
- [V] ★ Who was Albert, and was he healthy? (unsettled as of Oct 2026): Beck, Levinson & Irons 2009 (*Am Psychol* 64(7):605–614, doi:10.1037/a0017234) identified the child as Douglas Merritte (1919–1925); Fridlund, Beck, Goldie & Irons 2012 ("Little Albert: a neurologically impaired child", *Hist Psychol* 15(4):302–327, doi:10.1037/a0026720) argued from film footage and medical records (congenital hydrocephalus etc.) that he was NOT the healthy infant described and that Watson could have known; Powell, Digdon, Harris & Smithson 2014 (*Am Psychol* 69(6):600–611, doi:10.1037/a0036854) proposed instead Albert Barger as the better match; Digdon, Powell & Harris 2014 (*Hist Psychol* 17(4):312–324, doi:10.1037/a0037325) re-examined the impairment claims and arrive "at an alternative interpretation of Albert as a normal infant". Teach: identity and health are disputed; do not assert either as fact.
- STUDY "Little Albert" as evidence that fear is conditioned in infants — original: Watson & Rayner 1920, N = 1, 7 pairings, no control. Later evidence: no direct replication (not repeatable ethically; I found none); documented procedural and reporting distortions (Harris 1979; Griggs 2014); subject identity/health disputed (Beck 2009; Fridlund 2012; Powell 2014; Digdon 2014). That humans acquire fear by conditioning is separately well supported by controlled adult laboratory work (Lonsdorf et al. 2017, *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 77:247–285, doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2017.02.026; Vervliet 2013).
  GRADE D: as a stand-alone demonstration the study cannot carry the claims made for it; teach it as a historical case and a caution about textbook myths, not as evidence. — Harris 1979; Digdon 2014; Griggs 2014

#### A5. Latent learning (Blodgett, Tolman & Honzik)
- [V] ★ Tolman 1948 ("Cognitive maps in rats and men", *Psychol Rev* 55(4):189–208, doi:10.1037/h0061626; text read at psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm) summarises three experiments: Blodgett 1929 (six-unit alley maze; Group II was fed in the maze only from day 7, Group III from day 3; their error curves "did drop astoundingly" after the first food, so they had been learning while not showing it — "latent learning"); Tolman & Honzik 1930 (14-unit T-maze; groups never fed in the maze, always fed, and fed from day 11, which "showed the same sort of a sudden drop"; larger groups); and an unpublished Spence & Lippitt Y-maze study at Iowa (rats satiated for food and water during training; when later made only hungry they went to the food arm, when only thirsty to the water arm, "statistically more frequently"). Interpretation: a cognitive map used once motivation arrives. 潜伏学习/认知地图
- [V] ADDED: Later: latent-learning designs are still used, e.g. the rat "latent cue preference" task (Stouffer & Yoder 2011, *Neurobiol Aging* 32(12):2320.e11–e14, doi:10.1016/j.neurobiolaging.2010.04.021; Stouffer & Klein 2013, *Acta Neurobiol Exp* 73(3):430–437), where pre-exposure to cues without reward affects later performance; abstracts only.
- STUDY Latent learning (learning without reinforcement shows up when motivation or reward is introduced) — original: Blodgett 1929; Tolman & Honzik 1930; Spence & Lippitt (three labs, Tolman 1948). Later evidence: modern rodent latent-learning/latent-cue tasks (Stouffer 2011, 2013); the cognitive-map interpretation (versus incentive or S–R accounts) was disputed and I did not find a meta-analysis.
  GRADE B: that rats learn in the absence of reward is well supported by several labs; whether this proves "maps" is theory; no meta-analysis found. — Tolman 1948; Stouffer & Klein 2013
### B. Blocking, contingency, Rescorla-Wagner and successors
#### B1. Kamin blocking 阻断效应: design and numbers (rats, conditioned suppression 条件性抑制)
- [V] ★ Source note: the chapter everyone cites is Kamin LJ 1969, "Predictability, surprise, attention, and conditioning", in Campbell BA & Church RM (eds.), *Punishment and Aversive Behavior*, New York: Appleton-Century-Crofts (pp. 279–296, as given in the reference list of Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997 [V]; Maes et al. 2016 list it without pages). The text I OPENED is Kamin's own 1967 symposium version of that chapter (Symposium on Punishment, Princeton, May 1967; McMaster Univ. Dept of Psychology Technical Report No. 13; NASA NTRS NASA-CR-94558, http://hdl.handle.net/2060/19680014821). Every Kamin number below is read from that report (page numbers = the report's own); the 1969 chapter itself was NOT opened. Kamin attributes the first description of blocking to "Kamin, 1968" (a fuller earlier paper, not opened).
- [V] ★ Method (report pp. 2–4): hooded rats kept at 75% of free-feeding weight, bar-pressing for food on a variable-interval 2.5-min schedule in 2-h daily sessions; conditioned emotional response (CER 条件性情绪反应; Estes & Skinner 1941) with 4 CS–US sequences a day regardless of behaviour; CS = 3 min of white noise (typically 80 dB), a house light, or both together; US = 0.5-s shock, typically 1 mA; 8–20 rats per group. Measure = suppression ratio 抑制比 B/(A+B) (B = bar presses during the 3-min CS, A = in the 3 min just before): .50 = no effect of the CS, ≈.00 = complete suppression. Test = one non-reinforced test day; reported = the group median on the FIRST test trial.
- [V] ★ THE result (p. 5 table; median suppression ratio on the first test trial to the LIGHT, L): Group G (control: 8 reinforced noise+light compound trials, nothing before) .05; Group B (BLOCKING group: 16 noise-alone → 8 compound trials) .45; Group 2-B (24 noise-alone trials, light never added) .44; Group A (8 compound trials → then 16 noise-alone; test 4 days after the last compound trial) .25. Kamin's reading: B and 2-B are equivalent, so the 8 compound trials "produced literally no conditioning to the light" (p. 6, paraphrase-exact: "literally no conditioning to the light"); both sit slightly below .50 because a first light onset is mildly disruptive on its own; A's .25 is attributed to forgetting over the 4-day gap (a recency effect that works against the A-vs-B difference).
- [V] ★ Same number of light+noise+shock pairings (8) in G and B, yet .05 vs .45: pairing is not sufficient for conditioning; what matters is whether the US was already predicted. Blocking is symmetric: with the roles of light and noise reversed (4 new groups) prior conditioning to the light produced "a total block" of conditioning to the noise (p. 6, citing Kamin 1968). 
- [V] ★ Not a failure to notice the new cue (the "transitional trial", pp. 9–10): across 153 rats given 16 noise-alone trials then ≥1 compound trial, the median ratio was .02 on the 16th noise trial and .15 on the first compound trial (before the outcome of that trial could matter); 106 rats higher, 17 lower, 30 equal. Kamin: the observation is "fatal to our original theoretical notions" (his first idea was that the trained cue captures attention so the added cue is not noticed).
- [V] The added cue is used instantly when it carries news (pp. 8–9): Group Y (16 noise → compound NOT reinforced ×4) vs Group Z (16 noise → noise NOT reinforced ×12): both .02 on trial 16; on the first non-reinforced trial .18 (Y) vs .01 (Z); on the second .31 vs .02 (significant) — the rats had noticed the light and learned in one trial that it signalled no shock.
- [V] ★ Unblocking 去阻断 by surprise (p. 20 table; first-test ratio to the light): Group B (noise at 1 mA ×16 → compound at 1 mA ×8) .45; Group 2-M (noise 1 mA ×16 → compound at 4 mA ×8) .14; Group 3-U (noise at 4 mA ×8 → compound at 4 mA ×8) .36. Raising the shock at the compound stage "virtually" eliminates the block (2-M vs B significant); an intense shock throughout does not (3-U vs B not significant). Kamin (p. 20): it is "the change of shock intensity during the compound trials" that removes the block — i.e. blocking happens "because of the redundancy of the superimposed element".
- [V] ★ Kamin's own formulation of "only surprise teaches" (pp. 21–22): for an associative increment to occur, "the US instigate some 'mental work'"; this "mental work will occur only if the US is unpredicted" (exact fragments; "if it in some sense 'surprises' the animal"). He offers it as a conception, not a proof, and notes it has moved "some distance from the notion of attention" (p. 22); it anticipates the Rescorla–Wagner idea.
- [V] Savings (pp. 17–19): after noise ×16 → compound ×8, learning the light alone is slightly faster than in rats that never saw the light: median ratio over 4 light-conditioning trials .28 (Group 2-A: N16, LN8, L4) vs .38 (2-B: N24, L4); Group 2-N (N16, LN×1, N×7, L4) also .28 — i.e. the whole savings comes from the single "transitional" compound trial, when the US is partly surprising. A block is nearly but not strictly all-or-none.
- [V] Overshadowing 遮蔽 in the same report (pp. 11–17; Pavlov 1927 pp. 141ff. as cited by Kamin): naive rats, 16 compound trials of 80 dB noise + light → .05 to light, .25 to noise (both conditioned); light + a weak 50 dB noise (8 trials) → .03 to light and .42 to noise (noise nearly "blotted out"); with a 4 mA US virtually total suppression to every element. Kamin concludes overshadowing is not a direct consequence of relative intensity but of different acquisition rates (the faster element "blocks" the slower one).
- [V] Parameter notes Kamin reports (pp. 10–11): a complete block with a 1-min instead of 3-min CS; with a 3 mA US throughout; with "turning off of a background 80 dB noise" as the first cue; the block stays total with many more compound trials; it is partial if conditioning to A stopped before asymptote and varies smoothly with amount of prior training; it disappears if A is extinguished BEFORE compound training but stays if A is extinguished AFTER. His summary: "the blocking phenomenon is robust, and easily reproducible" (exact; compare the 2016 replication series below).

#### B2. Does blocking replicate? (the 2016–2018 dispute)
- [V] ★ ADDED: Maes, Boddez, Alfei, Krypotos, D'Hooge, De Houwer & Beckers 2016, "The elusive nature of the blocking effect: 15 failures to replicate", *J Exp Psychol Gen* 145(9):e49–e71, doi:10.1037/xge0000200 (PMID 27428670; accepted manuscript read: https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/7241679/file/7241692.pdf): 15 experiments in rodents — mice (C57BL/6J; Exps 1–4), rats at UCLA (5–6) and rats at KU Leuven (7–15); aversive conditioned suppression (Exps 1–10) and appetitive procedures (11–15), designs "highly similar or identical" to published blocking studies (Exp 15 replicated a published report "to the letter", Taylor et al. 2008, *Learn Behav* 36:145–148). Not one experiment gave a significant blocking effect (one-tailed, p < .05); for the closest replication (a published report copied "in great detail") the authors estimate power "well above .90", and their power analyses (Appendix E) make low power an unlikely explanation for the whole series; Bayesian meta-analytic Bayes factor BF10 = 0.13 (substantial evidence for no effect; 0.16 without possible floor-effect experiments; 0.20 using only the 6 experiments free of floor/ceiling concerns).
- [V] ADDED: The authors do not deny that blocking exists: "from the abundance of successful studies … undisputable that blocking is a genuine and important phenomenon" (paraphrase of p. 30), but conclude it is "a highly parameter-dependent phenomenon" (exact fragment) and suspect publication bias; they call for re-evaluating blocking's "canonical" status as the touchstone for learning theories.
- [V] ★ ADDED: Counter-argument: Soto 2018, "Contemporary associative learning theory predicts failures to obtain blocking: Comment on Maes et al. (2016)", *J Exp Psychol Gen* 147(4):597–602, doi:10.1037/xge0000341: theory itself predicts small or no blocking when the compound AX uses same-modality stimuli (10 of the 15 experiments), 3 more had floor effects, only 2 are unexplained. Reply: Maes et al. 2018, *J Exp Psychol Gen* 147(4):603–610, doi:10.1037/xge0000413 (disagree on theoretical, empirical and statistical grounds; call for empirical tests of boundary conditions). As of Oct 2026 I found no large pre-registered multi-lab blocking replication in animals ([~], searched PubMed/Europe PMC titles only).
- [V] ADDED: Blocking's neural and human evidence (independent of Kamin's behaviour): monkeys' dopamine neurons (Waelti, Dickinson & Schultz 2001, below); rats with optogenetic dopamine stimulation (Steinberg et al. 2013, below); humans — Tobler, O'Doherty, Dolan & Schultz 2006, *J Neurophysiol* 95(1):301–310, doi:10.1152/jn.00762.2005: behavioural blocking with juice rewards; fMRI responses in medial orbitofrontal cortex and ventral putamen were lower to blocked than to non-blocked reward-predicting stimuli. Elsewhere a spatial-learning blocking task in healthy adults failed to produce blocking (Deery & Commins 2025, *Brain Sci* 15:414, doi:10.3390/brainsci15040414 — abstract title only, not opened).
- STUDY Kamin blocking — original: Kamin 1967/1968/1969; rats, CER; 8–20 per group; first-test median suppression ratio to the added cue .45 (blocked) vs .05 (control), 4 mA-for-1 mA shock increase removes it (.14). Later evidence: replicated across species and methods (monkey dopamine recordings, Waelti 2001; human fMRI, Tobler 2006; rat optogenetics, Steinberg 2013); but 15 consecutive failures in rodents (Maes 2016, BF10 = 0.13) answered by a boundary-condition account (Soto 2018).
  GRADE B: the principle "a fully predicted outcome teaches little" is supported in many labs and species, but the size and even the occurrence of the textbook "complete block" depend on parameters that are not yet pinned down; teach the Kamin numbers as a best-case demonstration and say so. — Maes 2016; Soto 2018; Waelti 2001; Tobler 2006

#### B3. Contingency 列联性, not pairings (Rescorla 1968) and the Rescorla–Wagner model
- [V] ★ Rescorla 1968 ("Probability of shock in the presence and absence of CS in fear conditioning", *J Comp Physiol Psychol* 66(1):1–5, doi:10.1037/h0025984; PMID 5672628; abstract text via OpenAlex/PsycINFO, full paper not opened): rats, CER. Exp 1: equal probability of shock in the presence and absence of a tone CS produced NO suppression to the CS although CS–US pairings occurred; the same probability given only during the CS produced "substantial" conditioning. Exp 2: four combinations of P(US | CS) and P(US | no CS): conditioning rose with P(US | CS), fell with P(US | no CS), and when the two were equal there was none. Rescorla's definition: contingency = the probability of the US in the presence of the CS relative to its probability in its absence.
- [V] ADDED: Rescorla 1988, "Pavlovian conditioning: it's not what you think it is", *Am Psychol* 43(3):151–160, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.43.3.151 (abstract): the traditional description of conditioning as the acquired ability of one stimulus to evoke the original response of another "because of their pairing" is "inadequate"; conditioning is "now described as the learning of relations among events so as to allow the organism to represent its environment".
- [V] ★ ADDED: Dispute: Papini & Bitterman 1990, "The role of contingency in classical conditioning", *Psychol Rev* 97(3):396–403, doi:10.1037/0033-295x.97.3.396: reconsider the early random-control experiments and later ones showing conditioning in random training; conclude contingency is "neither necessary nor sufficient for conditioning". Balsam & Gallistel 2009 (*Trends Neurosci* 32(2):73–78, doi:10.1016/j.tins.2008.10.004) argue instead for learning of temporal maps whose speed scales with the informativeness of the CS–US relation, which they say also yields the cue-competition results behind Rescorla–Wagner; Harris & Gallistel 2026 (*eLife* 13, doi:10.7554/eLife.102155): 14 groups of rats trained with CS informativeness (C/T = US–US interval ÷ CS–US interval) from 1.5 to 300 — the number of trials before responding began was set by C/T, with the same scalar relation as in pigeons (Gibbon & Balsam 1981), "the findings establish that animals encode rates of reinforcement" (authors’ wording; an information-rate account, not a prediction-error one). Qian et al. 2025 (*Nat Neurosci* 28(6):1280–1292, doi:10.1038/s41593-025-01915-4): in mouse Pavlovian contingency degradation, CS licking and ventral-striatum dopamine fell when extra rewards came uncued but not when cued — consistent with TD learning given a suitable inter-trial-interval state, in conflict with traditional contingency definitions and with the ANCCR causal-learning model.
- STUDY Rescorla 1968 contingency — original: rats, CER (N per group not read). Later evidence: contingency effects are a standard result of animal learning (Rescorla 1988 review) and recently reproduced in dopamine data (Qian 2025), but whether "contingency" itself or trial-level error correction / temporal informativeness is the operative variable is disputed (Papini & Bitterman 1990; Balsam & Gallistel 2009).
  GRADE B: the empirical regularity (conditioning follows how much a cue improves prediction, not how often it is paired) is well supported; the contingency concept as an explanation is contested. — Rescorla 1988; Papini & Bitterman 1990; Qian 2025
- [V] ★ Rescorla & Wagner 1972, "A theory of Pavlovian conditioning: variations in the effectiveness of reinforcement and nonreinforcement", in Black AH & Prokasy WF (eds.), *Classical Conditioning II: Current Research and Theory*, New York: Appleton-Century-Crofts, pp. 64–99 (pages confirmed in the reference list of Maes et al. 2016; chapter itself not opened). Core idea as paraphrased by Sutton & Barto 2018 (§14.2.2): "an animal only learns when events violate its expectations, in other words, only when the animal is surprised". 雷斯科拉–瓦格纳模型
- [V] ★ THE EQUATION. Sutton & Barto 2018, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*, 2nd ed., MIT Press, §14.2.2 pp. 346–348 (http://incompleteideas.net/book/the-book-2nd.html), in Rescorla–Wagner's own terms: for a compound CS AX followed by US Y, ΔV_A = α_AY (R_Y − V_AX) and ΔV_X = α_XY (R_Y − V_AX), with the "key assumption" V_AX = V_A + V_X; α_AY, α_XY are step-size parameters depending on the identities of the CS component and the US, and R_Y is "the asymptotic level of associative strength that the US Y can support" (Rescorla & Wagner wrote λ where S&B write R). Only components PRESENT on the trial are updated; the same error term (R_Y − V_AX) multiplies every present cue. In vector form (S&B eq. 14.2–14.3): w ← w + α δ x, δ = R − w·x. The model is the Widrow–Hoff (LMS) error-correction rule.
- [V] The same equation in the form most papers use, ΔV = αβ(λ − ΣV): α = the CS's salience / learning rate for cues, β = the learning-rate parameter of the US (outcome), λ = the asymptote the US supports (λ = 1 when the outcome occurs, 0 when it does not), ΣV = associative strength summed over all cues present on the trial — Brudan, Eisenbarth & Glautier 2025, *Learn Behav*, doi:10.3758/s13420-025-00681-4 ("canonical form … Rescorla & Wagner 1972"); Avraham et al. 2022, *eLife* 11:e75801, doi:10.7554/eLife.75801 (same parameter meanings). Brudan et al. constrained fitted learning rates to [0.0001, 0.75]. (Textbook demo values and the 0–1 convention: see Widget constants (09).)
- [V] ★ Why it explains blocking: while V_AX is below the asymptote R_Y the error is positive and all present cues gain; once A alone has taught V_A ≈ R_Y, the error on AX trials is ≈ 0 so the new cue X gains almost nothing ("the error has already been reduced to zero, or to a low value … Prior learning blocks learning to the new component", S&B p. 347, exact fragment). Unblocking falls out too: raise R_Y (a bigger shock) and the error reappears. S&B also note the model "showed how a 'mechanistic' theory could account for the main facts about blocking" without scanning memory backward or explicit recognition of the added cue.
- [V] Limits: S&B (p. 348): RW is a trial-level model (nothing about timing within a trial) and has no mechanism for higher-order conditioning; "not a complete or perfect model"; the TD model extends it to within-trial timing and second-order conditioning. Miller, Barnet & Grahame 1995 (*Psychol Bull* 117(3):363–386, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.363; abstract): RW is "the most influential theory of associative learning to emerge from the study of animal behavior over the last 25 years" and has had a "positive influence", but should "neither lead to the model being regarded as inherently 'correct'" nor serve as a benchmark for other models (paraphrase of the last sentence). Brudan et al. 2025: the simple elemental RW fits human super-/deepened-extinction recovery data worse than configural variants.
- STUDY Rescorla–Wagner model — original: Rescorla & Wagner 1972 (formal model; explains blocking, overshadowing, conditioned inhibition, overexpectation in one rule). Later evidence: Miller et al. 1995 review lists predictive successes and failures; extended by attention models, TD, configural models.
  GRADE B: its core idea (learning ∝ prediction error on a summed prediction) survives in dopamine and human neuroimaging data, but as a literal model of conditioning it has well-documented failures. — Miller 1995; Sutton & Barto 2018

#### B4. Successors: attention models and TD
- [V] Mackintosh 1975 ("A theory of attention: variations in the associability of stimuli with reinforcement", *Psychol Rev* 82(4):276–298, doi:10.1037/h0076778): adds a stimulus-specific learning-rate parameter α that increases for good predictors and decreases for irrelevant ones, with an inverse relation between α of different stimuli (used to explain overshadowing and blocking). Pearce & Hall 1980 (*Psychol Rev* 87(6):532–552, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.87.6.532): "a CS will lose associability when its consequences are accurately predicted" (exact fragment of the abstract); the reinforcer's effectiveness stays constant.
- [V] ★ ADDED: Humans: Le Pelley, Mitchell, Beesley, George & Wills 2016 ("Attention and associative learning in humans: an integrative review", *Psychol Bull* 142(10):1111–1140, doi:10.1037/bul0000064): attention is biased toward reliable predictors (Mackintosh-style "learned predictiveness"), more so for high-value outcomes; the opposite Pearce–Hall idea that uncertainty raises attention "receives less support" in human data.
- [V] Temporal-difference (TD) learning: Sutton 1988 ("Learning to predict by the methods of temporal differences", *Machine Learning* 3:9–44, doi:10.1007/BF00115009; metadata only) is the real-time generalisation of the RW error rule; Sutton & Barto 2018 §14.2.3 describe the TD model of classical conditioning (real-time, bootstrapping, accounts for higher-order conditioning and timing). The dopamine/TD link is in the next section.
- [V] ★ Dopamine neurons obey the blocking logic: Waelti, Dickinson & Schultz 2001 ("Dopamine responses comply with basic assumptions of formal learning theory", *Nature* 412(6842):43–48, doi:10.1038/35083500; PMID 11452299): monkeys, blocking procedure; dopamine neurons' responses to conditioned stimuli were "governed differentially by the occurrence of reward prediction errors rather than stimulus-reward associations alone"; behavioural and neuronal learning occurred predominantly when dopamine neurons registered a prediction error at reward time (abstract wording).
- [V] ★ Causal tests: Steinberg, Keiflin, Boivin, Witten, Deisseroth & Janak 2013 ("A causal link between prediction errors, dopamine neurons and learning", *Nat Neurosci* 16(7):966–973, doi:10.1038/nn.3413; PMID 23708143): in rats, optogenetic activation of dopamine neurons at reward delivery, mimicking a prediction error, was "sufficient to cause long-lasting increases in cue-elicited reward-seeking behavior" in the blocking (and extinction) paradigm, i.e. it unblocked learning. Chang et al. 2016 (*Nat Neurosci* 19(1):111–116, doi:10.1038/nn.4191): brief optogenetic pauses in VTA dopamine firing at reward time mimic negative prediction errors in an over-expectation task. Sharpe et al. 2017 (*Nat Neurosci* 20(5):735–742, doi:10.1038/nn.4538): in sensory preconditioning (stimulus–stimulus, no reward) prediction errors "can be blocked behaviorally and reinstated by optogenetically activating dopamine neurons", and suppressing dopamine at the transition impairs the learning — dopamine transients are "sufficient and necessary". Maes et al. 2020 (*Nat Neurosci* 23(2):176–178, doi:10.1038/s41593-019-0574-1): shunting dopamine at the start of a reward-predicting cue prevents second-order conditioning without affecting blocking → cue-evoked transients behave as TD errors, not as reward predictions.
- STUDY Dopamine transients as causal prediction-error teaching signals (blocking/unblocking logic) — original: Waelti 2001 (monkey recordings); Steinberg 2013 (rat optogenetics). Later evidence: Chang 2016, Sharpe 2017, Maes 2020 (all rat optogenetics, Schoenbaum/Janak labs); human drug/fMRI work (Pessiglione 2006; Tobler 2006). Not yet independently replicated in a large multi-lab effort; most causal tests come from 2–3 labs.
  GRADE B: converging causal evidence in several designs; narrow set of labs and tasks, and the same Maes 2016 caveat about blocking itself applies. — Steinberg 2013; Sharpe 2017; Maes 2020
### C. Dopamine and prediction error (Schultz) and the live dispute
#### C1. The 1997 finding and the TD model (primary text read: Gatsby-hosted PDF of Science 275:1593–1599)
- [V] ★ Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997, "A neural substrate of prediction and reward", *Science* 275(5306):1593–1599, doi:10.1126/science.275.5306.1593 (PMID 9054347). Experimental part (a review of Schultz-lab single-neuron recordings in alert MONKEYS, not new data): midbrain (VTA / substantia nigra) dopamine neurons burst to unpredicted juice or food; after days of training on a light → reward task the burst moves to the light and the predicted reward no longer elicits one; if the reward is omitted the neuron is "depressed markedly below" its basal rate at exactly the time the reward was due (the dip occurs >1 s after the light with nothing in between). Their summary: positive signal if an event is better than predicted, "no signal" if as predicted, a negative signal (decreased spiking) if worse. 多巴胺奖赏预测误差
- [V] ★ The population number in that paper: the responses "occur in the majority of dopamine neurons (55 to 80%)" (exact) and are described as "relatively homogeneous". Aversive air puffs or saline "do not cause these same transient activations", and "very few dopamine neurons respond to stimuli that predict aversive outcomes" (exact fragments). Dopamine neurons are also activated by novel stimuli, but for most stimuli "only a few presentations". (1997-era claims; heterogeneity later qualified — see C3.)
- [V] ★ The model (their eqs. 1–5): V(t) = E[γ⁰r(t) + γ¹r(t+1) + γ²r(t+2) + …] with 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1 (discounted sum of future rewards; eq. 1); consistency V(t) = E[r(t) + γV(t+1)] (eq. 2); TD error δ(t) = r(t) + γV̂(t+1) − V̂(t) (eq. 3); prediction V̂(t) = Σ_i w_i x_i(t) over a "complete serial compound" time-tagged cue (eq. 4); weight update Δw_i = α_x Σ_t x_i(t) δ(t) (eq. 5). Dopamine output is hypothesised to be δ(t) + b(t) with b(t) the basal firing rate (so negative errors = dips below baseline). Their trained weights are offered as the model's explanation of "two well-described behavioral effects — blocking and secondary conditioning" (exact fragment: "two well-described behavioral effects"). They also predict (i) with several cues the burst transfers to the earliest consistent cue, (ii) omission of an intermediate cue gives a dip at the time it was due. (S&B 2018 footnote p. 381: because the error needs V(t+1), "the TD error available at t is actually δ_{t−1}" — a one-step shift.)
- [V] Schultz 1998 ("Predictive reward signal of dopamine neurons", *J Neurophysiol* 80(1):1–27, doi:10.1152/jn.1998.80.1.1, PMID 9658025; abstract): "Most dopamine neurons show phasic activations" to rewards and reward-predicting cues, "only few" to aversive stimuli, biphasic responses to novel or salient stimuli; dopamine neurons are "activated by rewarding events that are better than predicted, remain uninfluenced by events that are as good as predicted, and are depressed by events that are worse than predicted"; the responses "have the formal characteristics of a teaching signal postulated by reinforcement learning theories" — and, even in this review, dopamine neurons "signal alerting and motivating events".
- [V] ★ Who proposed the hypothesis: Montague, Dayan & Sejnowski 1996 ("A framework for mesencephalic dopamine systems based on predictive Hebbian learning", *J Neurosci* 16(5):1936–1947, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.16-05-01936.1996, PMID 8774460) — per Sutton & Barto 2018 §15.3 the first explicit statement of the reward prediction error hypothesis (TD error ↔ phasic dopamine); abstract: "fluctuations in the activity levels of neurons in diffuse dopamine systems above and below baseline levels would represent errors in these predictions", and fluctuations in dopamine release "can act to change the predictions" through synaptic plasticity. S&B (p. 383): the hypothesis "has received wide acceptance among neuroscientists studying reward-based learning, and … proven to be remarkably resilient"; but "not every dopamine neuron" behaved in all four ways, and there are "discrepancies" that parameter tuning cannot absorb.

#### C2. Replications, causal tests and human evidence (the core finding stands)
- [V] ★ ADDED: Identified dopamine neurons, mice: Cohen, Haesler, Vong, Lowell & Uchida 2012 (*Nature* 482(7383):85–88, doi:10.1038/nature10754): of recorded VTA neurons 52% were "type I" (phasic RPE-like), 31% / 18% had sustained delay activity modulated positively / negatively by outcome value; "All identified dopaminergic neurons were of type I" (optogenetically tagged), GABA neurons carried expected value. (52/31/18% are shares of ALL recorded VTA neurons, not of dopamine neurons.) Eshel et al. 2015 (*Nature* 525:243–246, doi:10.1038/nature14855): dopamine neurons compute prediction error by subtraction; VTA GABA neurons are a source of the expected-reward term.
- [V] ADDED: Dopamine RELEASE: Hart, Rutledge, Glimcher & Phillips 2014 (*J Neurosci* 34(3):698–704, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2489-13.2014): fast-scan cyclic voltammetry in rat nucleus accumbens core — reward-evoked dopamine "transmit[s] a bidirectional RPE signal with symmetrical encoding of positive and negative RPEs". Bayer & Glimcher 2005 (*Neuron* 47(1):129–141, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2005.05.020): monkey dopamine firing equals reward minus a weighted average of past rewards "but only for outcomes that were better than expected" (firing floor limits the negative range) — so no single "percent of neurons coding negative RPE" exists. Tobler, Fiorillo & Schultz 2005 (*Science* 307:1642–1645, doi:10.1126/science.1105370): responses adapt their gain to the variance of reward value.
- [V] ADDED: Humans: Pessiglione, Seymour, Flandin, Dolan & Frith 2006 (*Nature* 442(7106):1042–1045, doi:10.1038/nature05051): L-DOPA (raises) vs haloperidol (lowers) modulated the magnitude of striatal reward prediction errors and choice of the most rewarding action; adding the prediction-error magnitude to a standard learning model reproduced behaviour under both drugs. Corlett, Mollick & Kober 2022 (*Neuropsychopharmacology* 47(7):1339–1349, doi:10.1038/s41386-021-01264-3): MKDA meta-analysis of 264 human fMRI prediction-error studies (1999–2018) found midbrain prediction-error signals in cognitive and reward learning tasks and insula signals for perceptual, social, cognitive and reward errors; plus domain-specific errors (visual hierarchy, dorsomedial PFC). Stauffer et al. 2016 (*Cell* 166(6):1564–1571, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2016.08.024): in rhesus macaques optical stimulation added to reward outcomes promoted learning of reward-predicting stimuli (the causal tests of 09 B4 were in rodents).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Where the field stands (2026): Dudhabhate & Costa 2026 ("A brief history of dopamine prediction errors", *Front Comput Neurosci* 20:1825622, doi:10.3389/fncom.2026.1825622): over three decades experiments "have both replicated the core dopamine RPE finding across distinct experimental contexts and revealed important deviations from the canonical model predictions"; the field is "in the transition period to a new paradigm in which the TDRL framework will be significantly improved or abandoned altogether"; they call Steinberg 2013 an "instant classic".
- STUDY Phasic midbrain dopamine activity behaves like a reward prediction error in reward-learning tasks — original: Schultz 1997/98 (monkeys; 55–80% of neurons recorded showed the responses). Later evidence: identified-neuron recordings in mice (Cohen 2012; Eshel 2015), voltammetry (Hart 2014), monkey and rodent optogenetics (Stauffer 2016; Steinberg 2013; Chang 2016; Maes 2020), human drug + fMRI studies (Pessiglione 2006) and a 264-study fMRI meta-analysis (Corlett 2022); a decade of reviews (Glimcher 2011; Gershman 2024; Dudhabhate & Costa 2026) agree the core is replicated.
  GRADE A: independent labs, species (monkey, rat, mouse, human), methods (spikes, release, causal manipulation, imaging) agree on the direction (bursts to unexpected reward, dips to omissions, transfer to cues); the exact size of the dip and the share of responding neurons vary by task and cell group. — Cohen 2012; Hart 2014; Steinberg 2013; Corlett 2022

#### C3. Deviations from the canonical model (what "RPE is too simple" means)
- [V] ★ ADDED: Gershman, Assad, Datta, Linderman, Sabatini, Uchida & Wilbrecht 2024 ("Explaining dopamine through prediction errors and beyond", *Nat Neurosci* 27(9):1645–1655, doi:10.1038/s41593-024-01705-4; PMID 39054370): the RPE account of phasic dopamine "in its original form, probably too simple"; its three challenges (ramps toward rewards, responses to sensory and motor features, influence on action selection) can be answered by a suitably generalised prediction-error concept — "Nonetheless" some findings "appear to demand fundamentally different theoretical explanations beyond encoding RPE".
- [V] ADDED: Heterogeneity: Matsumoto & Hikosaka 2009 (*Nature* 459:837–841, doi:10.1038/nature08028): monkeys; a subset of dopamine neurons fit "value" coding (excited by reward-predicting, inhibited by airpuff-predicting cues) but "a greater number … were excited by both"; airpuff-excited neurons lay more dorsolaterally in the substantia nigra pars compacta. Engelhard et al. 2019 (*Nature* 570:509–513, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1261-9): two-photon imaging of >300 VTA dopamine neurons in mice in a virtual-reality decision task: neurons encoded "an array of sensory, motor and cognitive variables"; the responses were functionally clustered (subpopulations carry a subset of those variables in addition to reward) and spatially organised. Schultz 2016 (*Nat Rev Neurosci* 17(3):183–195, doi:10.1038/nrn.2015.26): the phasic response has two components — an initial brief, unselective, highly sensitive detection response that "quickly evolves" into the value/utility-coding component.
- [V] Ramps: Howe, Tierney, Sandberg, Phillips & Graybiel 2013 (*Nature* 500:575–579, doi:10.1038/nature12475): in rats running mazes, striatal dopamine (voltammetry) "gradually increased" as animals approached distant rewards, scaling with distance and size of reward — a prolonged signal, not a phasic burst. Hamid et al. 2016 (*Nat Neurosci* 19(1):117–126, doi:10.1038/nn.4173): accumbens dopamine over minutes covaried with reward rate and vigor, over seconds "encoded an estimate of temporally discounted future reward (a value function)", and altering it changed willingness to work and "reinforced preceding action choices by encoding temporal-difference reward prediction errors" — "a single, rapidly evolving decision variable". Kim et al. 2020 (*Cell* 183(6):1600–1616, doi:10.1016/j.cell.2020.11.013): virtual-reality designs separating RPE from value show ramps "consistent with RPEs rather than value" at every stage recorded (a derivative-like computation over value).
- [V] Release ≠ spikes: Mohebi et al. 2019 ("Dissociable dopamine dynamics for learning and motivation", *Nature* 570:65–70, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1235-y): identified VTA dopamine cell spiking and accumbens dopamine release recorded in the same task — cue-related bursts raised both, but accumbens-core release also tracked evolving reward expectation "without corresponding changes in VTA dopamine cell spiking": "broadcast burst signals promote learning, whereas local control drives motivation."
- [V] Newer theories: Dabney, Kurth-Nelson, Uchida, Starkweather, Hassabis, Munos & Botvinick 2020 (*Nature* 577:671–675, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1924-6): the brain may represent future reward as a probability distribution rather than a single mean; tested with single-unit recordings from mouse VTA: "strong evidence for a neural realization of distributional reinforcement learning" (abstract; I found no independent replication — [~]). Starkweather, Babayan, Uchida & Gershman 2017 (*Nat Neurosci* 20:581–589, doi:10.1038/nn.4520): dopamine RPEs reflect hidden-state ("belief state") inference. Jeong et al. 2022 (*Science* 378:eabq6740, doi:10.1126/science.abq6740): accumbens dopamine release "conveys causal associations but not RPE" (retrospective causal-learning algorithm), "thereby challenging the dominant theory"; answered by Qian et al. 2025 (*Nat Neurosci* 28:1280–1292, doi:10.1038/s41593-025-01915-4): a TD model with a suitable inter-trial-interval state fits the same contingency-degradation data, the ANCCR model does not.

#### C4. RPE vs incentive salience vs motivation: the live dispute and the middle ground
- [V] ★ Incentive-salience camp: Berridge & Robinson 1998 ("What is the role of dopamine in reward: hedonic impact, reward learning, or incentive salience?", *Brain Res Rev* 28(3):309–369, doi:10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8; PMID 9858756): rats depleted of accumbens/neostriatal dopamine by up to 99% (6-OHDA) kept normal "taste reactivity" hedonic reactions to sucrose vs quinine, normal learning of new hedonic values and normal benzodiazepine hedonic enhancement; they conclude dopamine mediates neither hedonic impact nor hedonic learning but "incentive salience" ('wanting'). Berridge 2007 ("The debate over dopamine's role in reward: the case for incentive salience", *Psychopharmacology* 191(3):391–431, doi:10.1007/s00213-006-0578-x): dopamine "neither necessary nor sufficient" for hedonic 'liking'; "not needed for new learning, and not sufficient to directly mediate learning by causing teaching or prediction signals"; but necessary for normal 'wanting'. 欲求 wanting / 喜爱 liking
- [V] ADDED: Berridge & Kringelbach 2015 ("Pleasure systems in the brain", *Neuron* 86(3):646–664, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2015.02.018): liking is generated by a small set of hedonic hot spots, while "some of the best known textbook candidates for pleasure generators, including classic pleasure electrodes and the mesolimbic dopamine system, may not generate pleasure after all" (exact). Salamone & Correa 2012 (*Neuron* 76(3):470–485, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2012.10.021): labelling dopamine neurons "reward" neurons "is an overgeneralization"; accumbens dopamine is not needed for primary food motivation or appetite but for effort, behavioural activation, approach and sustained task engagement.
- [V] ★ Where the camps now overlap: Zhang, Berridge, Tindell, Smith & Aldridge 2009 ("A neural computational model of incentive salience", *PLoS Comput Biol* 5(7):e1000437, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000437): incentive salience = a cached TD-learned cue value that is further modulated on the fly by physiological state (e.g. salt appetite, drug sensitisation) — a formal bridge between learning and wanting. Berke 2018 ("What does dopamine mean?", *Nat Neurosci* 21(6):787–793, doi:10.1038/s41593-018-0152-y): dopamine conveys motivational value and promotes movement "even on subsecond timescales"; release is "locally sculpted by receptors on dopamine terminals, independently from dopamine cell firing"; striatal targets "abruptly switch between learning and performance modes"; dopamine gives "a dynamic estimate of whether it is worth expending a limited internal resource, such as energy, attention, or time".
- [~] ★ MIDDLE GROUND as of Oct 2026 (my synthesis of Berke 2018, Mohebi 2019, Hamid 2016, Kim 2020, Gershman 2024, Berridge & Kringelbach 2015, Dudhabhate & Costa 2026 — not a single authors' consensus statement): (1) dopamine is NOT a "pleasure signal" (hedonic liking rests on other hot spots, Berridge); (2) fast bursts of identified dopamine neurons to unexpected events/cues do behave like prediction errors that teach, with causal tests (Steinberg 2013; Sharpe 2017; Maes 2020); (3) slower or locally controlled dopamine release carries motivational value/vigor, so the same transmitter does both jobs, at different time scales and release mechanisms (Berke; Mohebi; Hamid); (4) the 1997 scalar-TD form is too simple — heterogeneity, sensory/motor features, ramps, hidden states, distributional codes — and the best current account is "prediction error, suitably generalised" (Gershman 2024) with a live minority view that dopamine is not mainly an RPE (Jeong 2022 vs Qian 2025). Do NOT teach "dopamine = reward" or "dopamine = pleasure"; teach "dopamine bursts report better-than-expected news and train predictions, and dopamine levels also scale how much a cue pulls you".
- STUDY Dopamine is only/mainly a reward-prediction-error teaching signal ("the RPE theory is the whole story") — original: Montague 1996; Schultz 1997. Later evidence: ramps, release-vs-spike dissociation, heterogeneity, hidden-state and distributional results (Hamid 2016; Mohebi 2019; Engelhard 2019; Dabney 2020), a retrospective-causal-learning challenge (Jeong 2022) that a TD model with a different state space matched (Qian 2025); review consensus is "too simple in its original form" (Gershman 2024).
  GRADE C: the narrow claim (C2) is grade A, but as a complete theory of dopamine it is contested and mid-revision; present "RPE + motivation" as a live research area, not a conclusion. — Gershman 2024; Mohebi 2019; Jeong 2022; Qian 2025; Dudhabhate & Costa 2026
### D. Extinction is new learning
- [def] Extinction 消退 = repeatedly presenting a conditioned stimulus WITHOUT its outcome, after which the conditioned response declines (Milad & Quirk 2012, *Annu Rev Psychol* 63:129–151, doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.121208.131631, PMC4942586). Exposure therapy is its clinical proxy (Craske et al. 2014, below).
- [V] ★ The core claim and its evidence: "Together, they constitute strong evidence that extinction does not erase the initial association between the CS and US but forms a new association (CS-No US) that inhibits expression of the conditioned memory" (Milad & Quirk 2012, exact; "they" = spontaneous recovery, fear renewal, reinstatement, accelerated re-acquisition). Pavlov saw spontaneous recovery of extinguished responses in dogs "in the 1920's" and proposed extinction was "a special form of inhibition" (same review; Pavlov 1927 itself not opened [~]). 抑制性学习 / 新学习而非抹除
- [V] ★ Bouton 2002 ("Context, ambiguity, and unlearning: sources of relapse after behavioral extinction", *Biol Psychiatry* 52(10):976–986, doi:10.1016/S0006-3223(02)01546-9; abstract): extinction "does not reflect a destruction of the original learning"; it is new learning "stored along with the old", so the cue is like an ambiguous word with two meanings and its current meaning depends on what the current context retrieves; context = physical environment, internal drug state, time; the second thing learned is "especially dependent on the context for retrieval". Bouton 2004 ("Context and behavioral processes in extinction", *Learn Mem* 11(5):485–494, doi:10.1101/lm.78804): extinction generates "new learning that is especially context-dependent"; best-fitting accounts stress generalisation decrement and expectation violation but need to accommodate "a context-modulated form of inhibitory learning".
- [V] ★ The four return-of-fear effects, as defined in Craske, Treanor, Conway, Zbozinek & Vervliet 2014 (*Behav Res Ther* 58:10–23, doi:10.1016/j.brat.2014.04.006, PMC4114726): SPONTANEOUS RECOVERY 自发恢复 — the CR grows with time since extinction ended; RENEWAL 更新 — fear returns when the context changes between extinction and test (ABA, ABC, AAB); REINSTATEMENT 重建 — unsignalled USs between extinction and test bring fear back; RAPID REACQUISITION 快速再获得 — CS–US pairings after extinction re-establish the CR quickly. Clinical analogues they give: return of fear after treatment ends, outside the therapy context, after an unrelated adverse event, or after re-traumatisation.
- [V] ADDED: Mechanism notes: reinstatement is largely context conditioning — Bouton & Bolles 1979 (*J Exp Psychol Anim Behav Process* 5(4):368–378, doi:10.1037/0097-7403.5.4.368): in rats, shocks after extinction of conditioned suppression reinstated suppression to the CS "but only when they were presented in the context in which testing was later to occur", and extinguishing fear to the context removed it. Rescorla 2004 ("Spontaneous recovery", *Learn Mem* 11(5):501–509, doi:10.1101/lm.77504): spontaneous recovery is "one of the most basic phenomena of Pavlovian conditioning" yet the empirical literature on its determinants is "relatively sparse and quite mixed"; its ubiquity suggests multiple sources.
- [V] ADDED: Humans and clinic: Vervliet, Craske & Hermans 2013 ("Fear extinction and relapse: state of the art", *Annu Rev Clin Psychol* 9:215–248, doi:10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-050212-185542): fear extinction is relatively easy to "learn" but difficult to "remember"; treatment should focus on long-term retrieval of extinction. Wang, Olsson, Lipp & Ney 2024 ("Renewal in human fear conditioning: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 159:105606, doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2024.105606): 80 studies reviewed, 23 meta-analysed; the classic ABA design gives the strongest renewal (vs ABC and ABBA); extinction in multiple contexts, or contexts more similar to acquisition, reduces renewal; the large variability of procedures calls for standardisation.
- [V] ★ Brain: Milad & Quirk 2012 — rat infralimbic (IL) cortex lesions did not impair extinction within a session but impaired its retrieval the next day; IL neurons signal the tone only at retrieval and their response size is inversely related to freezing; stimulating IL reduces fear; human vmPFC activation to the extinguished CS during recall correlated with extinction retention and vmPFC thickness correlated too; prelimbic cortex drives fear and opposes extinction. Their reading: extinction "does not return the brain to its pre-conditioning state, but rather potentiates inhibitory circuits". Lacagnina et al. 2019 (*Nat Neurosci* 22(5):753–761, doi:10.1038/s41593-019-0361-z): extinction training suppresses reactivation of contextual fear-engram cells in dentate gyrus while activating a second ensemble (a putative extinction engram); silencing the fear ensemble reduced spontaneous recovery, stimulating extinction neurons suppressed fear — a new memory "coexists with the original".
- [V] ADDED: Rethinking: Dunsmoor, Niv, Daw & Phelps 2015 ("Rethinking extinction", *Neuron* 88(1):47–63, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.028; abstract): reviews extinction as learning and as technique, "revisit[s] the predominant view that extinction results in new learning that interferes with expression of the original memory" and discusses computational theories and limits of extinction against relapse (abstract-level; paper not opened).
- [V] ADDED: Can the original memory be ERASED instead? Monfils, Cowansage, Klann & LeDoux 2009 (*Science* 324:951–955, doi:10.1126/science.1167975): in rats a single retrieval trial before extinction "permanently attenuates the fear memory"; Schiller et al. 2010 (*Nature* 463:49–53, doi:10.1038/nature08637): the same retrieval–extinction procedure in humans, effect "lasted at least a year". Kredlow, Unger & Otto 2016 (*Psychol Bull* 142(3):314–336, doi:10.1037/bul0000034): meta-analysis of 63 comparisons: post-retrieval extinction beat standard extinction at preventing return of fear in humans (g = .40, small-to-moderate) and of appetitive responses in animals (g = 0.89), but for fear in animals g = 0.21 and non-significant. Chalkia, Schroyens, Leng, Vanhasbroeck, Zenses, Van Oudenhove & Beckers 2020 (*Cortex* 129:496–509, doi:10.1016/j.cortex.2020.04.017): a highly powered, pre-registered, direct, independent replication of Schiller 2010 Exp 1 found no benefit of reactivation-extinction over regular extinction; they also report that the original paper misrepresented its exclusion criteria, and "give cause to question whether there is robust evidence" for the effect. Craske 2014 add that results were not replicated in all cases and occur whether the brief reminder comes before or after extinction (consistent with strengthened retrieval of extinction rather than erasure).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Clinical translation of "extinction is inhibitory learning" is still being tested: Craske 2014 propose 8 exposure-optimisation strategies (expectancy violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction, removal of safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple contexts, affect labelling) — a proposal paper with case studies ("there is a need for further evidence"). Brudan et al. 2025 (human lab, *Learn Behav*, doi:10.3758/s13420-025-00681-4): super-extinction (extinguishing in compound with another excitatory cue) produced MORE recovery than single-cue extinction, the opposite of the "bigger prediction error is better" idea; Johnco et al. 2025 (*J Consult Clin Psychol* 93(5):369–381, doi:10.1037/ccp0000953; N = 249 adults with public-speaking anxiety): exposure organised around expectancy violation (behavioural experiments) or preceded by cognitive restructuring beat exposure without explicit processing of expectancies (nonsignificant small advantage of behavioural experiments); Wannemüller et al. 2026 (*Psychother Psychosom*, doi:10.1159/000550395; N = 519 with fear of flying): one-session group exposure with four add-on strategies (peer support, self-efficacy, expectancy violation, coping with bodily symptoms) — "no condition yielded superior outcomes" although ~70% no longer met diagnostic criteria. Overall exposure-based CBT works vs placebo (Carpenter et al. 2018, *Depress Anxiety* 35:502–514, doi:10.1002/da.22728: 41 RCTs, N = 2,843, g = 0.56 on target-disorder symptoms) — covered in the therapy lesson.
- STUDY Extinction is new, context-dependent inhibitory learning, not erasure — original: Pavlov 1927 (spontaneous recovery); Bouton & Bolles 1979 (reinstatement, rats); Bouton 2002/2004 syntheses. Later evidence: decades of rodent studies (reviews: Myers & Davis 2007 *Mol Psychiatry* 12:120–150, doi:10.1038/sj.mp.4001939; Milad & Quirk 2012), human return-of-fear work incl. a 23-study renewal meta-analysis (Wang 2024), engram evidence of a coexisting second memory (Lacagnina 2019).
  GRADE A: rodents and humans, behaviour and brain, multiple labs and designs agree that extinction leaves the original learning in place and that fear returns with time, context change and unsignalled USs; cautions: spontaneous recovery's causes are "mixed" (Rescorla 2004) and the dedicated "unlearning" view has not been completely excluded (Dunsmoor 2015). — Milad & Quirk 2012; Wang 2024; Lacagnina 2019
- STUDY ADDED: Retrieval–extinction (reconsolidation update) erases fear — original: Monfils 2009 (rats); Schiller 2010 (humans). Later evidence: Kredlow 2016 meta-analysis (human fear g = .40; animal fear g = .21 ns); Chalkia 2020 registered replication fails and reports misreported exclusions; ADDED: Houtekamer, Henckens, van den Berg, Homberg & Kroes 2021 (*Behav Neurosci* 135(5):610–621, doi:10.1037/bne0000477, PMID 34096741): in rats conditioned at three shock intensities, a reminder before extinction did not prevent reinstatement in any group; Stemerding, Stibbe, van Ast & Kindt 2022 (*Sci Rep* 12:2285, doi:10.1038/s41598-022-06119-5, PMID 35145138) could not replicate a prediction-error-dependent propranolol reconsolidation effect in humans (an adjacent paradigm; "corroborate other failed reconsolidation studies").
  GRADE C: some positive results, a failed pre-registered direct replication, small pooled effects; do not teach that a reminder can "rewrite" a fear memory. — Kredlow 2016; Chalkia 2020
- STUDY Inhibitory-learning-optimised exposure beats standard exposure in patients — original: Craske et al. 2014 (proposals from lab theory). Later evidence: mixed (Johnco 2025 positive in an analogue sample; Wannemüller 2026 no add-on advantage in N = 519; Brudan 2025 lab contradiction).
  GRADE C: no consistent independent replication of clinical benefit over standard exposure that I could find (searched PubMed titles/abstracts, Oct 2026; [~]). — Craske 2014; Wannemüller 2026
### E. Schedules, punishment
- [def] ★ The four basic partial-reinforcement schedules 强化程式 (Ferster & Skinner 1957, *Schedules of Reinforcement*, New York: Appleton-Century-Crofts — book not opened; definitions as in OpenStax Psychology 2e §6.3, Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett 2020): fixed-ratio (a set number of responses per reward), variable-ratio (a varying number), fixed-interval (first response after a set time), variable-interval (varying time). Typical patterns (their Fig. 6.13): variable-ratio high and steady with little pause; fixed-ratio high with a short post-reward pause; variable-interval moderate and steady; fixed-interval "scallop-shaped" with a long pause after reward. 固定比率/可变比率/固定间隔/可变间隔
- [V] ★ Partial reinforcement extinction effect (PREE 部分强化消退效应): responses acquired with partial (inconsistent) reinforcement "extinguish more slowly than responses acquired with continuous (consistent) reinforcement" (Wilcher & Harris 2026, below, exact fragment). First reported by Humphreys 1939 (*J Exp Psychol* 25(2):141–158, doi:10.1037/h0058138; human eyelid conditioning with random alternation of reinforcement — bibliographic data from Crossref, paper not opened [~]).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Why it happens is still disputed: Capaldi's sequential theory (1966, 1994) is "the most popular account" — under partial reinforcement subjects learn that reward follows sequences of non-reward; Amsel 1958 (*Psychol Bull* 55(2):102–119, doi:10.1037/h0043125) proposed frustrative non-reward. Wilcher & Harris 2026 (*J Exp Psychol Anim Learn Cogn* 52(3):169–182, doi:10.1037/xan0000428; PMID 41801746): four groups of rats; rats used "temporal intervals, and not sequences, to predict reinforcement", which "directly challenges the main premise of sequential theory"; a PREE appeared only in groups that could NOT predict reinforcement — "the PREE depends on uncertainty about reinforcement" (certainty makes non-reinforcement more informative and speeds extinction). This also fits the informativeness account in B3 (Harris & Gallistel 2026) and the "extinction is learning" account in D.
- [V] ADDED: Folklore check, variable-ratio schedules "cause" gambling addiction: textbook claim traced to Skinner 1953 (quoted in OpenStax: casinos return "part of the patron's money on a variable-ratio schedule", p. 397; second-hand [~]). Laskowski, Dorchak, Ward, Christensen & Euston 2019 (*J Gambl Stud* 35(3):887–914, doi:10.1007/s10899-019-09852-1; PMID 31049772): rats trained 6 weeks on random-ratio vs fixed-ratio food schedules showed higher response rates and much shorter post-reward pauses, but addiction-like tests were "largely negative" (no difference with cued no-reward periods, punishment, food devaluation or reinstatement; one exception: more persistence on a progressive-ratio test in experiment 2) — "random-ratio schedules are motivating but not addictive" (authors' reading "on the surface"; longer training or more appetitive rewards might differ).
- [V] ★ Punishment 惩罚 — what is solid: in learning theory punishment is a consequence that lowers the future frequency of a behaviour (positive punishment adds an aversive event, negative punishment removes a pleasant one; OpenStax §6.3). The applied question is parental corporal punishment, where the evidence is correlational/longitudinal and the dispute is about SIZE and causation. Gershoff 2002 (*Psychol Bull* 128(4):539–579, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.539; abstract): meta-analyses of 11 child outcomes — parental corporal punishment "was associated with all child constructs, including higher levels of immediate compliance and aggression and lower levels of moral internalization and mental health".
- [V] ADDED: Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor 2016 ("Spanking and child outcomes: old controversies and new meta-analyses", *J Fam Psychol* 30(4):453–469, doi:10.1037/fam0000191; abstract): 111 unique effect sizes, 160,927 children; "Thirteen of 17 mean effect sizes were significantly different from zero and all indicated a link between spanking and increased risk for detrimental child outcomes"; effect sizes did not substantially differ between spanking and physical abuse or by study design. Heilmann et al. 2021 (*Lancet* 398(10297):355–364, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(21)00582-1; narrative review of 69 prospective longitudinal studies): physical punishment consistently predicts increasing behaviour problems, is "not associated with positive outcomes over time", associations robust across child and parent characteristics, "some evidence of a dose-response relationship" — authors conclude it is harmful and call for policy remedies.
- [V] ★ ADDED: The other side: Ferguson 2013 (*Clin Psychol Rev* 33(1):196–208, doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2012.11.002; abstract): longitudinal studies only; bivariate correlations small but non-trivial (spanking/corporal punishment r = .14/.18 with externalizing problems, .12/.21 internalizing, −.09/−.18 cognitive performance) but with better-controlled partial r "statistically significant but trivial (at or below pr = .10)" — he concludes the impact is "minimal" and urges a "more nuanced approach". Larzelere & Kuhn 2005 (*Clin Child Fam Psychol Rev* 8(1):1–37, doi:10.1007/s10567-005-2340-z; 26 studies): effect sizes favoured "conditional spanking" over 10 of 13 alternative tactics, customary physical punishment equalled alternatives, and "only overly severe or predominant use" compared unfavourably.
- [V] ADDED: Professional body: American Academy of Pediatrics policy statement (Sege & Siegel 2018, "Effective discipline to raise healthy children", *Pediatrics* 142(6):e20183112, doi:10.1542/peds.2018-3112): aversive strategies including all corporal punishment and yelling or shaming are "minimally effective in the short-term and not effective in the long-term" and "link[ed]" to increased risk of negative outcomes; the statement "supports the need for adults to avoid physical punishment and verbal abuse of children".
- STUDY Parental corporal punishment is associated with worse child outcomes — original: Gershoff 2002 (meta-analysis); Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor 2016 (111 effect sizes, N = 160,927). Later evidence: Heilmann 2021 (69 prospective studies) agrees in direction; Ferguson 2013 (longitudinal, partial r ≤ .10) and Larzelere & Kuhn 2005 dispute size/causation; no randomized trials are possible.
  GRADE B: direction consistent across reviews and major pediatric bodies advise against; the size after confounder control and the causal reading are disputed; teach the dispute rather than a single effect size. — Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor 2016; Ferguson 2013; Heilmann 2021
### F. Learning from surprise in humans; hypercorrection; pretesting
- [V] ★ Infants allocate attention by surprise level: Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012 ("The Goldilocks effect", *PLoS One* 7(5):e36399, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0036399; PMID 22649492): two experiments with 7- and 8-month-olds watching sequences of events whose complexity (negative log probability under an ideal-learner model) varied; the probability of looking away was highest for items of very low OR very high model complexity — infants "implicitly seek to maintain intermediate rates of information absorption". Extended to sound by the same lab (Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2014, *Child Dev* 85(5):1795–1804, doi:10.1111/cdev.12263; abstract only). 金发姑娘效应
- [V] ★ Violated expectations boost learning: Stahl & Feigenson 2015 ("Observing the unexpected enhances infants' learning and exploration", *Science* 348(6230):91–94, doi:10.1126/science.aaa3799; PMID 25838378): 11-month-olds (N = 110) saw events that violated expectations about object behaviour or nearly identical events that did not; infants "learned more effectively about objects that committed violations, explored those objects more, and engaged in hypothesis-testing behaviors" matching the kind of violation. Same lab, children aged 3–6 (four experiments): Stahl & Feigenson 2017 (*Cognition* 163:1–14, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2017.02.008) found novel-word learning enhanced after expectation-violating events (abstract only).
- STUDY Infants/children learn more from expectation-violating (surprising) events; infants attend most to events of intermediate predictability — original: Kidd 2012 (N not read); Stahl & Feigenson 2015 (N = 110). Later evidence: extensions from the same labs (Kidd 2014 auditory; Stahl & Feigenson 2017 children); I found no independent-lab replication and no multi-lab study (searched PubMed/Europe PMC titles, Oct 2026).
  GRADE C: no independent replication found; plausible and consistent with the prediction-error literature (B1–B4), but treat as a promising finding, not a settled one. — Kidd 2012; Stahl & Feigenson 2015
- [V] Adults: Behrens, Woolrich, Walton & Rushworth 2007 ("Learning the value of information in an uncertain world", *Nat Neurosci* 10(9):1214–1221, doi:10.1038/nn1954; PMID 17676057): human subjects "assess volatility in an optimal manner" and weight recent outcomes accordingly (learning rate rises when the environment is volatile); the volatility estimate and the salience of each new outcome are reflected in anterior cingulate fMRI signal, and individual ACC variation predicts individual learning rates — an empirical version of the "learning rate should follow surprise/volatility" idea of Pearce–Hall (B4). Greve, Cooper, Kaula, Anderson & Henson 2017 ("Does prediction error drive one-shot declarative learning?", *J Mem Lang* 94:149–165, doi:10.1016/j.jml.2016.11.001; PMC5381756): five experiments on one-shot scene–item memory; "In all experiments, the PE hypotheses were supported" (greater mismatch with the prior, more precise priors and more precise evidence each predicted better memory).
- [V] ★ Hypercorrection effect 超校正效应: Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001 ("Errors committed with high confidence are hypercorrected", *J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 27(6):1491–1494, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491; PMID 11713883): contrary to the authors' expectation from memory-strength models, "highly confident errors were the most likely to be corrected in a subsequent retest". Fazio & Marsh 2009 ("Surprising feedback improves later memory", *Psychon Bull Rev* 16(1):88–92, doi:10.3758/PBR.16.1.88; PMID 19145015): participants better remembered the appearance and content of feedback that did not match expectations — supports a surprise-attention account. Metcalfe 2017 (*Annu Rev Psychol* 68:465–489, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022): errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial, errors "committed with high confidence are corrected more readily than low-confidence errors", feedback including analysis of the reasoning is crucial.
- [V] ADDED: Boundaries and replications: Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011 (*Psychon Bull Rev* 18(6):1238–1244, doi:10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y): the effect persisted over a 1-week delay but error correction decreased and high-confidence errors were more likely than low-confidence errors to be REPRODUCED if the correction was forgotten. Carpenter, Haynes, Corral & Yeung 2018 (*Memory* 26(10):1379–1384, doi:10.1080/09658211.2018.1477164): a "significant hypercorrection effect" in an authentic introductory horticulture class (college students; higher prior knowledge → higher confidence and better correction). Griffiths & Higham 2018 (*Memory* 26(2):201–218, doi:10.1080/09658211.2017.1344249): low-confidence errors on "trick" questions were corrected as often as high-confidence errors, and "memory for the feedback and original responses, not confidence or surprise" predicted correction — the mechanism is not simply surprise.
- STUDY Hypercorrection (high-confidence errors are corrected more readily after feedback) — original: Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001. Later evidence: Fazio & Marsh 2009; Butler 2011; Metcalfe 2017 review; classroom replication (Carpenter 2018); boundary studies (Griffiths & Higham 2018). Sample sizes not read; no meta-analysis located.
  GRADE B: direction reliable across labs, materials and a real classroom; size, durability and mechanism (surprise vs retrieval/elaboration) vary. — Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001; Butler 2011; Griffiths & Higham 2018
- [V] ★ Pretesting effect 预测试效应: Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009 ("Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning", *J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 35(4):989–998, doi:10.1037/a0015729; PMID 19586265): six experiments (fictional trivia questions and cue → weak-associate guessing; rare correct guesses excluded) — attempting to answer before seeing the answer beat read-only study even though the attempt failed. Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009 (*J Exp Psychol Appl* 15(3):243–257, doi:10.1037/a0016496; PMID 19751074): five experiments with an essay on vision; posttest better after answering questions BEFORE reading than after extended study, even though only unsuccessfully retrieved items were analysed, and also when the questions were merely presented — "the testing effect appears to be attributable, in part, to the role unsuccessful tests play in enhancing future learning".
- [V] ADDED: Newer and boundary results: Sana & Carpenter 2023 (*Psychon Bull Rev* 30(5):1908–1916, doi:10.3758/s13423-023-02274-6): the benefit for PRETESTED items is "well documented", but the broader benefit for non-pretested items: "Sometimes this broader benefit is present, but other times it disappears or reverses" (depends on where the non-pretested information sits in the lesson). Mera, Dianova & Marin-Garcia 2026 (*Mem Cognit* 54(4):1334–1356, doi:10.3758/s13421-025-01813-x): pretest, post-test and copy–test–copy all beat errorless copying (no difference among the three), pretesting also helped older adults, and participants consistently underestimated how effective testing is. Pan & Carpenter 2023 (narrative review "Prequestioning and Pretesting Effects", PsyArXiv doi:10.31234/osf.io/9rqpm; published version not checked [~]). I located no meta-analysis of the pretesting effect.
- STUDY Pretesting / unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance later learning — original: Kornell 2009 (6 experiments); Richland 2009 (5 experiments). Later evidence: independent groups (Sana & Carpenter 2023; Mera 2026; Kliegl, Bartl & Bäuml 2023, *Memory* 31(5):705–714; Bartl, Kliegl & Bäuml 2025, *Psychol Res* 89:77 — abstracts only) keep finding the effect for pretested material; broader benefits inconsistent; no meta-analysis found.
  GRADE B: direction reliable across several labs for the pretested items; size, generalisation to other content and optimal conditions vary. — Richland 2009; Sana & Carpenter 2023; Mera 2026
- [~] Link to the lesson's engine: errors that carry surprise (high confidence, violated expectation, unanswered question) are where the update is largest — the human-scale analogue of Kamin's "only if the US is unpredicted"; but "surprise" is measured in different ways in these studies (confidence ratings, model-based complexity, object-behaviour violations) and the studies do not show a single learning-rate equation.
### G. Model-free vs model-based; heuristics learned or innate
- [def] ★ ADDED: Goal-directed vs habitual control 目标导向/习惯 (Dolan & Dayan 2013, "Goals and habits in the brain", *Neuron* 80(2):312–325, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2013.09.007, PMC3807793, CC BY): behaviour is goal-directed if it reflects knowledge of the action→outcome relation (R-O) AND the outcome is currently desired; habitual behaviour is "stamped in by past reinforcement" and "divorced from the current value of an associated outcome" — automatic, computationally cheap, inflexible. Test: after training, devalue the outcome (e.g. make the food unpalatable) and test in extinction — goal-directed responding falls, habitual responding continues; "extended training on interval schedules of reinforcement involving single actions and single outcomes" promotes habits. Rodent dorsomedial striatum/prelimbic cortex support goal-directed, dorsolateral striatum/infralimbic cortex support habits (same review).
- [V] Classic rodent evidence: Adams & Dickinson 1981 ("Instrumental responding following reinforcer devaluation", *Q J Exp Psychol B* 33:109–121, doi:10.1080/14640748108400816) — devaluing the reinforcer lowers later lever pressing (outcome devaluation); Adams 1982 (*Q J Exp Psychol B* 34:77–98, doi:10.1080/14640748208400878) — sensitivity to devaluation varies with amount of training (overtrained behaviour became insensitive); Dickinson 1985 (*Phil Trans R Soc B* 308:67–78, doi:10.1098/rstb.1985.0010) "Actions and habits". Citation data from Crossref; the content summary is the standard one in Dolan & Dayan 2013 — papers themselves not opened [~].
- [V] ★ Dual-control as reinforcement learning: Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005 ("Uncertainty-based competition between prefrontal and dorsolateral striatal systems for behavioral control", *Nat Neurosci* 8(12):1704–1711, doi:10.1038/nn1560): a "key trade-off pitting computational simplicity against the flexible and statistically efficient use of experience" realised as competition between dorsolateral striatum (cheap, cached) and prefrontal (flexible) systems, arbitrated by uncertainty ("each controller is deployed when it should be most accurate"). 无模型/有模型强化学习 Site pointers: all_lessons/computational_mind/lessons/04_rl_dopamine.html and 05_model_based.html; all_lessons/reinforcement_learning/lessons/05_value_based.html (value-based = model-free), 07_planning.html (model-based), 08_exploration.html.
- [V] ★ Two-step task in humans: Daw, Gershman, Seymour, Dayan & Dolan 2011 (*Neuron* 69(6):1204–1215, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2011.02.027): multistep task in which model-based and model-free influences can be separated; "choices reflected both influences", and the ventral-striatal BOLD prediction-error signal "reflected both model-free and model-based predictions in proportions matching those that best explained choice behavior" — challenging a separate model-free learner. Otto, Gershman, Markman & Daw 2013 ("The curse of planning", *Psychol Sci* 24(5):751–761, doi:10.1177/0956797612463080): a demanding secondary task increased reliance on model-free choice, trial by trial, and choice latencies tracked the computational expense. Kool, Cushman & Gershman 2016 (*PLoS Comput Biol* 12(8):e1005090, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005090): the hallmark two-step task does not itself embody an accuracy–effort trade-off (five factors reduce the payoff of model-based control); a modified version that does, shows people adapt.
- [V] ★ ADDED: CORRECTION/qualifier: Feher da Silva & Hare 2020 ("Humans primarily use model-based inference in the two-stage task", *Nat Hum Behav* 4(10):1053–1066, doi:10.1038/s41562-020-0905-y; PMID 32632333): with more detailed instructions participants made "primarily model-based choices that have little, if any, simple model-free influence"; purely model-based agents with wrong task models can falsely look like a model-free/model-based mixture; many participants do misconceive the task — "the simple dichotomy of model-free versus model-based learning is inadequate".
- [V] ★ ADDED: Habits in humans are hard to induce: de Wit, Kindt, Knot, Verhoeven, Robbins, Gasull-Camos et al. 2018 ("Shifting the balance between goals and habits: five failures in experimental habit induction", *J Exp Psychol Gen* 147(7):1043–1065, doi:10.1037/xge0000402; PMID 29975092): rats become habitual after overtraining but "only a single study has been published in humans"; they report five failed attempts (two avoidance-learning tasks, an appetitive slips-of-action task, and two independent attempts to replicate the original human demonstration): extensive training did not produce more habits; implication: failures of devaluation in compulsive patients may reflect weak goal-directed control rather than overactive habits.
- STUDY Goal-directed vs habitual control dissociate (outcome devaluation; overtraining → habit) — original: Adams & Dickinson 1981; Adams 1982 (rats). Later evidence: decades of rodent lesion/pharmacology work (Dolan & Dayan 2013 review); in humans, five failures to induce habits by overtraining incl. two direct replications (de Wit 2018) and the "model-free + model-based mixture" reading of the two-step task challenged (Feher da Silva & Hare 2020).
  GRADE C (for humans; the rodent dissociation itself is well established): mixed or thin replications in humans; do not teach "practice makes habits" or "people are part model-free, part model-based" as established findings in people. — de Wit 2018; Feher da Silva & Hare 2020; Daw 2011
- [V] ★ Heuristics: learned or innate? Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier 2011 ("Heuristic decision making", *Annu Rev Psychol* 62:451–482, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-120709-145346; PMID 21126183): heuristics "ignore part of the information"; reviewed work indicates (a) individuals and organisations "often rely on simple heuristics in an adaptive way" and (b) ignoring information "can lead to more accurate judgments than weighting and adding all information, for instance for low predictability and small samples". The "adaptive toolbox" question is empirical.
- [V] Evidence that strategy choice is LEARNED from feedback and the environment: Rieskamp & Otto 2006 ("SSL: a theory of how people learn to select strategies", *J Exp Psychol Gen* 135(2):207–236, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.135.2.207; PMID 16719651): strategy selection learning (a reinforcement-learning model over strategies) — "in all 4 studies" the best-performing strategy in participants' repertoire predicted inferences after enough learning, and SSL beat three extensions and an exemplar model. Bröder 2003 (*J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 29(4):611–625, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.29.4.611; 2 experiments, 220 participants, simulated stock market): "the majority of participants" used strategies adequate to the payoff structure (compensatory vs noncompensatory); shifts were moderated by intelligence, not by working-memory load.
- [V] ADDED: Limits: Newell & Shanks 2003 (*J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 29(1):53–65, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.29.1.53; PMID 12549583): take-the-best (TTB) use rose when information was costly, cue validities known and environment deterministic, but with "large individual variability" and behaviour inconsistent with TTB's stopping rule — they "question the validity of TTB as a psychologically plausible and pervasive model". Bröder & Schiffer 2006 (*J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 32(4):904–918, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.32.4.904; n = 241): most participants found the right strategy early but kept it after the payoffs changed (a routine at the level of strategies); a hint changed this little and money not at all.
- [V] Expertise and its limits: Kahneman & Klein 2009 ("Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree", *Am Psychol* 64(6):515–526, doi:10.1037/a0016755; PMID 19739881): the quality of an intuitive judgment depends on "the predictability of the environment" and on "the individual's opportunity to learn the regularities of that environment"; "Subjective experience is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy" (exact). I.e. intuitions are learned and are only as good as the feedback environment — the common ground of the heuristics-and-biases and Gigerenzer camps.
- [V] Memory as an environment-tuned learner: Anderson & Schooler 1991 ("Reflections of the environment in memory", *Psychol Sci* 2(6):396–408; text read): the probability that an item will be needed (measured in the *New York Times*, parental speech, electronic mail) shows the same dependence on frequency, recency and pattern of prior use as memory availability does — "human memory has the form it does because it is adapted to these environmental relationships". (Whether the adaptation is evolutionary, developmental or both is not settled by this paper.)
- STUDY People learn which simple strategy to use from feedback and environment structure (adaptive strategy selection) — original: Bröder 2003 (N = 220); Rieskamp & Otto 2006 (4 studies). Later evidence: Newell & Shanks 2003 (boundary conditions, high individual variability); Bröder & Schiffer 2006 (maladaptive routines); Kahneman & Klein 2009 (conditions under which learned intuition is trustworthy).
  GRADE B: adaptation to environment structure is reproducible across labs, but most people also show inertia, individual differences and use of non-matching strategies; I found no direct evidence for innate heuristic repertoires (see COULD NOT VERIFY). — Rieskamp & Otto 2006; Bröder 2003; Newell & Shanks 2003
### H. Myth list (09)
- [✗] ★ "Learning styles" 学习风格 ("teach each learner in their diagnosed visual / auditory / kinesthetic style and they learn more" — the meshing hypothesis 匹配假说). Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008 (*Psychol Sci Public Interest* 9(3):105–119, doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x; PMID 26162104; abstract read): a valid test needs learners grouped by style, randomly assigned to different methods, one common final test, and a crossover interaction (each style's best method differs); "we found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern", of the few proper tests "several found results that flatly contradict the popular meshing hypothesis", and "there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice" (exact fragments). What IS true (same abstract): people differ in how they prefer information to be presented and in specific aptitudes — preference has not been shown to steer which method works for whom. Direct tests: Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal 2015 (*J Educ Psychol* 107(1):64–78, doi:10.1037/a0037478; adults; not opened — characterised by their 2020 paper) and Rogowsky et al. 2020 (*Front Psychol* 11:164, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00164, PMID 32116958; fifth graders): "results failed to find a significant relationship between auditory or visual learning style preference and comprehension" (exact, 2020 abstract).
- [V] ★ ADDED: Latest meta-analysis (as of Oct 2026) and how common the belief is: Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger 2024 ("Is it really a neuromyth? A meta-analysis of the learning styles matching hypothesis", *Front Psychol* 15:1428732, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732, PMID 39055994, CC BY): 21 studies, 101 effect sizes, 1,712 participants; overall benefit of matching instruction to modality learning styles g = 0.31 (SE 0.12; 95% CI 0.05–0.57; p = .02), BUT only 26% of learning-outcome measures showed the required crossover (matched instruction best for at least two styles) — and 25% of 12 further studies; low study quality; authors: the benefit is "too small and too infrequent to warrant widespread adoption". So "exactly zero, ever" is slightly too strong, and "it works" is unsupported. Belief: Macdonald et al. 2017 (*Front Psychol* 8:1314, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01314; read): "individuals learn better when they receive information in their preferred learning style (e.g., auditory, visual, kinesthetic)" was endorsed by 93% of the US general public (N = 3,045), 76% of educators (N = 598) and 78% of the high-neuroscience-exposure group (N = 234); same-wording item in Dekker et al. 2012's teachers (as compared by Macdonald): 93% UK, 96% Netherlands.
- STUDY Matching instruction to a diagnosed learning style improves learning — original: the learning-styles literature (Pashler 2008: very few designs able to test it; those that could "flatly contradict" it). Later evidence: Rogowsky 2015 (adults) and 2020 (children) null; Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger 2024 meta-analysis g = 0.31 [0.05, 0.57] on low-quality studies with the required crossover in about a quarter of outcomes.
  GRADE D: for the practice claim (diagnose a style, then tailor teaching). The 2024 meta-analysis shows the old "no evidence at all" wording is a little too strong (small average benefit, rarely the predicted crossover); lessons should say "preferences are real; matching to them is not supported as a basis for teaching" — T6 owns the final wording. — Pashler 2008; Rogowsky 2020; Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger 2024
- [✗] ★ "It takes 10,000 hours of practice to become an expert (and 10,000 hours will do it)" 一万小时定律 (Gladwell 2008, *Outliers* — book not opened). - [V] ★ What the 1993 study found and what a replication found. - [V] ★ How much do practice differences explain? ADDED: a music-only analysis restricted to task-relevant practice (Platz, Kopiez, Lehmann & Wolf 2014, *Front Psychol* 5:646, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00646, PMID 25018742; 13 studies, N = 788) found r_c = .61, 95% CI [.54, .67] (Macnamara 2014's music figure, 23% of variance after the 2018 correction, corresponds to r̄ = .48). - [V] ADDED: Kernel of truth, so writers do not over-correct: chronic heavy drug use changes dopamine systems and part of it recovers with long abstinence — in 5 methamphetamine abusers re-scanned after 12–17 months of abstinence, striatal dopamine-transporter binding rose (caudate +19%, putamen +16%) though cognitive tests did not improve to the same extent (Volkow et al. 2001, *J Neurosci* 21(23):9414–9418, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.21-23-09414.2001, PMID 11717374); in addicted subjects imaging shows "decreases in dopamine release and in dopamine D2 receptors in striatum" (Volkow et al. 2004 review, *Mol Psychiatry* 9(6):557–569, doi:10.1038/sj.mp.4001507, PMID 15098002). This concerns addictive drugs, not everyday stimuli. Separately, a "digital detox" can help some outcomes for behavioural reasons: Setia et al. 2025 scoping review (*Cureus* 17(1):e78250, doi:10.7759/cureus.78250; 14 studies): such interventions "may alleviate depression and problematic internet use", effects on well-being variable — a different claim from "lowering dopamine", and not graded here.
- [✗] ★ "Dopamine is the pleasure / reward chemical" and "dopamine neurons fire when you get a reward". (1) Berridge & Robinson 1998: rats with up to 99% dopamine depletion kept normal 'liking' reactions to sucrose; Berridge & Kringelbach 2015: textbook candidates "including classic pleasure electrodes and the mesolimbic dopamine system, may not generate pleasure after all". (2) Schultz 1997: a fully predicted reward evokes no burst and an omitted one a dip — bursts report better-than-expected news. Details and the middle ground in 09C1–C4 [~].
- [✗] ★ "Repetition alone builds learning: more pairings, more learning". Kamin: 8 identical light+noise+shock pairings left a suppression ratio of .05 in controls but .45 in rats whose noise had already predicted the shock — pairing is not sufficient (09B1); Rescorla 1968: equal shock probability with and without the tone gave no conditioning despite pairings (09B3). Repetition matters while it brings news (the Rescorla–Wagner curve in Widget constants). Caveat: blocking itself is parameter-dependent (Maes 2016: 0 of 15 rodent experiments significant) — teach "learning tracks surprise" at grade B, not "pairings never matter".
- [✗] "Pavlov won the Nobel Prize for classical conditioning" — the 1904 prize was for the physiology of digestion; his lecture's signals are the sight and smell of food, not a bell (09A1).
- [✗] "Extinction erases the memory" — extinction is new, context-dependent inhibitory learning; fear returns by spontaneous recovery, renewal, reinstatement and rapid reacquisition (09D, grade A). "A reminder before extinction can rewrite a fear memory" is grade C (Kredlow 2016; Chalkia 2020; Houtekamer 2021).
- [✗] "Variable-ratio schedules are why slot machines and feeds hook people" — textbook folklore traced to Skinner 1953; in rats random-ratio schedules were motivating but "not addictive" (Laskowski 2019; 09E).
- [✗] "Little Albert: a healthy baby who learned to fear everything furry" — N = 1, no control, generalisation misreported, subject's identity and health disputed (09A4, grade D).
- [~] "Blocking is a rock-solid, universal law" — the principle (a predicted outcome teaches little) is supported across species and methods, but 15 rodent replication attempts found no block (Maes 2016) and the dispute over boundary conditions is open (09B2, grade B).
### I. Bet candidates (09)
- [V] ★ Bet (Kamin's blocking): rats get 8 light+noise compound trials, each followed by a shock. How afraid of the LIGHT alone are they afterwards? Control group (no earlier training): median suppression ratio .05 (≈ complete suppression of bar-pressing). Blocked group (16 noise→shock trials first): .45 — close to .50 = "no effect". Same 8 pairings; only the prior prediction differs (Kamin 1967 report p. 5; first test trial, group medians, 8–20 rats per group). Surprise restores learning: raise the shock from 1 to 4 mA when the light is added and the ratio is .14 (p. 20).

### Widget constants (09)
- [V] ★ The Rescorla–Wagner update in Rescorla's own words (Rescorla 2008, "Rescorla-Wagner model", *Scholarpedia* 3(3):2237, doi:10.4249/scholarpedia.2237, http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Rescorla-Wagner_model — written and curated by R. A. Rescorla, reviewed by A. G. Barto; read in full): for a compound AX followed by US1, ΔV_A = [α_A β_1](λ_1 − V_AX) and ΔV_X = [α_X β_1](λ_1 − V_AX), with V_AX = V_A + V_X; λ_1 "is the maximum conditioning US1 can produce; it represents the limit of learning"; α and β "are rate parameters dependent, respectively, on the CS and US" and "are viewed as having fixed values based on the physical properties of the particular CS and US"; the model is "essentially identical to the learning algorithm of Widrow an Hoff (1960)" (sic). His blocking account: prior conditioning leaves V_A "close to λ", so on AX trials the error is "close to zero" and V_X barely changes. Conditioned inhibition: after nonreinforced AX the asymptote is 0, so the error (0 − V_AX) is negative and pushes V_X below zero.
- [V] ★ Parameter conventions in recent papers (Brudan, Eisenbarth & Glautier 2025, *Learn Behav*, doi:10.3758/s13420-025-00681-4; Avraham et al. 2022, *eLife* 11:e75801, doi:10.7554/eLife.75801): λ = 1 when the outcome occurs and 0 when it does not; α = salience of the cue (a learning rate for cues); β = learning rate of the outcome (may differ on reinforced and non-reinforced trials); ΣV = strength summed over the cues present. Brudan et al. fitted human data with every learning rate constrained to [0.0001, 0.75] and optimiser start values of 0.1.
