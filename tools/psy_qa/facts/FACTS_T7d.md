#### 19-A · Stated ideals vs live attraction: Eastwick & Finkel 2008 and the speed-dating design
- [V] ★ Design (accepted-manuscript text, dated 6-5-07 "in press"; published version may differ in detail): 163 Northwestern undergraduates (81 female; mean age 19.6, SD 1.0; 74% White, 10% Asian), recruited by flyers/listserv emails; pre-event online questionnaire 6–13 days before; seven 2-hour speed-dating events (3 with men seated and women rotating, 4 with women seated and men rotating); each speed-date lasted 4 minutes; participants dated all opposite-sex participants; 206 matching pairs in total; follow-up questionnaires (10 waves) tracked relationships with matches and with "write-ins" (people met outside the event). — Eastwick & Finkel 2008, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 94(2):245–264, PMID 18211175, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.94.2.245; full text read (accepted manuscript, https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/MatePrefs_FINAL6-5-07.pdf)
- [V] ★ Abstract (verified from PubMed): stated ideal-partner preferences showed the "traditional" sex differences (attractiveness, earning prospects) for an ideal partner and ideal speed date, but "no sex differences in the associations between participants' romantic interest in real-life potential partners … and the attractiveness and earning prospects of those partners"; and "participants' ideal preferences, assessed before the speed-dating event, failed to predict what inspired their actual desire at the event." The authors frame this with Nisbett & Wilson 1977 (introspective access). — PMID 18211175
- [V] ★ Numbers (manuscript text): stated sex difference in the importance of "physically attractive" d = −.55 (mean across measures, medium-to-large) — but the correlation between a live partner's rated attractiveness and the participant's romantic interest was .43 (men) and .46 (women) for speed-dating partners/matches and .26 / .31 for write-ins; the sex difference in these correlations was r = .03 (p = .673) for speed-dating partners and r = .05 (p = .499) for write-ins. For earning prospects the correlations were .19 (men) / .16 (women) for speed-dating partners and .16 / .18 for write-ins; sex differences r = −.04 (p = .480) and r = .02 (p = .825), "descriptively very small".
- [V] ★ The key null: correlations between participants' stated preferences (4 assessments, which agreed with each other, e.g. r = .52 between pre-event and speed-dating reports of the same preference) and their "in-vivo" preferences (the individual slope relating a trait in a live partner to their desire/chemistry/saying yes) were small: out of 72 correlations "only 2 were significant and positive and 1 was actually significant and negative"; e.g. stated "personable" vs in-vivo yes-slope r = .05 (p = .578). The authors' summary: stated preferences consistent across assessments, in-vivo preferences consistent within a live context, but stated preferences "unrelated to their in-vivo preferences" (manuscript).
- [V] Limits the authors name: undergraduates, ~4-minute encounters, speed-dating "yessing"; null results ≠ proof of no relation, and moderator explorations (e.g. desire for a serious relationship, restricted range) were unsupported in the manuscript's own checks. — manuscript text
- [V] ★ Direct replication in a larger sample: Selterman, Chagnon & Mackinnon 2015, "Do men and women exhibit different preferences for mates? A replication of Eastwick and Finkel (2008)", *SAGE Open* 5(3), doi:10.1177/2158244015605160 (abstract read via Semantic Scholar record; open access): 307 participants, speed dating with 30-day follow-up surveys, multilevel models aggregated by meta-analysis; "participants were more romantically interested in potential partners if they were viewed as attractive and good potential earners, and these associations were not moderated by gender"; the authors themselves add "some general methodological limitations". (Replicates the live-attraction half of Eastwick & Finkel; does not test the stated-vs-live correlation part.)
- STUDY Stated ideal-partner preferences do not predict who produces live attraction (speed dating) — original: Eastwick & Finkel 2008 (N = 163 undergraduates, seven events, 4-minute dates; 72 stated-vs-in-vivo correlations, 2 significant positive, 1 negative; stated sex differences d = −.55 for attractiveness but live-partner associations r ≈ .43–.46 for both sexes). Later evidence: Selterman et al. 2015 direct replication (N = 307), Eastwick et al. 2014 meta-analysis (below), Joel et al. 2017 machine-learning analysis (Sample A, 163 people, matches the 2008 sample in N, age and event count — inferred — plus 187 more; below), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE B: the live-vs-stated dissociation is reproduced in more than one dataset and in a meta-analysis, but every speed-dating sample is young, university-based and mostly Western, encounters last minutes, and the stated-vs-in-vivo null comes mostly from one lab group (Finkel/Eastwick) and one decade — independent replications are few. — Eastwick & Finkel 2008; Selterman 2015; Joel 2017

#### 19-B · Meta-analysis: do ideal-partner preferences predict evaluations of real vs hypothetical partners?
- [V] ★ Eastwick, Luchies, Finkel & Hunt 2014, "The predictive validity of ideal partner preferences: a review and meta-analysis", *Psychol Bull* 140(3):623–665, PMID 23586697, doi:10.1037/a0032432 (abstract read): reviews research on predictive validity of ideal partner preferences and offers an integrative model of "when and why ideals succeed or fail to predict relational outcomes". Men and women "reliably differ in the extent to which these qualities affect their romantic evaluations of hypothetical targets" (descriptions, vignettes, photographs). New meta-analysis, k = 97 (attraction and relationships literatures): physical attractiveness predicted romantic evaluations with a moderate-to-strong effect (r ≈ .40) for both sexes; earning prospects predicted with a small effect (r ≈ .10) for both sexes; sex differences in the correlations small (r difference = .03) and uniformly nonsignificant. The abstract also says some alternative measures of preferences have stronger predictive validity "in certain theoretically sensible contexts" and discusses the gap between live and hypothetical interactions.
- [V] Two published comment articles accompany it in the same issue (PubMed CommentIn: *Psychol Bull* 140(3):666–672, doi:10.1037/a0036225; 140(3):673–681, doi:10.1037/a0036111) — the paper was debated, not just accepted; contents not opened (see COULD NOT VERIFY).
- [~] "Sample size N" of the meta-analysis (number of participants) is not in the abstract — not given here.
- STUDY Preference-match predicts romantic evaluation less in live encounters than in hypothetical ones (Eastwick et al. 2014 integrative model) — original: Eastwick & Finkel 2008 (live speed-dating null) plus hypothetical-target studies showing sex-differentiated preferences. Later evidence: Eastwick et al. 2014 meta-analysis (k = 97: attractiveness r ≈ .40, earning prospects r ≈ .10 for both sexes; sex difference r = .03 n.s.), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE B: a Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis supports "trait effects are similar for both sexes, and live evaluations differ from stated ones"; but the "stated preferences are poor predictors" half rests on a handful of studies, effect sizes are for attractiveness/earning prospects only, and the paper drew published critiques. — Eastwick et al. 2014 (PMID 23586697)

#### 19-C · Machine learning on initial attraction (Joel, Eastwick & Finkel 2017)
- [V] ★ Joel, Eastwick & Finkel 2017, "Is romantic desire predictable? Machine learning applied to initial romantic attraction", *Psychol Sci* 28(10):1478–1489, PMID 28853645, doi:10.1177/0956797617714580 (published PDF read, via a gwern.net mirror; abstract verified in PubMed). Two speed-dating studies: Sample A = 163 undergraduates (81 women, 82 men; seven events in 2005 — identical N, mean age 19.6 (SD 1.0) and event count to Eastwick & Finkel 2008's sample, so very likely the same data; inferred, not stated); Sample B = 187 undergraduates (93 women, 94 men; eight events in 2007). Pre-event 30-minute questionnaire (182 constructs usable in A, 112 in B; "more than 100" in the abstract); each participant had a 4-min date with ~12 opposite-sex others; desire measured by a 3-item scale (liked, sexually attracted, likely to say yes; 9-point).
- [V] ★ Variance partition (social relations model, Table 1): actor (how much a person desires others on average) 12.15% (A) / 13.60% (B); partner (how desirable one is to others on average) 25.90% / 22.52%; relationship (unique desire for a particular partner, above actor+partner) 34.74% (men for women) and 31.0% (women for men) in A; 35.98% / 32.1% in B; error ≈ 27–32%. "Relationship variance was the largest source of variance" — so the unique part is big but unexplained by traits.
- [V] ★ Random-forest prediction from pre-date traits and preferences (Table 2): actor desire 4.95%–17.78% of variance explained (abstract: 4–18%); partner desire 18.48%–26.70% (abstract: 7–27%); relationship desire (the unique part): −4.55% to −0.18% (men for women) and −2.68% to +1.34% (women for men) — i.e. a model using all pre-date measures predicted about 0% of the unique-partner variance (negative = worse than predicting the grand mean). Abstract: "unable to predict relationship variance using any combination of traits and preferences reported before the dates." (The paper's text says "approximately 5% to 18%" and "18% to 27%"; the abstract says 4–18% and 7–27%; give the abstract's ranges and cite Table 2 for the model-by-model values.)
- [V] Train-on-A, test-on-B (87 shared predictors): predicted vs actual correlation r = .19 (actor desire, 95% CI .05–.33), r = .26 (partner desire, .12–.39), r = −.06 (men's relationship desire, CI −.121 to −.002) and r = .02 (women's relationship desire, CI −.04 to .08); the authors: "at best, we could predict less than 0.1% of the variance in relationship desire in Sample B".
- [V] ★ It is not a method failure: models using POST-interaction ratings (chemistry, perceived attractiveness of this date, etc.) predicted 21–29% (men's) and 16–24% (women's) of relationship desire; "feelings of chemistry with a partner" was the best predictor in both samples. Simulated data showed random forests recover preference × trait interaction effects when such effects exist.
- [V] Limits named by the authors: undergraduate samples (demographically homogeneous; age/SES/religion matching might be predictable in more diverse groups); 4-minute encounters; "the present findings address only obliquely the predictability of long-term romantic compatibility"; relationship desire may consist of many tiny dyad-specific interactions (they suggest genomics-scale samples) or simply be unpredictable from pre-meeting measures. — Joel et al. 2017 Discussion
- STUDY Machine learning cannot predict whom you will especially desire from pre-meeting traits and preferences — original: Joel, Eastwick & Finkel 2017 (two speed-dating samples, N = 163 and 187; > 100 self-report measures before the dates; unique-partner desire = 31–36% of variance; pre-date model R² ≈ 0 (−4.6% to +1.3%), out-of-sample r = −.06 and .02; chemistry measured after the 4-minute date predicted 16–29%). Later evidence: Joel et al. 2020 (43 datasets; relationship-quality prediction, below) is consistent; no independent preregistered replication of the speed-dating null was found, as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE B: a strong test (large predictor set, out-of-sample, simulation validation, open data), but two student samples from one university and one lab group; the null is for initial 4-minute desire only and its scope is limited by the predictors measured. — Joel et al. 2017 (PMID 28853645)

#### 19-D · What does predict relationship quality (43-dataset machine-learning project)
- [V] ★ Joel et al. 2020, "Machine learning uncovers the most robust self-report predictors of relationship quality across 43 longitudinal couples studies", *PNAS* 117(32):19061–19071, PMID 32719123, doi:10.1073/pnas.1917036117 (abstract verified in PubMed; numbers on N read in the accepted manuscript at https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/29101/1/preprint%20Joel%20et%20al%20Prediction%20Project%20PNAS%202020.pdf — published figures may differ slightly). Abstract: across 43 dyadic longitudinal datasets from 29 laboratories, the top relationship-specific predictors were perceived-partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived-partner satisfaction and conflict; the top individual-difference predictors were life satisfaction, negative affect, depression, attachment avoidance and attachment anxiety.
- [V] ★ ✗ CORRECTION to the task file: the sample is **11,196 couples (baseline N = 22,163 participants)**, not "11,196 individuals" — 43 longitudinal datasets (funded by 39 grants), 86 researchers, 2,413 mostly self-report baseline measures, average four time points (range 2–11) over an average 14 months (range 2–48). — accepted manuscript text
- [V] ★ Variance explained (abstract): relationship-specific variables predicted up to 45% of the variance in relationship quality at baseline and up to 18% at the end of each study; individual differences alone 21% and 12%; actor-reported variables predicted two to four times more variance than partner-reported ones; individual differences and partner reports "had no predictive effects beyond actor-reported relationship-specific variables alone"; relationship-quality change over the study was "largely unpredictable". Manuscript detail: baseline satisfaction — actor-reported individual variables 19%, partner-reported individual variables 5%, combined 21%; actor-reported relationship variables 45%, partner-reported relationship variables 15%, combined actor+partner relationship 46%, everything combined 44%.
- [V] Ideal standards as a predictor: in the manuscript's Table 3 (success rates of the most commonly measured individual-difference constructs; a construct's success rate = share of its measures that were a contributing predictor in at least one of baseline/follow-up/change models), "ideal standards" had 39 measures tested and an overall success rate of 10% — among the weakest listed (cf. neuroticism 41%, agreeableness 54%, relationship length 57% among relationship constructs; perceived partner commitment 85%, intimacy 81%). The authors stress the success rate "does not have any independent meaning or effect size" — it is only a relative ordering within a class. — accepted manuscript Tables 2–3
- [V] Boundaries named by the authors: all 43 datasets from Western countries (US, Canada, Switzerland, New Zealand, the Netherlands, Israel); 99.4% of 1,149 relationship-specific variables were explicit self-report rating scales (no behavioural observation); one dataset (Supporting Healthy Marriages, N = 6,298 couples) over-sampled low-income couples and its results mirrored the other 42; the predictors were measured at baseline in existing couples.
- STUDY Relationship-specific perceptions (not personality traits or stated ideals) predict relationship quality — original: interdependence theory and many single-lab longitudinal studies. Later evidence: Joel et al. 2020 (43 datasets, 11,196 couples / 22,163 participants; relationship-specific variables explained up to 45% of baseline and 18% of end-of-study variance vs 21% and 12% for individual differences; ideal-standard measures among the weakest), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE A: a pre-planned multi-laboratory analysis of 43 independent datasets with consistent direction across datasets; the sizes depend on the self-report nature of both predictor and outcome (shared-method variance) and on Western samples, so the "A" is for the direction and ranking, not the percentages. — Joel et al. 2020 (PMID 32719123)

#### 19-E · Counter-evidence and the newest large tests (stated preferences DO sometimes predict)
- [V] ★ Li, Yong, Tov, Sng, Fletcher, Valentine, Jiang & Balliet 2013, "Mate preferences do predict attraction and choices in the early stages of mate selection", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 105(5):757–776, PMID 23915041, doi:10.1037/a0033777 (abstract read): online-messaging and modified speed-dating experiments; stated importance of status/attractiveness "on paper" predicted how much those traits mattered in actual choices; the authors argue earlier null speed-dating results came from mating pools without people at the low end of the trait range (restricted range) and that the sexes' choices only separated in long-term contexts. Directly challenges the Eastwick & Finkel null; sex-difference parts of the abstract are NOT for lesson use (care rule).
- [V] Todd, Penke, Fasolo & Lenton 2007, "Different cognitive processes underlie human mate choices and mate preferences", *PNAS* 104(38):15011–15016, PMID 17827279, doi:10.1073/pnas.0705290104 (abstract read): 46 adults at one speed-dating event; stated preferences from a pre-event questionnaire replicated earlier self-report patterns, but "the stated preferences did not predict actual choices made during the speed-dates" — an independent small-sample echo of the null (note N = 46).
- [V] Kurzban & Weeden 2007, "Do advertised preferences predict the behavior of speed daters?", *Personal Relationships* 14(4):623–632, doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2007.00175.x (abstract read via OpenAlex/Wiley record): HurryDate (a commercial speed-dating firm) data from "over 10,000 people in their 20s, 30s, and 40s" across US cities; advertised preferences predicted decisions to attend particular events but "did not, in contrast, substantially predict decisions within events" — "advertised preferences predict behavior in the mating domain in some contexts but not others." (An independent, much larger dataset than Eastwick & Finkel's; the abstract also lists sex-difference and assortative findings — not for lesson use.)
- [V] Luo & Zhang 2009, "What leads to romantic attraction: similarity, reciprocity, security, or beauty? Evidence from a speed-dating study", *J Pers* 77(4):933–964, doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.2009.00570.x (abstract read via OpenAlex): Social Relations Model analyses showed initial attraction is "a function of the actor, the partner, and the unique dyadic relationship"; "The strongest predictor of attraction for both sexes was partners' physical attractiveness"; "there was some support for the reciprocity principle but no evidence for the similarity principle" (actual similarity, in live speed-dating). The abstract also reports sex-specific patterns — not for lesson use. N not in the abstract.
- [V] ★ Eastwick, Eagly, Finkel & Johnson 2011, "Implicit and explicit preferences for physical attractiveness in a romantic partner: a double dissociation in predictive validity", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 101(5):993–1011, PMID 21767032, doi:10.1037/a0024061 (abstract read): five studies; explicit (stated) preferences predicted romantic interest in opposite-sex PHOTOGRAPHS but not in real-life speed-daters or confederates, while an implicit (go/no-go association) preference showed the opposite pattern; explicit–implicit correlation ≈ .00 on average. A clean "stated ≠ live" demonstration inside one paper.
- [V] ★ Eastwick, Finkel & Eagly 2011, "When and why do ideal partner preferences affect the process of initiating and maintaining romantic relationships?", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 101(5):1012–1032, PMID 21707198, doi:10.1037/a0024062 (abstract read): two lab experiments — a written profile matching (vs mismatching) a participant's idiosyncratic ideals raised romantic interest, but after a live interaction with that partner the match/mismatch manipulation was no longer associated with interest (participants reinterpreted what a trait meant for this particular person; an Asch 1946 context effect); a longitudinal study of middle-aged adults found ideal-match predicted evaluations of a current partner but not of a merely desired partner. Authors' summary: match predicts outcomes "when participants are learning about a partner in the abstract and when they are actually in a relationship with the partner, but not when considering potential dating partners they have met in person."
- [V] ★ Gerlach, Arslan, Schultze, Reinhard & Penke 2019 (Göttingen Mate Choice Study), "Predictive validity and adjustment of ideal partner preferences across the transition into romantic relationships", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 116(2):313–330, PMID 28921999, doi:10.1037/pspp0000170 (abstract read): preregistered, large-scale online prospective design; N = 763 predominantly heterosexual singles aged 18–40, followed ~5 months; stated preferences (4 domains) "prospectively predicted the characteristics of later partners" (equally for both sexes except one domain); preferences were fairly stable over 5 months, less so for those who entered a relationship; people adjusted preferences downward when a partner fell short, not consistently upward when exceeded. (The abstract gives no effect sizes.)
- [V] ★ Conroy-Beam & Buss 2016, "Do mate preferences influence actual mating decisions? Evidence from computer simulations and three studies of mated couples", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 111(1):53–66, PMID 27337140, doi:10.1037/pspi0000054 (abstract read): agent-based simulations plus three studies of real couples (n = 214, 259, 294): low-magnitude correlations between stated preferences and the traits of chosen partners are what preference-driven markets would produce too (a weak per-trait correlation is not evidence that preferences do nothing); real-world choices matched preference-driven simulations rather than random ones. A methodological counter to "preferences don't matter".
- [V] ★ Zhao, Sidari, Lee & Zietsch 2025 (online 2024), "Speed-dating and simulation data explain the discrepancy between stated and revealed mate preferences", *Eur J Pers* 39(4):535–557, doi:10.1177/08902070241286254 (abstract read via Crossref): Study 1 high-powered speed dating (n = 1,145): four of nine traits showed correspondence between stated and revealed preferences; Study 2 simulations: when attractiveness depends on several independent traits, the per-trait stated–revealed association can be only minimal even when preferences drive choice; Study 3 omnibus metrics (Euclidean distance, pattern metric) showed apparent associations that permutation tests suggest are statistical artefacts. Conclusion: detecting any stated–revealed association "will be difficult under realistic assumptions about the number of traits involved". (Reconciles the two camps: the usual test is weak, not necessarily the underlying link.)
- [V] ★★ Eastwick, Sparks, Finkel, Meza, Adamkovič, Adu et al. 2025, "A worldwide test of the predictive validity of ideal partner preference matching", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 128(1):123–146, PMID 39480282, doi:10.1037/pspp0000524 (abstract read via Crossref/Semantic Scholar): registered report with the Psychological Science Accelerator, N = 10,358, 43 countries, 22 languages. "The most rigorous tests revealed significant preference-matching effects" in the whole sample and for partnered and single participants separately: the corrected pattern metric (collapsing across 35 traits) gave a zero-order β = .19 and β = .11 when included alongside a normative preference-matching metric; specific-trait ("level metric") interaction tests gave "very small (average β = .04)" effects; effect sizes were similar for partnered participants who reported ideals before entering a relationship; no consistent evidence that individual differences moderated effects. [The abstract also compares stated vs revealed preferences by gender — do NOT use under the care rule.]
- [V] Methodological caution: Eastwick, Finkel & Simpson 2019, "Best practices for testing the predictive validity of ideal partner preference-matching", *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 45(2):167–181, doi:10.1177/0146167218780689 (abstract read via OpenAlex): a samplewide positive correlation between a participant's ideals and their current partner's traits (an A–B correlation) does NOT show that people pursued or selected partners matching their ideals — "at least six alternative explanations" — so proper tests need the ideal × partner-trait → outcome (A × B → C) form. The 2025 registered report follows this line. (Relevant to "your type predicts your partners": a correlation between stated ideals and partners' traits is weaker evidence than it looks.)
- STUDY Ideal-partner preference matching predicts evaluation of real partners (the registered worldwide test) — original: Eastwick & Finkel 2008 and 2011 papers (stated ideals predicting little in live encounters), vs Li 2013 and Gerlach 2019 (preferences predict choices/partners). Later evidence: Eastwick, Sparks et al. 2025 registered report (N = 10,358; 43 countries; pattern metric β = .19 → .11 with normative control; level-metric β ≈ .04); Zhao et al. 2025 (per-trait tests are statistically weak by design), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE B: the best-powered, preregistered, cross-national test finds real but small preference-matching effects (pooled β ≈ .1–.2 for the whole-profile metric, ≈ .04 per trait), which reconciles earlier "null" and "positive" camps; but it is self-report, cross-sectional for most participants, and the size depends on the metric chosen. — Eastwick et al. 2025 (PMID 39480282); Zhao 2025
- STUDY Stated ideal vs hypothetical vs live partner (context effect) — original: Eastwick, Finkel & Eagly 2011 (profile match raises interest; after a live interaction it does not) and Eastwick, Eagly, Finkel & Johnson 2011 (explicit preferences predict interest in photographs, not live speed-daters; implicit measure the reverse). Later evidence: Eastwick et al. 2014 meta-analysis (k = 97; live vs hypothetical), Eastwick et al. 2025 (preference-matching effects exist but small). 
  GRADE B: the "described people vs real people" gap is reproduced across several studies and a meta-analysis from one research programme; independent replications of the specific implicit-vs-explicit double dissociation were not found. — PMIDs 21767032, 21707198

#### 19-F · Real people differ from imagined ones: willingness to reject; choice overload
- [V] Joel, Teper & MacDonald 2014, "People overestimate their willingness to reject potential romantic partners by overlooking their concern for other people", *Psychol Sci* 25(12):2233–2240, PMID 25344347, doi:10.1177/0956797614552828 (abstract read): two studies of single people offered the chance to accept or decline advances from physically unattractive (Study 1) or preference-incompatible (Study 2) potential dates; participants were significantly less willing to reject them when they believed the situation real than when hypothetical; partly explained by other-focused motives (not wanting to hurt the other's feelings). Sample sizes not in the abstract.
- [V] Lenton & Francesconi 2010, "How humans cognitively manage an abundance of mate options", *Psychol Sci* 21(4):528–533, doi:10.1177/0956797610364958 (abstract via Crossref): 1,868 women and 1,870 men across 84 speed-dating events; with more options choosers attended less to slow-to-assess characteristics (occupational status, education) and more to quickly assessed ones (height, weight).
- [V] Pronk & Denissen 2020 (online 2019), "A rejection mind-set: choice overload in online dating", *Soc Psychol Pers Sci* 11(3):388–396, doi:10.1177/1948550619866189 (abstract via Crossref): three studies; with continued access to many options participants rejected more hypothetical and actual partners — "on average a decrease of 27% in chance on acceptance from the first to the last partner option". [~] One research group, no independent replication found.
- STUDY Choice overload / rejection mind-set in online dating — original: Pronk & Denissen 2020 (3 studies; −27% acceptance chance from first to last option); Lenton & Francesconi 2010 (3,738 speed-daters, 84 events; attention shifts to quickly assessed traits with more options). Later evidence: none independent found, as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE C: two observational/lab datasets in the same direction, no independent replication found, effect sizes beyond the abstracts not read. — Pronk & Denissen 2020; Lenton & Francesconi 2010

- STUDY People overestimate their willingness to reject real suitors (hypothetical vs real choice) — original: Joel, Teper & MacDonald 2014 (two studies; participants given the chance to accept/decline advances from physically unattractive or preference-incompatible potential dates were significantly less willing to reject when the situation was believed real; partly explained by concern for the other's feelings). Later evidence: none independent found, as of Oct 2026; consistent with Eastwick et al. 2011 (ideals predict hypothetical but not live evaluations).
  GRADE C: two studies from one lab with the key N not retrieved; no independent replication found; plausible mechanism. — Joel, Teper & MacDonald 2014 (PMID 25344347)

#### 19-G · Perceived vs actual similarity; what "similar" means (the "opposites attract" question)
- [V] ★ Montoya, Horton & Kirchner 2008, "Is actual similarity necessary for attraction? A meta-analysis of actual and perceived similarity", *J Soc Pers Relat* 25(6):889–922, doi:10.1177/0265407508096700 (abstract read via an institutional repository page; Crossref/APA abstract elided): 460 effect sizes from 313 laboratory and field studies; both actual similarity (r = .47) and perceived similarity (r = .39) were strongly related to attraction overall; actual similarity mattered in no-interaction and short-interaction studies, but its effect was significantly weaker in short-interaction than in no-interaction studies and was not significant in existing relationships; perceived similarity predicted attraction in all three settings (no-interaction, short-interaction, existing relationship). The authors discuss why perceived, not actual, similarity matters once people interact.
- [V] ★ Tidwell, Eastwick & Finkel 2013 (online 2012), "Perceived, not actual, similarity predicts initial attraction in a live romantic context: evidence from the speed-dating paradigm", *Pers Relatsh* 20(2):199–215, doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2012.01405.x (abstract via Crossref): speed-dating, ~12 four-minute dates each; similarity computed from questionnaires before the event and after each date; "perceived, but not actual, similarity significantly predicted romantic liking"; perceived similarity was "a far weaker predictor of attraction when assessed using specific traits rather than generally." (N not in the abstract.)
- [V] Luo & Klohnen 2005, "Assortative mating and marital quality in newlyweds: a couple-centered approach", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 88(2):304–326, PMID 15841861, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.88.2.304 (abstract read): 291 newlywed couples; substantial similarity on attitude-related domains but little on personality-related domains; similarity was not due to social homogamy or convergence; similarity on attachment characteristics was the most predictive of satisfaction; profile-based similarity indices beat absolute difference scores.
- [V] ★ Dyrenforth, Kashy, Donnellan & Lucas 2010, "Predicting relationship and life satisfaction from personality in nationally representative samples from three countries: the relative importance of actor, partner, and similarity effects", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 99(4):690–702, PMID 20718544, doi:10.1037/a0020385 (abstract read): married couples in Australia (N = 5,278), the UK (N = 6,554), Germany (N = 11,418); actor effects ≈ 6% of variance in relationship satisfaction (10–15% for life satisfaction); partner effects 1–3% (relationship) and 1–2% (life); couple similarity "consistently explained less than .5%" of variance after controlling for actor and partner effects. - [V] Weidmann, Schönbrodt, Ledermann & Grob 2017, "Concurrent and longitudinal dyadic polynomial regression analyses of Big Five traits and relationship satisfaction: does similarity matter?", *J Res Pers* 70:6–15, doi:10.1016/j.jrp.2017.04.003 (abstract via OSF record): 237 couples; beyond actor effects (neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness) and partner effects (agreeableness), "similarity plays a negligible role for relationship satisfaction"; two longitudinal similarity effects emerged (dissimilar neuroticism; modest openness in both partners).

- [def] 相似性吸引 similarity–attraction; 实际相似 actual similarity = the objective correlation between two people's attitudes/traits; 感知相似 perceived similarity = how similar one believes the other to be (definitions as used in Montoya 2008 / Tidwell 2013). 互补性 complementarity ("opposites attract") = the hypothesis that dissimilar traits attract.
- STUDY Actual similarity drives attraction and satisfaction ("birds of a feather") — original: classic similarity–attraction research. Later evidence: Montoya et al. 2008 (460 effect sizes; actual r = .47, perceived r = .39; actual similarity not significant in existing relationships), Tidwell et al. 2013 (live speed dating: perceived not actual), Dyrenforth et al. 2010 (similarity < .5% of variance in satisfaction, N > 23,000 across three nations), Weidmann et al. 2017 (negligible), Youyou et al. 2017 (couples do resemble each other: rs .20–.47). 
  GRADE B: couples reliably resemble each other (attitudes, values, some behavioural personality), and perceived similarity predicts liking at every stage; but ACTUAL personality similarity adds almost nothing to satisfaction beyond each partner's own traits. "Opposites attract" has no support. — Montoya 2008; Dyrenforth 2010; Tidwell 2013; Weidmann 2017

#### 19-H · Familiarity, exposure and liking
- [V] ★ Montoya, Horton, Vevea, Citkowicz & Lauber 2017, "A re-examination of the mere exposure effect: the influence of repeated exposure on recognition, familiarity, and liking", *Psychol Bull* 143(5):459–498, PMID 28263645, doi:10.1037/bul0000085 (abstract read): meta-analysis of 268 curve estimates from 81 articles; the exposure–liking relationship is characterised by a positive slope and a negative quadratic term — an inverted-U — associated with (a) all visual but not auditory stimuli, (b) exposure durations shorter than 10 s and longer than 1 min, (c) homogeneous and heterogeneous presentation, (d) ratings taken after all stimuli were presented; "existing models for the mere exposure effect do not adequately account for the findings". #### 19-I · Reasons-introspection, and online-dating matching claims

### Myths and corrections
- [✗] ★ "Opposites attract." Meta-analytic evidence (Montoya 2008: 460 effect sizes; actual similarity r = .47, perceived r = .39), the finding that couples resemble each other on attitudes and values (Luo & Klohnen 2005) and on measured personality (Youyou 2017: rs .20–.47) all go the other way; what is surprising is that ACTUAL personality similarity adds almost nothing to satisfaction beyond each partner's own traits (Dyrenforth 2010: < .5%; Weidmann 2017: "negligible"). So: similarity predicts who ends up together; it does not predict who is happier.
- [✗] ★ "Compatibility algorithms can match you to the right person." Finkel et al. 2012: "no compelling evidence" that matching algorithms work; Joel et al. 2017: with > 100 pre-date self-report measures, unique-partner desire in 4-minute dates was predicted at about 0% (−4.6% to +1.3%); Joel et al. 2020: individual-difference predictors and partner reports add nothing beyond a person's own relationship-specific perceptions, and change in relationship quality is "largely unpredictable". What survives: a person's general tendency to desire others and to be desired (actor/partner variance, 4–27%) is somewhat predictable. A narrow reading: algorithms might still help with long-term compatibility among people who have met — nobody has shown it (Joel 2017 says so).
- [✗] ★ "Your stated type predicts who you'll fall for" (and its mirror, "stated preferences are meaningless"): both overstated. Stated ideals are stable measures of beliefs (e.g. for earning prospects the stated assessments correlated r = .39–.53 with each other in Eastwick & Finkel 2008), predict evaluations of DESCRIBED people (Eastwick et al. 2014: hypothetical targets), and in a 10,358-person registered test showed small preference-matching effects (pattern β = .19, trait-level β ≈ .04; Eastwick 2025); but in live 4-minute encounters they predicted almost nothing in the original studies (72 correlations, 2 positive significant, 1 negative). Per-trait tests are statistically weak by design (Zhao 2025; Conroy-Beam & Buss 2016).
- [✗] Any "men want X, women want Y" line — NOT a lesson claim, in either direction. Sex-difference claims in this literature (both "stated differences vanish when people meet live" and "the differences hold") are contested in method and depend on the paradigm: Eastwick & Finkel 2008 and Selterman et al. 2015 report no moderation by sex in live speed dating, while Li et al. 2013 report that the sexes' choices separated only in long-term contexts (abstract). Lesson 19 takes no side. The only cleared statement is the meta-analytic one from Eastwick et al. 2014: attractiveness (r ≈ .40) and earning prospects (r ≈ .10) predicted romantic evaluations to a similar degree for both sexes (difference r = .03, n.s.). The care rule bans any further group-difference or "evolutionary" claim.
- [✗] "Familiarity breeds contempt" as a rule: Reis et al. 2011 (live interaction: more interaction, more attraction) vs Norton, Frost & Ariely 2007/2011 (randomly assembled trait lists): the effect depends on what information increases; mere-exposure liking is inverted-U (Montoya 2017), not monotonic.
- [✗] "Love at first sight is a reliable predictor of lasting love" — not tested in anything opened; the strongest relevant result is that attraction to a specific partner after 4 minutes is mostly "relationship variance" (31–36% of variance in desire) that traits measured beforehand cannot predict, while post-date chemistry can (16–29%) (Joel 2017). No data on longevity from the sources opened → do not claim.
- [V] ★ Classic "computer dance": Walster, Aronson, Abrahams & Rottman 1966, "Importance of physical attractiveness in dating behavior", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 4(5):508–516, doi:10.1037/h0021188, PMID 6008393 (abstract read via OpenAlex): individuals "randomly paired with one another at a Computer Dance"; the level-of-aspiration (matching) hypothesis was NOT confirmed; "Regardless of S's own attractiveness, by far the largest determinant of how much his partner was liked, how much he wanted to date the partner again, and how often he actually asked the partner out was simply how attractive the partner was"; personality measures (MMPI, Minnesota Counseling Inventory, Berger Self-Acceptance scale) and intellectual measures (scholastic aptitude, high-school percentile rank) "did not predict couple compatibility." (Sample size 752 [~] second-hand only.) A 1966 single-event field study; modern speed-dating studies (Eastwick & Finkel 2008: r ≈ .43–.46; Luo & Zhang 2009) are consistent. Eastwick & Finkel (2008, manuscript) describe Feingold 1990's meta-analysis (7 dating studies incl. the computer dance; *JPSP* 59(5):981–993, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.59.5.981, abstract not retrieved) as finding a positive correlation between liking and date attractiveness for both sexes with a small, nonsignificant sex difference — second-hand.
- [def] 理想型 ideal partner preferences = ratings of the desirability of attributes (attractiveness, earning prospects, warmth …) in a hypothetical partner — Eastwick et al. 2014 abstract. 现场吸引 initial/live attraction = romantic interest after meeting in person (speed dating = a series of ~4-minute dates). 偏好匹配 preference matching = whether partners who match (vs mismatch) one's ideals are evaluated more positively — Eastwick et al. 2025.

### Boundary
- Settings: speed-dating and lab encounters are minutes long, among undergraduates or volunteers; initial attraction after four minutes ≠ long-run partner choice ≠ relationship quality. Joel 2017 itself says its findings "address only obliquely" long-term compatibility.
- Samples: almost all speed-dating studies are young, university-based, mostly Western and heterosexual-dominant (Joel 2017 names undergraduates and limited demographic diversity; Joel 2020's 43 datasets are all from six Western countries; the 2025 registered report is the exception: 43 countries).
- Stated preferences are not worthless: they predict evaluations of described people (Eastwick 2014), show small matching effects in a 10,358-person registered test (β ≈ .11–.19 whole-profile, ≈ .04 per trait), and prospectively predict later partners' traits in a 763-person preregistered study (Gerlach 2019); the live-encounter null may partly be a power/restricted-range artefact (Li 2013; Zhao 2025). The honest summary is "weak, context-dependent link", not "no link".
- Predictability is an upper bound under one dataset and one measurement set: "R² ≈ 0 from > 100 measures" shows these measures cannot predict unique desire; it does not prove no measure ever could (Joel 2017 suggests genomics-scale samples might reveal many tiny interactions).
- Relationship-quality prediction relies on self-report for both predictors and outcomes (99.4% of 1,149 relationship-specific variables were rating scales; shared-method variance inflates the 45%) and on couples who were already together (survivor selection).
- Not claimed: anything about sex/gender or other group differences beyond the Eastwick 2014 meta-analytic statement (similar attractiveness r ≈ .40 for both sexes); anything about dating tactics; anything about "types" of people.

#### 20-A · Independence and social influence: does talking to others ruin the crowd?
- [def] ★ 群体智慧 wisdom of crowds: the aggregate of many individual judgments (mean/median) is more accurate than a typical individual. Davis-Stober et al. 2014 give the precise definition: "a crowd is wise if a linear aggregate, for example a mean, of its members' judgments is closer to the target value than a randomly, but not necessarily uniformly, sampled member of the crowd." — *Decision* 1(2):79–101, doi:10.1037/dec0000004 (abstract read).
- [V] ★ Surowiecki's four conditions — 多样性 diversity of opinion, 独立性 independence (members not swayed by an opinion leader), 去中心化 decentralisation (local knowledge), 聚合 aggregation (a good method for combining opinions): verified against the publisher-linked description of *The Wisdom of Crowds* (2004; Publishers Weekly listing for ISBN 978-0-385-50386-0). The book itself was not opened — chapter-level claims (e.g. which example illustrates which condition) are [~].
- [V] ★ Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer & Helbing 2011, "How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect", *PNAS* 108(22):9020–9025, PMID 21576485, doi:10.1073/pnas.1008636108 (abstract read; design details from the re-analysis by Mavrodiev et al., arXiv 1204.3463): N = 144 subjects (ETH Zürich students) in 12 sessions of 12; six factual estimation questions (geography and crime statistics) per session, two per information condition; each question answered over five periods (independent first estimate, then four revisions); conditions — no information, the average of others' previous answers ("aggregate"), or all others' answers ("full"); incentives for accuracy. Result: "even mild social influence" narrows the diversity of estimates without improving the collective error ("social influence effect"), moves the truth to the periphery of the range of estimates ("range reduction effect") and raises confidence without improving accuracy ("confidence effect"). Note: these are NOT claims that groups got worse on average accuracy in every condition — the abstract says diversity fell "without improvements of its collective error".
- [V] ★ Counter-evidence #1 — Becker, Brackbill & Centola 2017, "Network dynamics of social influence in the wisdom of crowds", *PNAS* 114(26):E5070–E5076, PMID 28607070, doi:10.1073/pnas.1615978114 (abstract read; numbers from the accepted manuscript text): 1,360 online participants; networks of 40; 13 experimental trials × 520 subjects per condition (decentralised vs centralised networks; 1,040 experimental subjects) plus 8 control groups of 40 (320 controls); 4–5 numerical estimation questions per trial (e.g. calories in a plate of food); three rounds (independent, then revised after seeing the average of network neighbours twice). In DECENTRALISED networks, diversity fell by 43% yet accuracy rose: average individual error −23%, error of the group median −12% (0.76 → 0.67 s.d.), error of the group mean −10% (0.69 → 0.62 s.d.); controls (no social information) improved only 3%. In CENTRALISED networks (one hub with many ties) diversity fell by 42% but neither group mean (P > .63) nor median (P > .78) improved reliably — the outcome followed the hub's initial estimate. A published comment exists (PNAS 114(44):E9184); contents not opened.
- [V] Becker, Porter & Centola 2019, "The wisdom of partisan crowds", *PNAS* 116(22):10717–10722, PMID 31085635, doi:10.1073/pnas.1817195116 (abstract read): two web experiments on factual questions known to elicit partisan bias; social information exchange within politically homogeneous networks increased accuracy and reduced polarisation (abstract gives no N). Limited to those questions/settings.
- [V] Mavrodiev, Tessone & Schweitzer 2013, "Quantifying the effects of social influence", *Sci Rep* 3:1360, PMID 23449043, doi:10.1038/srep01360 (abstract read): re-analysing the Lorenz-type experiment, individual adjustments depend linearly on the distance to the mean of all guesses; the effect of social influence on accuracy depends on the group's initial collective error and diversity (arXiv 1204.3463, 2nd-hand summary); in that data the arithmetic mean of first guesses was closer to the truth than individuals' first estimates in only 21.3% of cases, vs 77.1% for the log-scale mean (2nd-hand from the arXiv text; shows that estimates must be scaled for the mean to work).
- [V] Farrell 2011 (PNAS 108(36):E625, "Social influence benefits the wisdom of individuals in the crowd", PMID 21876181) is a published comment on Lorenz et al., with an author reply (E626); letter text not opened — [~] only record verified.
- [V] Jayles, Kim, Escobedo, Cezera, Blanchet, Kameda et al. 2017, "How social information can improve estimation accuracy in human groups", *PNAS* 114(47):12620–12625, doi:10.1073/pnas.1703695114 (abstract read): subjects answered, then revised after seeing the average of previous participants; unbeknownst to them virtual participants always giving the true answer controlled the quality/quantity of social information; social influence can improve group accuracy depending on the quality and quantity of information; "giving slightly incorrect information could drive the group to a better performance" (model). 
- [V] ★ Davis-Stober, Budescu, Dana & Broomell 2014 (theory + re-analysis of two published datasets; abstract read): "crowd wisdom is highly robust: even if judgments are biased and correlated, one would need to nearly deterministically select only a highly skilled judge before an individual's judgment could be expected to be more accurate than a simple averaging of the crowd"; contrary to the folk explanation that errors must be independent, "crowd wisdom is maximized when judgments systematically differ as much as possible" — an accuracy rationale for diversity. 
- STUDY Social influence undermines crowd accuracy (Lorenz) — original: Lorenz et al. 2011 (N = 144; 12 sessions of 12; five rounds; aggregate or full information on others' answers; diversity ↓ without collective-error gain; range reduction; overconfidence). Later evidence: Becker et al. 2017 (N = 1,360; decentralised networks: group-mean error −10%, median −12%, individual error −23% while diversity −43%; centralised networks: no reliable gain, follows the hub); Mavrodiev 2013 (effect depends on initial error and diversity); Jayles 2017 (information quality matters); Becker 2019 (homogeneous partisan networks improved accuracy), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE B: the direction "social influence reduces diversity" is replicated (−42/−43% in Becker 2017) but "and therefore hurts accuracy" is NOT general — in decentralised networks accuracy improved; harm appears with a dominant hub or a crowd already near the truth. — Lorenz 2011 (PMID 21576485); Becker 2017 (PMID 28607070)

#### 20-B · The averaging principle: why averaging beats the average judge, and why people distrust it
- [V] ★ Larrick & Soll 2006, "Intuitions about combining opinions: misappreciation of the averaging principle", *Management Science* 52(1):111–127, doi:10.1287/mnsc.1050.0459 (abstract read; an Erratum, 52(2):309–310, doi:10.1287/mnsc.1060.0518, only fixes a typesetting mislabel of Figures 1–4 and Tables 1–3 — no numbers changed): if two judges' estimates fall on opposite sides of the truth ("bracketing"), "averaging must outperform the average judge for convex loss functions, such as mean absolute deviation". Four experiments: many people hold the false belief that the average of two judges' estimates would be no more accurate than the average judge; the misconception was common across tasks (summary data, instances, conceptual), but weakened as bracketing rate rose or became more transparent; flawed inferential rules and poor extensional reasoning contribute; people "may face few opportunities to learn the benefits of averaging". (No percentages are in the abstract — do not quote any.)
- [V] Soll & Larrick 2009, "Strategies for revising judgment: how (and how well) people use others' opinions", *J Exp Psychol Learn Mem Cogn* 35(3):780–805, doi:10.1037/a0015145 — bibliographic record verified only (no abstract retrieved); do not quote its findings.
- [V] ★ Mannes, Soll & Larrick 2014, "The wisdom of select crowds", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 107(2):276–299, PMID 25090129, doi:10.1037/a0036677 (abstract read): averaging "typically more accurate than most of the individual estimates" and often exceeds the individual perceived as most knowledgeable, but neither averaging nor relying on one judge is robust; the "select-crowd" strategy (rank judges on a cue to ability, e.g. accuracy of recent judgments, and average the top ~5) was accurate and robust in simulation and in 90 archival datasets; in 3 experiments people "are drawn to experts and dislike crowd averages" but view the select-crowd strategy favourably.
- [V] ★ "The crowd within": Vul & Pashler 2008, *Psychol Sci* 19(7):645–647, PMID 18727777, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02136.x (abstract partly retrieved): the average of two guesses from the SAME person tends to beat either single guess. The pre-registered replication by Steegen, Dewitte, Tuerlinckx & Vanpaemel 2014 (*Front Psychol* 5:786, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00786; abstract + results table read) "successfully replicated the crowd within effect" both immediately and 3 weeks later: immediate (n = 471) average vs guess 1 d = 0.40 [0.31, 0.49], vs guess 2 d = 0.47 [0.38, 0.57]; delayed (n = 140 returning) d = 0.34 [0.17, 0.51] and 0.72 [0.53, 0.90]; original n = 255 (immediate) and 173 (delayed) with mean squared error 555/638 (guesses 1/2) vs 508 for the average (immediate) and 542/610 vs 447 (delayed). The original's claim that the delay boosts the benefit was only partly confirmed (delay effect not significant against guess 1, p = .858).
- STUDY Averaging beats the typical judge, and people underuse it (misappreciation of averaging) — original: Larrick & Soll 2006 (four experiments; bracketing logic; most people wrongly think averaging two judges is no better than the average judge). Later evidence: Mannes et al. 2014 (90 archival datasets; select crowds of 5 accurate and robust; people dislike crowd averages), Steegen et al. 2014 (pre-registered replication of the inner-crowd effect, N = 471 + 140), Davis-Stober 2014 (averaging beats a random member under broad conditions), as of Oct 2026.
  GRADE A: the arithmetic of bracketing is a mathematical fact; the "people misjudge it" finding and the inner-crowd effect have been replicated (a pre-registered replication succeeded); the robust advantage of averaging over the typical member appears across theory and 90 datasets. The exact size of the misconception is not given in the sources opened. — Larrick & Soll 2006; Mannes 2014; Steegen 2014

#### 20-C · Diversity, ability and the Condorcet theorem
- [V] ★ Hong & Page 2004, "Groups of diverse problem solvers can outperform groups of high-ability problem solvers", *PNAS* 101(46):16385–16389, PMID 15534225, doi:10.1073/pnas.0403723101 (abstract read): a modelling framework of "functionally diverse problem-solving agents"; "a team of randomly selected agents outperforms a team comprised of the best-performing agents" because "as the initial pool of problem solvers becomes large, the best-performing agents necessarily become similar". It is a MODEL result (computer simulations plus a theorem), not an experiment on people; Page 2007 (*The Difference*) popularised it as "diversity trumps ability".
- [V] ★ The critique and the defence: Thompson 2014, "Does diversity trump ability?", *Notices of the AMS* 61(9):1024ff, doi:10.1090/noti1163 (record verified via Crossref; the article could not be opened — content here is second-hand): argues that in the Hong–Page model the randomness of how the diverse groups are formed, not diversity, explains their success, and that the theorem has little real-world application. Singer 2019, "Diversity, not randomness, trumps ability", *Philos Sci* 86(1):178–191, doi:10.1086/701074 (abstract read) defends the diversity interpretation: "the failure of Thompson's argument shows that to understand the value of functional diversity, we should be clearer about how we conceive of and measure that diversity." Luan, Katsikopoulos & Reimer 2012, *PLoS ONE* 7(2):e31043, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0031043 (abstract read): in simulations, "individual accuracy is more important in task environments in which cues differ greatly in the quality of their information, and diversity matters more when such differences are relatively small". Verdict: it is a conditional, model-based claim; the contested part is the slogan, not the arithmetic.
- [def] ★ Diversity prediction theorem (Page 2007): collective squared error = average individual squared error − diversity (variance of predictions) — this is the algebraic identity already DERIVED in FACTS_T6a; it holds for any numbers and says nothing about whether diversity can be had for free. (Page's book was not opened; the identity needs no source.)
- [V] ★ Condorcet jury theorem 孔多塞陪审团定理: [def] if each voter is independently more likely than not to be right (p > ½), the probability that the majority is right rises with group size toward 1 (and falls toward 0 if p < ½). The correlated-error caveat: Ladha 1992, "The Condorcet jury theorem, free speech, and correlated votes", *Am J Polit Sci* 36(3):617–634, doi:10.2307/2111584 (abstract read): the classic theorem assumes independent votes; generalised to correlated votes, "for large groups, Condorcet's result would hold under fairly general conditions. For small groups, the conditions are severe." Dietrich 2008, *Episteme* 5(1):56–73, doi:10.3366/E1742360008000233 (title/venue verified; abstract not retrieved): argues the theorem's premises (independence, competence) are not simultaneously justified. Kao & Couzin 2014, *Proc R Soc B* 281:20133305, doi:10.1098/rspb.2013.3305 (abstract read): when cues are correlated across space/time and several cues exist, "in only a minority of environments" does accuracy increase monotonically with group size; often a finite, small group size maximises accuracy.
- STUDY Diverse groups beat groups of the best (Hong–Page "diversity trumps ability") — original: Hong & Page 2004 (agent-based model + theorem; random teams beat teams of best individual performers). Later evidence: Thompson 2014 (critique: randomness explains success), Singer 2019 (defence), Luan et al. 2012 (simulations: depends on how much cue quality differs across the environment), Davis-Stober 2014 (accuracy rationale for diversity), as of Oct 2026. No human experiment testing the "trumps ability" claim was found.
  GRADE C: the theorem is valid for the model, but the real-world claim "diversity trumps ability" rests on simulations, is conditional on the task environment, and is disputed in print; teach the conditional statement (diversity helps when members' errors differ and ability differences are small), not the slogan. — Hong & Page 2004; Thompson 2014; Luan 2012
- STUDY Majority accuracy grows with group size (Condorcet jury theorem) — original: Condorcet 1785 (theorem). Later evidence: Ladha 1992 (correlated votes: holds for large groups only under fairly general conditions; severe for small groups), Kao & Couzin 2014 (correlated cues: small groups often optimal), Dietrich 2008 (premises not jointly justified).
  GRADE B: a mathematical theorem with known, well-documented boundary conditions; its assumptions (independent, better-than-chance voters) often fail in real groups. — Ladha 1992; Kao & Couzin 2014

#### 20-D · Cascades and social influence in markets of opinion
- [def] ★ 信息级联 information cascade (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992, "A theory of fads, fashion, custom, and cultural change as informational cascades", *J Polit Econ* 100(5):992–1026, doi:10.1086/261849; bibliographic record verified, paper not opened): sequential decision-makers, each seeing private signals and predecessors' choices, rationally ignore their own signal once the public history outweighs it; later decisions then carry no new information, so a cascade can lock in a wrong choice. Anderson & Holt's classroom description (J Econ Perspect 10(4):187–193, 1996, doi:10.1257/jep.10.4.187, abstract read): "Once a cascade starts, it is rational for students to ignore their private signals and follow the pattern of previous decisions."
- [~] Anderson & Holt 1997, "Information cascades in the laboratory", *Am Econ Rev* 87(5):847–862: the paper could not be located through the sources used (Crossref search returned other items), so none of its numbers (the often-quoted share of rounds in cascades) are given — see COULD NOT VERIFY. Hung & Plott 2001 (*Am Econ Rev* 91(5):1508–1520, doi:10.1257/aer.91.5.1508): title "Information cascades: replication and an extension to majority rule and conformity-rewarding institutions" — record only.
- [V] ★ Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, "Experimental study of inequality and unpredictability in an artificial cultural market", *Science* 311(5762):854–856, PMID 16469928, doi:10.1126/science.1121066 (abstract + the author-hosted PDF read, https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_dodds_watts06.pdf and the full-length supplement): an artificial "music market" with 14,341 participants (Experiment 1: 7,149 subjects, 69 days from 7 Oct 2004; Experiment 2: 7,192 subjects, 83 days to 8 Mar 2005; mostly teenagers/young adults from one US website) downloading 48 previously unknown songs by 48 unknown bands, either with an independent condition (no download counts) or with download counts shown in one of eight parallel "social influence worlds" that evolved independently; "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success. Success was also only partly determined by quality: The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible." ("Quality" = market share in the independent condition; Experiment 2 used a ranked list rather than a grid, giving stronger influence.)
- [V] Krumme, Cebrian, Pickard & Pentland 2012, "Quantifying social influence in an online cultural market", *PLoS ONE* 7(5):e33785 (not PNAS, as the task list had it), PMID 22590493, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0033785 (abstract read): re-analyses the ~14,000-user data; behaviour is a two-step process (sample, then download) and "social influence is material to the first step only" — the effect works mainly through which songs get sampled (position), with feedback cues serving an informational rather than normative role.
- [V] Abeliuk, Berbeglia, Cebrian & Van Hentenryck 2015, "The benefits of social influence in optimized cultural markets", *PLoS ONE* 10(4):e0121934, PMID 25831093, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0121934 (abstract read; the task list's "Abeliuk 2015?" is confirmed as PLoS ONE): in computational experiments, a profit-maximising policy that uses quality, appeal, position bias and social signals "leverages social influence to produce significant performance benefits" without introducing significant unpredictability and identifies "blockbusters"; a simulation/theory result, not a human experiment.
- [V] ★ Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, "Social influence bias: a randomized experiment", *Science* 341(6146):647–651, PMID 23929980, doi:10.1126/science.1240466 (abstract read; N and duration from MIT News 8 Aug 2013): a randomised experiment on a social news aggregation website — ratings on 101,281 comments were randomly manipulated over five months. CORRECTION to the task list: positive social influence increased the likelihood of a positive rating by 32% (not "+25% odds of up-votes") and accumulating herding raised final ratings by 25% on average; MIT News adds "30% more likely to receive a very high favorable rating". Negative manipulation inspired users to correct the manipulated rating (asymmetric herding). The effect was topic-dependent and depended on whether users saw friends' or enemies' opinions. Two comments were published (Science 342:1315). 
- STUDY Social influence makes success unpredictable and unequal (MusicLab) — original: Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006 (N = 14,341; 48 songs; one independent condition + eight parallel social-influence worlds per experiment; stronger influence → more inequality and unpredictability; quality only partly determines success). Later evidence: Krumme et al. 2012 (reanalysis: influence acts at the sampling step), Muchnik et al. 2013 (field RCT, 101,281 comments: positive herding +32% likelihood, +25% final rating; negative influence corrected), Abeliuk et al. 2015 (simulation: influence can be used beneficially), as of Oct 2026. No direct independent replication of the artificial market with new songs was found.
  GRADE B: direction replicated in two designs (artificial market; field randomised trial) and in re-analyses, but the artificial market was run once with ~14,000 mostly young participants and the field trial covered one news site; effect sizes depend strongly on the interface. — Salganik 2006 (PMID 16469928); Muchnik 2013 (PMID 23929980)
- STUDY Information cascades — original: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch 1992 (theory; no data). Later evidence: Anderson & Holt 1997 (lab; not retrieved), Hung & Plott 2001 (replication; record only), Çelen & Kariv 2004 (*Am Econ Rev* 94(3):484–498, doi:10.1257/0002828041464461; record only — "Distinguishing informational cascades from herd behavior in the laboratory"); no numbers verified here.

#### 20-E · Brainstorming: groups produce fewer ideas than the same people working alone
- [V] ★ The claim being tested: Osborn (1957, *Applied Imagination*; book not opened) "claimed that if these rules are followed 'the average person can think up twice as many ideas when working with a group than when working alone'" (p. 229, as quoted by Diehl & Stroebe 1987). The first test: Taylor, Berry & Block 1958 had people brainstorm for 12 minutes individually or in 4-person groups, forming "nominal groups" (pooling the non-redundant ideas of 4 people who had worked alone); "contrary to Osborn's claim, Taylor et al. found that nominal groups produced nearly twice as many different ideas as the real groups" (Diehl & Stroebe 1987, text read in a hosted copy).
- [V] ★ Diehl & Stroebe 1987, "Productivity loss in brainstorming groups: toward the solution of a riddle", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 53(3):497–509, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.53.3.497 (full text read, hosted copy https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Productivity-Loss-In-Brainstorming_Toward-the-Solution-of-a-Riddle.pdf): the paper's own review — "Of the 22 experiments listed in Table 1, 18 reported the performance of nominal groups to be superior to that of real groups, and only 4, all involving 2-person groups, … reported no difference"; for quality, all six studies measuring total quality had nominal groups better, other measures inconsistent. (CORRECTION: this "18 of 22" count is the 1987 paper's own tally, not Mullen et al.'s meta-analysis, to which a course document attributed it.) Four experiments of their own: Exp 1 (48 male students aged 15–17, 15 minutes; group vs individual sessions under personal vs collective assessment) — productivity was higher under personal assessment but session type "still had a major impact" with free riding eliminated; Exp 2 — inducing evaluation apprehension lowered individual productivity; Exp 3 — no interaction between evaluation apprehension and session type (doubt about it as a main cause); Exp 4 — production blocking directly manipulated, "accounted for most of the productivity loss of real brainstorming groups".
- [V] ★ Exp 4 numbers (Table 5 of the 1987 paper; 60 psychology students in same-sex 4-person groups; topic: unemployment; 15 minutes; number of non-redundant ideas per group, group means): group control 55.67; Condition 1 (blocking, with communication) 37.67; Condition 2 (blocking, no communication) 45.67; Condition 3 (no blocking, no communication) 102.67; individual (nominal) control 106.00. Authors: subjects able to speak when ideas occurred "produced approximately twice as many ideas as they did when working under conditions in which subjects had to wait their turn"; whether subjects could overhear each other made little difference to productivity; talk filled only 73% of the time available. Design caveat: 60 students ≈ 15 groups, i.e. very few groups per cell (cell sizes not stated in the part read); the "light signal" system simulated a one-speaker-at-a-time rule.
- [V] ★ Mullen, Johnson & Salas 1991, "Productivity loss in brainstorming groups: a meta-analytic integration", *Basic Appl Soc Psychol* 12(1):3–23, doi:10.1207/s15324834basp1201_1 (abstract read via OpenAlex record of the PsycINFO abstract): "Generally, brainstorming groups are significantly less productive than nominal groups, in terms of both quantity and quality"; productivity loss stronger for (a) larger groups, (b) experimenter presence, (c) tape-recorded vocalisation vs writing, (d) comparison with a nominal group of truly alone individuals (vs together). The abstract gives no k, d or r — none are given here (COULD NOT VERIFY: the task file's "k = 18, effect size").
- [V] Paulus, Dzindolet, Poletes & Camacho 1993, "Perception of performance in group brainstorming: the illusion of group productivity", *Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 19(1):78–89, doi:10.1177/0146167293191009 (abstract read): "most individuals believed they would generate more ideas in groups than alone"; those who actually brainstormed in groups also rated their performance more favourably than those alone; the illusion may derive in part from social comparison, and people "tend to take credit for a disproportionate amount of the brainstorming activity in groups". Explains why brainstorming stays popular despite the data.
- [V] ★ Girotra, Terwiesch & Ulrich 2010, "Idea generation and the quality of the best idea", *Management Science* 56(4):591–605, doi:10.1287/mnsc.1090.1144 (abstract read): the "team structure" (work together) vs the "hybrid structure" (individuals work alone first, then together), judged by the quality of the best ideas; "groups organized in the hybrid structure are able to generate more ideas, to generate better ideas, and to better discern the quality of the ideas they generate"; building on others' ideas "is counterproductive" (no more ideas, no better ideas). One experiment; N not in the abstract.
- [~] Electronic brainstorming (anonymous typing at computers), where blocking disappears: Dennis & Valacich 1993, "Computer brainstorms: more heads are better than one", *J Appl Psychol* 78(4):531–537, doi:10.1037/0021-9010.78.4.531; DeRosa, Smith & Hantula 2007 (meta-analysis, *Comput Hum Behav* 23(3):1549–1581, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2005.07.003); Dennis & Williams 2005 (*Int J e-Collab* 1(1):24–42, doi:10.4018/jec.2005010102). - STUDY Interactive brainstorming groups produce fewer ideas than the same people working alone (productivity loss) — original: Taylor, Berry & Block 1958 (12 minutes, 4-person groups; nominal groups produced nearly twice as many different ideas); Diehl & Stroebe 1987 (tally: 18 of 22 experiments nominal > real, the other 4 all 2-person groups; own Exp 4: blocking cut output from ≈ 103–106 to ≈ 38–46 ideas per 4-person group). #### 20-F · Social loafing 社会惰化 and the Ringelmann effect
- [V] ★ Latané, Williams & Harkins 1979, "Many hands make light the work: the causes and consequences of social loafing", *J Pers Soc Psychol* 37(6):822–832, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.37.6.822 (abstract read via OpenAlex): "Two experiments found that when asked to perform the physically exerting tasks of clapping and shouting, people exhibit a sizable decrease in individual effort when performing in groups as compared to when they perform alone. ### Myths and corrections
- [✗] ★ "Brainstorming in a group is the best way to generate ideas." Quantity: in Diehl & Stroebe's tally 18 of 22 experiments had pooled individuals ahead of interacting groups (the other 4 were all 2-person groups); Mullen 1991: groups significantly less productive in quantity AND quality; the main mechanism is blocking (≈ 38–46 vs ≈ 103–106 ideas per 4-person group in the blocking experiment). People nonetheless believe groups do better (the illusion of group productivity, Paulus 1993). What survives: structured hybrids (alone first, then together; Girotra 2010, one experiment).
- [✗] ★ "The more people, the more effort" — social loafing (78-study meta-analysis; robust across tasks) reduces individual effort in additive tasks when contributions are not identifiable or the task is not meaningful. And the textbook Ringelmann story is partly folklore: Ringelmann's 1880s data were on pushing/pulling a load; the rope-pulling picture and per-person percentages come via Moede 1927 and later retellings (Kravitz & Martin 1986).
- [✗] ★ "Crowds are always wise." Needs independence/diversity/decentralisation/aggregation (Surowiecki; four conditions verified at the publisher-linked description) — but also not "always broken by influence": decentralised networks improved accuracy (Becker 2017: −10% group-mean error) while a central hub (centralised networks) removed the gain; Lorenz 2011's harm was in diversity, not always in accuracy. Davis-Stober 2014: averaging beats a random member under very broad conditions.
- [✗] "Two heads are better than one" (as a rule): depends on the task and the process — hidden-profile groups are eight times less likely to find the best solution than full-information groups (Lu 2012); real groups lose ideas to blocking; but structured aggregation (markets, weighted polls, select crowds) can beat individuals (Atanasov 2017; Mannes 2014).
- [✗] "Diversity trumps ability" as a slogan: Hong & Page 2004 is a model; its real-world form is disputed (Thompson 2014 vs Singer 2019); the supportable form is conditional (Luan 2012). The diversity prediction identity itself is algebra, not a claim about people.
- [✗] "Groupthink is a proven law of cohesive groups." The one meta-analysis opened (Mullen et al. 1994) found no overall effect of cohesiveness on decision quality; Park 1990 called empirical tests few. Teach as a list of risk factors.
- [✗] "Collective intelligence is a trait of smart groups, like IQ": the c factor explains a modest share (AVE corrected from 44% to 19.6%), is disputed (Credé & Howardson 2017), and is not strongly tied to members' average or maximum IQ in the original (Woolley 2010).
- [✗] Task-list slips corrected: Krumme et al. 2012 is *PLoS ONE*, not *PNAS*; Abeliuk et al. 2015 is *PLoS ONE*; Muchnik 2013's headline is +32% likelihood of a positive rating / +25% final rating (not "+25% odds of up-votes"); the "18 of 22" brainstorming count belongs to Diehl & Stroebe 1987, not Mullen 1991; Becker 2017 is N = 1,360 (confirmed) — the group sizes are 40, not 1,360 per network.
- Better than their reputation: the "crowd within" (averaging your own two guesses) survived a pre-registered replication (Steegen 2014, N = 471 + 140; d = 0.34–0.72); Larrick & Soll's averaging principle is arithmetic; Salganik's MusicLab is a clean randomised design that has not been overturned.

### Bet candidates
- BET 20-1 (hidden profiles): Across 65 studies and 3,189 groups, how much less likely were groups holding the "hidden profile" (the best answer only discoverable by pooling what each member privately knew) to find the best solution than groups who were simply given all the information? TRUTH: eight times less likely; groups mentioned two standard deviations more common information than unique information. Tolerance: "several times less likely" accepted; a guess of "about equal" or "2×" is the textbook-wrong answer. Grade A. — Lu, Yuan & McLeod 2012 (abstract). Not sensitive.
- BET 20-2 (blocking): Four people brainstorm for 15 minutes on "unemployment". In one condition they can speak whenever an idea occurs; in another a light signal means only one person may speak at a time (they cannot hear each other either). How do the numbers of non-redundant ideas per group compare? TRUTH: ≈ 103–106 ideas per group when everyone could speak (individual control 106.00; "no blocking, no communication" 102.67) vs ≈ 38–46 ideas when waiting for a turn (37.67 with communication, 45.67 without) and 55.67 for ordinary group control — "approximately twice as many" when not blocked. Tolerance: "about double". Grade B (one experiment, 60 students). — Diehl & Stroebe 1987 Table 5. Not sensitive.
- BET 20-3 (MusicLab): In a 14,341-person artificial music market (48 unknown songs; eight parallel worlds each in which people saw others' download counts), could the final ranking of a song be predicted from its "quality" (its popularity when nobody saw counts)? TRUTH: only partly — "the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible"; stronger social influence → more inequality and more unpredictability. Tolerance: qualitative pick. Grade B. — Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006 (abstract). Not sensitive.
- BET 20-4 (network wisdom): In groups of 40 who estimated quantities (e.g. calories in a plate of food) and then saw the average of their network neighbours' guesses, by how much did the spread of estimates (SD) shrink after two rounds, and did the group's average get better or worse in decentralised networks? TRUTH: SD fell 43%, and the group mean's error fell by 10% (median −12%, individual error −23%); in networks with a hub (SD −42%) there was no reliable improvement. Tolerance: "diversity down by about two-fifths; accuracy up by about a tenth". Grade B. — Becker, Brackbill & Centola 2017 (accepted manuscript; PMID 28607070). Not sensitive.

- ★ DERIVED closed forms for a toy "n judges with correlated errors" model (no source needed beyond the variance of a sum): let judge i's error e_i have variance σ² and each pair correlation ρ; then Var(ē) = (1/n²)[nσ² + n(n−1)ρσ²] = σ²[ρ + (1 − ρ)/n]. Limits: ρ = 0 → σ²/n (errors cancel); ρ = 1 → σ² (averaging does nothing); n → ∞ → ρσ² (a floor set by shared error). Effective number of independent judges n_eff = n/[1 + (n − 1)ρ]. If all judges also share a bias b, MSE of the mean = b² + σ²[ρ + (1 − ρ)/n] — averaging never removes b (the "noise vs bias" split of lesson 08). Mean of n copies of the same judge (ρ = 1) gives no gain. Check: for n = 10, ρ = 0.3 → Var(ē)/σ² = 0.3 + 0.7/10 = 0.37; n_eff = 10/(1 + 9·0.3) = 2.70 (consistent: σ²/2.70 = 0.37σ²).
- ★ DERIVED social-influence step (toy): replace each x_i by x_i' = (1 − λ)x_i + λ x̄ (move toward the group mean by fraction λ). Then the arithmetic mean is unchanged (x̄' = x̄) and the variance of estimates shrinks by (1 − λ)² — so influence toward the mean cannot change the crowd mean by itself; harm arises when weights are unequal (a hub: x_i' = Σ_j w_ij x_j with unequal in-weights moves the mean toward the hub's estimate — exactly the Becker 2017 centralised result, DeGroot model). Use λ as a free slider; the published data fix no value of λ (示意).
- Numbers that do NOT exist: a published ρ for human estimation errors in general (it depends on the question; Davis-Stober 2014 treat it as a parameter); any published "wisdom vs n" curve for real groups with interaction; an empirical λ; the "tipping" size of a cascade in people; any per-song rank trajectory from MusicLab in the sources opened. ### Boundary
- Wisdom needs a verifiable numeric or probabilistic target and a rule for combining answers; on matters of taste or values, the "closer to the truth" test has no meaning (Salganik's "quality" is itself defined by independent popularity).
- Aggregation removes noise, not shared bias (lesson 08): the n → ∞ floor is ρσ² + b².
- Interaction is not always harmful: decentralised exchange improved estimates (Becker 2017), and structured deliberation/teams beat polls under strong aggregation (Atanasov 2017); in-person groups can also discover information individuals lack — hidden-profile failures show it needs the right process (information coverage predicted decision quality in Lu 2012).
- Lab/online tasks are short, numeric and one-shot (estimating calories, distances, crime rates); real organisations mix independence and interaction and have incentives and hierarchy; the 43%/10% figures come from 40-person online networks with a small monetary reward.
- Brainstorming results concern idea COUNT in minutes-long sessions with speaking turns; they do not show that discussion is useless for evaluating or selecting ideas.
- Several constructs here are contested (collective intelligence factor; groupthink; the Hong–Page slogan) — present them as conditional, with the grade.
