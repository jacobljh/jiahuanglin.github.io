### "The Dress" 裙子 (Feb 2015)
- ★[V] Origin: Cecilia Bleasdale (mother of the bride) photographed a dress at Cheshire Oaks outlet (Cheshire, England) in Feb 2015, a week before the wedding of Grace and Keir Johnston on Colonsay (Scotland); Grace saw it white/gold. Wedding guest/band member Caitlin McNeill reposted it on Tumblr on the evening of Thu 26 Feb 2015; BuzzFeed (Cates Holderness, 26 Feb) and Wired (Adam Rogers, 26 Feb) ran it the same day; BuzzFeed post > 37 million views by 1 Mar. The retailer's dress (Roman Originals) is royal blue with black lace. — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dress (pointer only; its dates cross-checked against The Drum 27 Feb 2015 "Thursday evening" + Scotsman/BuzzFeed News 27 Feb; I did NOT open the BuzzFeed/Wired originals). Say "26 Feb 2015 went viral", not "25 Feb".
- ★[V] Best survey: Lafer-Sousa, Hermann & Conway 2015, N = 1,401 (313 naive to the image; 53 tested in lab; 28/53 retested): **57.0% blue/black [95% CI 54.4–59.5], 30.3% white/gold [28.0–32.8], 10.6% blue/brown [9.1–12.4], 2.1% other**. Free-response colour naming, mostly online (1,190 US; 161 India; 50 other countries; 731 men/668 women/2 other) — NOT a representative sample, so the percentages describe this sample only. — Current Biology 25(13):R545–R546, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2015.04.053 (opened Supplement PDF).
- [V] Same paper: white/gold answers rose with age and were more common in women; some people flipped on retest (image is multistable like a Necker cube). With the dress embedded in scenes of simulated warm/cool light: 64% of original white/gold perceivers switched to blue/black under the warm-light scene; 80% of original blue/black perceivers switched to white/gold under the cool-light scene. Authors' hypothesis: different internal priors about the illuminant (cool "blue sky" light → discount short wavelengths → white/gold; warm artificial light → blue/black; neutral → blue/brown). — same source.
- [V] Wallisch 2017 (J Vision 17(4):5, doi:10.1167/17.4.5): ~13,000 online observers; assumptions about illumination (natural vs artificial light vs shadow) predict the percept much more strongly than age/gender; circadian type ("larks"/"owls" differing in exposure to long/short wavelength light) related in the predicted direction. {B}: illuminant-prior account is supported by several labs (also Toscani et al., Aston & Hurlbert, Witzel et al. 2017, same J Vision volume — not individually opened), but WHY people hold different priors is correlational/unsettled. — Europe PMC abstract opened.

### The retina in numbers 视网膜
- ★[V] Photoreceptor counts: Curcio, Sloan, Kalina & Hendrickson 1990 (8 whole-mount retinas from 7 donors aged 27–44): **4.6 million cones (4.08–5.29 M) and 92 million rods (77.9–107.3 M)**. The textbook "≈120 M rods, ≈6 M cones" comes from Osterberg 1935 (6.4 M cones; 110–125 M rods, as tabulated by Kolb, Webvision). Say "~90–125 million rods, ~5–6 million cones" or cite one source; do not splice. — J Comp Neurol 292:497–523, doi:10.1002/cne.902920402 (abstract opened); webvision.med.utah.edu "Facts and Figures concerning the human retina" (opened).
- [V] Fovea 中央凹: rod-free centre averages 0.350 mm across (1.25°); peak foveal cone density averages 199,000/mm² but varies ~3× between people (100,000–324,000); cone density falls ~10-fold within 1 mm of the foveal centre. Fovea rim-to-rim ≈ 1.5 mm; 1° of visual angle ≈ 288 µm of retina; the fovea lies ≈ 11.8° (3.4 mm) temporal to the optic-disc edge. — Curcio 1990; Kolb/Webvision.
- [V] ~1 million axons leave each eye (Webvision lists 0.56–1.14 M, 0.8–1.0 M, 1.2 M by study) versus ~97 million photoreceptors: ≥ ~100:1 convergence before anything reaches the brain. — Kolb/Webvision + Curcio (arithmetic mine).
- [~] Cortical share of the centre: ≈ 55% of primary visual cortex (V1) surface represents the central 10° of the visual field (Horton & Hoyt 1991 revision of Holmes's map; figure quoted by Horton's UCSF lab page; Holmes's older map gave ~25% to the central 15°). The popular "1% of the retina gets 50% of the cortex" is a loose paraphrase — I could not trace it to a primary paper; use the 10°/55% version. — Arch Ophthalmol 109:816–824, doi:10.1001/archopht.1991.01080060080030 (abstract opened); hortonlab.ucsf.edu/node/56 (search excerpt).
- ★[V] Blind spot 盲点: the optic disc is 1.86 × 1.75 mm (Kolb) ⇒ ≈ 6° across (derived: 1.8 mm ÷ 0.288 mm/°) and sits ~15° off the fixation point on the temporal side [~ position from general sources]. Edme Mariotte announced it in *Nouvelle découverte touchant la veüe* (Paris: Frédéric Léonard, 1668; a letter to Jean Pecquet). Some web pages say 1660 or "demonstrated to the king" — I found no primary support: use 1668 and drop the anecdote. — encyclopedia.com/Mariotte; historyofmedicine.com catalogue entry (secondary).

### Seth, "controlled hallucination" 受控幻觉 — the exact wording
- ★[V] TED2017 (recorded 24 Apr 2017, published 18 Jul 2017), "Your brain hallucinates your conscious reality", official TED transcript. Seth calls perception "a controlled hallucination" — one in which the brain's predictions are reined in by sensory information from the world — and says "we're all hallucinating all the time"; the key sentence, verbatim: "It's just that when we agree about our hallucinations, we call that reality." — ted.com/talks/anil_seth_your_brain_hallucinates_your_conscious_reality/transcript (transcript JSON opened).
- ★[✗] The lesson's line 「当幻觉与现实达成一致，我们就管它叫『知觉』」 is NOT Seth's: (i) what gets called by the name is **reality** (现实), not "perception"; (ii) the agreement is **among people about their hallucinations**, not between hallucination and reality. Faithful Chinese rendering (translation, mark as such): 「我们一直都在产生幻觉；只不过，当大家对各自的幻觉达成一致时，我们就管它叫『现实』。」 In Seth's own definition, perception IS the (controlled) hallucination — controlled by sensory input.
- [V] *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness* (Faber & Faber / Dutton, 2021) says nearly the same thing; as quoted by MIT Technology Review (M. Hutson, 25 Aug 2021), perceptual experience is "a neuronal fantasy" kept tied to the world by continual best guesses ("controlled hallucinations"), and "when we agree about our hallucinations, that's what we call reality." Page number not checked. — technologyreview.com/2021/08/25/1032121/brains-controlled-hallucination/
- [~] The phrase is not Seth's coinage: secondary reports say Seth credits Chris Frith for the phrase, and that Rick Grush (2004) and Andy Clark (*Surfing Uncertainty*, 2016) trace the slogan to Ramesh Jain's unrecorded talk. I could not open any primary text for this chain — don't assert an origin beyond "popularised by Seth and Clark".
- [def] Predictive processing 预测加工 / "brain as prediction engine" is a framework, not a settled fact — see grading of the broader program in lesson 02/06 notes: evidence is strong for prior-dependent perception (illusions, ambiguous figures), contested for "the brain literally minimises prediction error everywhere" (Seth's own talk is a popular summary, not a test).

### Energy budget and the information bottleneck
- ★[V] Brain ≈ 2% of body weight, ≈ 20% of the body's oxygen (hence calorie) consumption; the rate is "remarkably constant" across mental activity. — Raichle & Gusnard 2002, PNAS 99(16):10237–10239, doi:10.1073/pnas.172399499 (opening paragraph seen in the PMC124895 excerpt; full text not opened). "≈ 20 W" is [~]: it is simply 20% of a ~100 W resting body — I did not find a primary paper stating "20 W" for the human brain; say "roughly 20 W".
- [V] Herculano-Houzel 2012 (PNAS 109 Suppl 1:10661–68, doi:10.1073/pnas.1201895109) calls "100 billion neurons / 20% of energy because human neurons are costlier" a bundle of unverified "facts": the human brain has ≈ 86 billion neurons (86.1 ± 8.1 billion; Azevedo et al. 2009, J Comp Neurol 513:532–541), and its energy cost is what one expects of a scaled-up primate brain with that many neurons.
- [~] Signaling energy shares: Attwell & Laughlin 2001 (rodent grey matter model: action potentials 47%, postsynaptic effects 34%, resting potential 13%, glutamate recycling 3%) were revised by Howarth, Gleeson & Attwell 2012 (cerebral cortex: postsynaptic glutamate receptors 50%, action potentials 21%, resting potentials 20%, presynaptic release 5%, transmitter recycling 4%). Don't quote the 2001 split as human fact. — J Cereb Blood Flow Metab 21:1133–45 (doi:10.1097/00004647-200110000-00001); 32:1222–32 (doi:10.1038/jcbfm.2012.35) (abstracts opened).
- ★[V] Zheng & Meister, "The unbearable slowness of being: Why do we live at 10 bits/s?", Neuron 113(2):192–204 (online Dec 2024, print Jan 2025), doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2024.11.008 (a Perspective — no new experiments; numbers below from the arXiv v2 text 2408.10234): behavioural throughput "about 10 bits/s" (typing 120 words/min ≈ 10 bits/s; blindfolded speedcubing 11.8; StarCraft 10; Tetris ~7; binary-digit memorisation 4.9; listening comprehension ~13; choice-reaction ~5) versus sensory capture "~10^9 bits/s" (one cone ≈ 270 bits/s; ~6 million cones/eye ≈ 1.6 Gbit/s; optic nerve ≈ 10^6 axons × 50 Hz × ~2 bits/spike ≈ 100 Mbit/s); they define a "sifting number" ≈ 10^8. {C}: a single research group's synthesis of heterogeneous tasks; no independent test of the number as a "speed limit".
- [~] Critique: Sauerbrei & Pruszynski 2025 (Nature Neuroscience 28(7):1365–1366, doi:10.1038/s41593-025-01997-0, "The brain works at more than 10 bits per second"): the 10 bits/s limit "appears to hold for high-level cognitive functions", but unconscious real-time motor control — a majority of CNS neurons and most of the information throughput — substantially exceeds it (abstract opened; body paywalled). So: "~10 bits/s for conscious, task-defined behaviour", not "the brain runs at 10 bits/s".
- [~] Two arithmetic cautions for writers: the 6-million-cone figure used by Zheng & Meister is the Osterberg-type textbook number (Curcio: 4.6 M); "10^9" is an order-of-magnitude ceiling (photoreceptor capacity), not what reaches cortex.


## 01 · measurement 测量: introspection 内省 → behaviour 行为 → cognitive revolution 认知革命

Reading order: A introspection and why it stalled; B behaviourism 行为主义 and what its critics actually argued (Watson, Skinner, Chomsky, Köhler); C the cognitive turn (Miller, Broadbent, Neisser); D chronometry 心理计时学 as a method (Donders, Sternberg, Stroop) and the limits of inferring mechanisms from reaction times. Historical claims rest mainly on Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entries I opened plus the primary texts that were openly available (Watson, Chomsky, Miller, Stroop, Sternberg's own pages); I could not open the books (Boring, Broadbent, Neisser, Skinner 1938, Köhler). Net message: disputes about private reports had no referee; behaviourism answered by restricting the data to behaviour; the cognitive turn kept behavioural data but used it to infer hidden processes — a stronger method, but one whose data can often fit more than one mechanism.

### A. Introspection 内省 and why it stalled
- ★[V] Wundt and "1879" 冯特与莱比锡实验室: Wilhelm Wundt (1832–1920) was called to Leipzig in 1875; Alan Kim's SEP entry (rev. 21 Oct 2022) says that in 1879 the university formally recognised his little room of equipment as a laboratory, "the world's first devoted to psychology" — and its note 7 adds that William James had set up a psychological "laboratory" at Harvard in 1875. So "first psychology lab, Leipzig, 1879" is a convention of the Boring tradition, not a clean fact; Boring himself published "On the subjectivity of important historical dates: Leipzig 1879" (J Hist Behav Sci 1(1):5–9, 1965; title taken from the SEP bibliography, paper not opened). Safe wording: "usually dated to 1879". — plato.stanford.edu/entries/wilhelm-wundt/ and .../notes.html (opened).
- [V] Wundt was not the caricature 冯特并非内省的漫画形象: SEP note 4 (Kim 2022) says the once-dominant portraits of Wundt by Titchener 铁钦纳 and Titchener's student Boring are now judged one-sided and insufficiently informed by Wundt's own writings (Blumenthal 1979 is cited). Schwitzgebel's SEP entry "Introspection" (rev. 25 Apr 2024): Wundt rejected retrospective "inner perception" (it cannot discover new elements, and association contaminates memory) in favour of attentive, planful observation of processes as they occur, but allowed this only for the "simpler" processes (perception, memory, feeling, volition); language, myth, culture and development had to be studied by non-introspective methods (Völkerpsychologie, SEP Wundt §6). Training was the remedy for unreliability: Wundt reportedly refused to credit observers with fewer than 50,000 trials [~; SEP's word is "reportedly", after Boring 1953], and Titchener wrote a 1,600-page introspective training manual (1901–1905), arguing introspection is at least as hard as observation in the physical sciences. — plato.stanford.edu/entries/introspection/ (opened).
- ★[V] Why it stalled 为何内省走进死胡同: Schwitzgebel (SEP 2024): by the early twentieth century consensus on many questions was proving "elusive"; the most famous dispute concerned whether there is "imageless thought" 无意象思维 (thinking without sensory images; Humphrey 1951 and Kusch 1999 are the sources he cites); other stuck topics were the structure of emotion and what shifts of attention do to experience. Behaviourists portrayed introspective psychology as bogged down in irresolvable disputes between rival introspective "experts" (SEP's summary of the Watson 1913 critique). [~] The link between that dispute and the Würzburg school 维尔茨堡学派 (Külpe's group; SEP Popper calls Karl Bühler a founder member of it) is standard in histories, but I opened no source saying who reported what and when — give no names or dates beyond this. A modern echo, one lab only {C}: Hurlburt & Akhter 2008 (Consciousness and Cognition 17(4):1364–1374, doi:10.1016/j.concog.2008.03.021; abstract opened) argue that "unsymbolized thinking" (explicit thought with no words, images or other symbols) is one of five common features of sampled inner experience, alongside inner speech, inner seeing, feelings and sensory awareness; no independent replication found.

### B. Behaviourism 行为主义 and its critics
- ★[V] Watson 1913 华生宣言: "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It", Psychological Review 20(2):158–177, doi:10.1037/h0074428 (text opened, psychclassics.yorku.ca/Watson/views.htm). Opening claims: psychology so viewed is "a purely objective experimental branch of natural science"; "Its theoretical goal is the prediction and control of behavior"; "Introspection forms no essential part of its methods". Mandler 2002 (below) attributes behaviourism's later difficulty partly to Watson's insistence on a seamless continuity between human and nonhuman animal behaviour.
- [V] Skinner and the operant paradigm 斯金纳与操作性条件反射: SEP Behaviorism (George Graham, rev. 13 Jan 2023; opened): the standard illustration is a hungry rat in a chamber — if pressing a lever when a light is on is followed by food, pressing becomes more likely when the rat is hungry and the light is on (operant response, reinforcement 强化, discriminative stimulus); Skinner's work on schedules of reinforcement is called psychological behaviourism's fullest and most influential expression. Skinner's 1938 *The Behavior of Organisms* states the "law of conditioning" (an operant followed by a reinforcing stimulus increases in strength; p. 21, as cited in Chomsky's review) and *Verbal Behavior* (1957) extends the scheme to language. [~] The operant chamber 斯金纳箱 was built during Skinner's Harvard graduate years (Wikipedia pointer only) — give no year for it.
- ★[V] Chomsky 1959 乔姆斯基书评: review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, Language 35(1):26–58 (text opened as reprinted with a 1967 preface in Jakobovits & Miron (eds.), *Readings in the Psychology of Language*, Prentice-Hall: chomsky.info/1967____/). What it argued: (1) Skinner's laboratory terms — stimulus, response, reinforcement, control, strength — either keep their technical meaning, and then the account covers almost nothing of linguistic behaviour, or are used metaphorically, and then it is no more scientific than traditional descriptions; (2) as reinforcement is defined, the "law of conditioning" becomes a tautology; (3) readers and children constantly understand new sentences that are not similar "in a simple, physical sense" to any heard before, so appeals to stimulus generalisation are empty; (4) the speed with which all normal children acquire comparably complex grammars suggests a specially designed capacity, a "hypothesis-formulating" ability of unknown character. He also grants "certain nontrivial applications of operant conditioning to the control of human behavior" (e.g. verbal conditioning of plural nouns with "right"/"good"). In the 1967 preface he says the review was meant as a general critique of behaviourist (he would now say empiricist) speculation about higher mental processes, with Skinner's book as its most careful instance, and that he would change little of substance.
- ★[✗] "Chomsky's 1959 review killed behaviourism / started the cognitive revolution" is overstated. (i) Chronology: Miller's span paper (1956), Chomsky's own *Syntactic Structures* (1957, per SEP Attention) and Broadbent's *Perception and Communication* (1958) all precede it. (ii) Mandler 2002 (J Hist Behav Sci 38(4):339–353, doi:10.1002/jhbs.10066; abstract opened): the "revolution" was largely an evolving return to attitudes and trends that existed before behaviourism and survived outside the US; it was often slow and piecemeal, illustrated by four memory conferences held 1955–1966; the behaviourism of 1920–1950 was replaced because it could not address central issues in human psychology. (iii) SEP Behaviorism: Chomsky was "one of behaviorism's most successful and damaging critics", chiefly on language acquisition and the poverty of reinforcement histories; SEP Introspection: strict behaviourism declined in the 1960s–70s. Safe wording: "one influential blow in a gradual, multi-causal shift".
- ★[V] Köhler's apes 苛勒的黑猩猩 — correct the date: Ruiz & Sánchez 2014, The Spanish Journal of Psychology 17:E69, doi:10.1017/sjp.2014.70 (abstract opened): the Anthropoid Station of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences near La Orotava, Tenerife; first director Eugen Teuber; Köhler, "second and final director", arrived in late 1913; the experiments at the core of *Intelligenzprüfungen an Menschenaffen* (English: *The Mentality of Apes*) were done during his stay; the book is dated 1921. Virues-Ortega, Maier-Wolthausen & Taylor 2026, European Psychologist, doi:10.1027/1016-9040/a000582 (abstract opened): station directed by Köhler 1914–1920, closed 1920, when the six surviving chimpanzees (Sultan, Rana, Chica, Grande, Tercera, Tschego) went to the Berlin Zoological Gardens. Safe wording (my phrasing, not a quotation): Köhler worked on Tenerife during the First World War, from late 1913 until 1920; the German book appeared in 1921, the English translation in 1925. "1920s" is wrong for the experiments and right only for the book. [~] The English edition (translator Ella Winter, 1925) and an earlier 1917 report, *Intelligenzprüfungen an Anthropoiden*, come from the Wikipedia pointer; a "1913–1917" range for the experiments could not be confirmed (the station and Köhler's stay ran to 1920).
- [def] Falsifiability 可证伪性 (Popper 波普尔): SEP Popper (Stephen Thornton, rev. 31 Jul 2026; opened): Popper took the central problem of the philosophy of science to be demarcation — separating science from non-science — and contrasted Einstein's "risky" theory, whose deducible consequences could have refuted it, with Freud's and Adler's theories, which he judged couched in terms open only to confirmation. Use it as a definition and a teaching contrast, not as a verdict on any psychological theory.

### C. The cognitive turn 认知革命
- ★[V] Miller 1956 米勒"神奇的数字七": *The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on our Capacity for Processing Information* (title), Psychological Review 63(2):81–97, doi:10.1037/h0043158 (text opened, psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/). It opens "My problem is that I have been persecuted by an integer." Content: (a) absolute judgement of one stimulus dimension has a channel capacity of only a few bits — pitch 2.5 bits (about 6 tones), loudness 2.3 bits (about 5 levels), salt concentration 1.9 bits (about 4) — so the "span of absolute judgment" is about seven categories; (b) immediate-memory span is also about seven items for many materials, but Miller warns the recurrence of seven may be a coincidence: absolute judgement is limited by bits, immediate memory by chunks 组块 — decimal digits (3.3 bits each) give a span of about seven (≈ 23 bits), yet Hayes found a span of about nine binary digits and about five monosyllabic words, far from constant information; (c) recoding into larger chunks is how people stretch the limit. So "7 ± 2 things" is a simplification; later work puts the central capacity nearer four chunks (Cowan 2001, §06).
- [V] Broadbent 1958 and Neisser 1967 — the information-processing programme 信息加工: Broadbent, *Perception and Communication* (1958; doi:10.1037/10037-000, bibliographic data checked; publisher not checked). SEP Attention (Christopher Mole, rev. 2 Feb 2026; opened) calls it instrumental to the waning of behaviourism, the establishment of cognitive psychology and a new theory of attention: capacity limits borrowed from communication engineering (the telephone exchange as metaphor) and a single bottleneck between a large-capacity automatic first system and a small-capacity second system; Broadbent himself rarely used the word "attention" in the book. Neisser, *Cognitive Psychology* (1967; publisher not checked): per an APS Observer obituary (27 Apr 2012; opened) the "father of cognitive psychology" brought together perception, pattern recognition, attention, problem solving and remembering, did not explicitly attack behaviourism, and had studied with the Gestalt psychologists Köhler and Wallach at Swarthmore; he later grew disenchanted with reaction-time and information-processing studies and championed ecological research. [~] His definition of cognition (p. 4; sensory input "transformed, reduced, elaborated, stored, recovered, and used") I saw only on Wikipedia — do not quote it.
- [V] The 1950s in three sources: Miller 2003, "The cognitive revolution: a historical perspective", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 7(3):141–144, doi:10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00029-9 (abstract opened): cognitive science is "a child of the 1950s"; psychology could not take part until it had freed itself from behaviourism; several disciplines found their problems depended on one another, so collaboration was needed. SEP Cognitive Science (Paul Thagard, rev. 31 Jan 2023; opened): around 1956 Miller (capacity limits, chunking), McCarthy, Minsky, Newell and Simon (artificial intelligence) and Chomsky (rule-based mental grammars) can be viewed as the founders. SEP Computational Theory of Mind (Michael Rescorla, rev. 18 Dec 2024; opened): the Logic Theorist (Newell & Simon 1956) proved 38 of the first 52 theorems of *Principia Mathematica* — the computer became a model of thought.

### D. Chronometry 心理计时学 as a method
- ★[V] Donders 1868 唐德斯减法法: Posner 2005 (PLoS Biology 3(2):e51, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.0030051; opened): Donders subtracted the time for one response to an unvarying stimulus from the time for the same response to one of two events, giving the time to discriminate; and subtracted discrimination time from a situation with two possible responses, giving the time to choose; from the late 1800s until 1950, Posner says, the field was built almost entirely around one method, measuring and comparing reaction times. Original: *Die Schnelligkeit psychischer Processe* (1868), English "On the speed of mental processes" (trans. 1969), Acta Psychologica 30:412–431, doi:10.1016/0001-6918(69)90065-1 (bibliographic data checked; paywalled, so Donders's own times are not in this file). The weak point is pure insertion 纯插入: Sternberg 1998 (chapter in *Invitation to Cognitive Science* vol. 4, MIT Press, pp. 365–454; text opened at sas.upenn.edu/~saul/invitation.chap9.withintro.pdf) — the method assumes that adding one stage leaves the durations of the other operations unchanged, and later psychologists recognised that this needed justification.
- STUDY Memory scanning 记忆扫描 (Sternberg 1966) — original: Sternberg 1966, Science 153(3736):652–654, doi:10.1126/science.153.3736.652 (abstract opened): mean RT rises linearly with the length of the memorised list, implying an internal serial-comparison process "between 25 and 30 symbols per second". Numbers from Sternberg's 2016 review (QJEP 69(10):2020–2075, doi:10.1080/17470218.2016.1198820; text opened at sas.upenn.edu/~saul/defence.pdf, Table 1): Exp. 1 of 1966 (varied-set task, digits, 8 subjects, set sizes 1–6): zero intercept 397 ms, slope 37.9 ms/item (RT ≈ 397 + 37.9 × set size; ≈ 511 ms at three items); Exp. 2 (fixed-set, 6 subjects, sizes 1, 2, 4): 369 ms, 38.3 ms/item; the slopes for "yes" and "no" answers were about equal, the basis for the "exhaustive" interpretation. Later evidence: across the 12 experiments in that table (Sternberg's tasks and Monsell-type tasks from other labs) slopes run from 18.9 to 45.2 ms/item, so "about 38 ms/item" belongs to Sternberg's procedures; the 2016 abstract says critics claimed the serial exhaustive scanning process "never occurred" and that other mechanisms produce the RT patterns, while Sternberg argues the repeated claims of violated predictions are false for the original procedures. GRADE A for "mean RT grows roughly linearly with memory-set size in these procedures" (several labs; slopes vary by procedure); GRADE C for "the search is serial and exhaustive" (see model mimicry below). — doi:10.1126/science.153.3736.652; 10.1080/17470218.2016.1198820.
- [def] Additive-factors method 加性因素法: Sternberg 1969, "The discovery of processing stages: Extensions of Donders' method", Acta Psychologica 30:276–315, doi:10.1016/0001-6918(69)90055-9 (bibliographic data checked; the open PDF on his page is image-only, so I could not read it). Logic: if two experimental factors act on different processing stages, their effects on mean RT add; an interaction would mean they share a stage [standard logic; the interaction half was not read in an opened source]. Posner 2005 (above) gives the standard example, Dehaene's 1996 number-comparison task (is a digit above or below five?): notation (Arabic vs spelled out), numerical distance from five and responding hand had additive effects, read as separate encoding, comparison and response stages. Posner's book *Chronometric Explorations of Mind* (Erlbaum, 1978) is listed in Posner 2005's references; not opened.
- ★[V] Stroop 1935 斯特鲁普: "Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions", Journal of Experimental Psychology 18(6):643–662, doi:10.1037/h0054651 (text opened, psychclassics.yorku.ca/Stroop/). Timed with a stopwatch to a fifth of a second. Exp. 1 (70 undergraduates): reading 100 colour names printed in conflicting ink took on average 2.3 s (5.6%) longer than reading the same names in black — "not reliable". Exp. 2 (100 students): naming the ink colour of 100 colour-conflicting words took 110.3 s versus 63.3 s for 100 coloured squares: +47.0 s, +74% (means after the author's error correction; raw 108.7 vs 63.0 s; SDs 10.8 → 18.8). Exp. 3 (32 Arizona undergraduates): daily practice shortened times considerably (a gain of 16.8 s, 33.9% of the first day's mean). The classic effect is asymmetric: words interfere with naming colours, but colours barely interfere with reading words.
- STUDY Stroop interference — original: Stroop 1935 (Exp. 2, N = 100: colour naming +47.0 s, +74%; Exp. 1, N = 70: word reading +2.3 s, not reliable). Later evidence: MacLeod 1991, "Half a century of research on the Stroop effect: an integrative review", Psychological Bulletin 109(2):163–203, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.109.2.163 (title and bibliographic data checked; abstract not retrievable); Hedge, Powell & Sumner 2018 (Behavior Research Methods 50(3):1166–1186, doi:10.3758/s13428-017-0935-1; open text opened) state that in Many Labs 3 (Ebersole et al. 2016) the Stroop effect "was replicated in 100% of attempts", unlike most effects tested (their statement; ML3's full text could not be opened). The same paper documents the reliability paradox 信度悖论: the Stroop RT cost has test–retest ICC .60 (95% CI .31–.78; Study 1, N = 47) and .66 (.26–.83; Study 2, N = 56), SEM 21 and 24 ms, and no behavioural measure in Studies 1–2 exceeded .8; the condition RTs themselves reach .67–.77, so the difference score is less reliable than its components; correlations among "response-control" tasks are weak (Stroop vs flanker RT cost ρ = .14, N = 99–104, not significant). GRADE A for "naming the ink colour is slowed by a conflicting colour word" (group-level, robust across labs); GRADE B for using the Stroop RT cost as a stable individual-difference measure (reliability moderate at best). — doi:10.1037/h0054651; 10.3758/s13428-017-0935-1.
- [V] What RT patterns cannot show 反应时的推断限度: (i) model mimicry 模型模仿 — Townsend 1971, "A note on the identifiability of parallel and serial processes", Perception & Psychophysics 10(3):161–163, doi:10.3758/BF03205778 (open text opened): the typical Sternberg finding, mean latency linear in memory-set size, "has generally been interpreted as convincing evidence" of a serial process, but there is "no mathematically compelling reason" for that reading — a limited-capacity ("constant energy") parallel system predicts the same results as a serial exponential system with constant rate; telling them apart needs elements with different processing rates and more than one processing order, or materials and instructions that favour some orders. (ii) Reverse inference 反向推理 — Poldrack 2006, "Can cognitive processes be inferred from neuroimaging data?", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 10(2):59–63, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004 (abstract opened): inferring that a cognitive process was engaged from the activation of a brain region is "not deductively valid" but can still carry some information; a Bayesian analysis of the BrainMap database shows its usefulness is limited by how selective the region's activation is, so researchers should be circumspect when selectivity is weak or unknown.
- [V] Pointer — introspection is not worthless 内省并非一无是处: Nisbett & Wilson 1977, "Telling more than we can know: Verbal reports on mental processes", Psychological Review 84(3):231–259, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231 (bibliographic data checked) is covered in FACTS_T4b (when reports are accurate and when they are confabulated); for §01 say only that a verbal report is a datum about the report, not a direct readout of mental processes. SEP Cognitive Science (Thagard, opened) likewise says conclusions about how the mind works must rest on more than common sense and introspection, which can give a misleading picture of mental operations.

## 02 · the logic of evidence 证据的逻辑 (replication crisis 可重复性危机 · effect sizes 效应量 · inference traps · who was studied)

### A1. How the crisis started (2011): ESP, fraud, and a method scare
- [V] Bem 2011 "Feeling the future" 预知实验: 9 experiments, > 1,000 participants, mean effect size d = 0.22, and "all but one of the experiments yielded statistically significant results"; subgroup high on stimulus-seeking d = 0.43. Exp 1 (precognitive hits on erotic pictures): 100 Cornell undergraduates, 36 trials, 53.1% hits on erotic trials vs 50% chance (49.8% on non-erotic), p = .01 [~ Exp 1 figures second-hand via press/critiques]. — JPSP 100(3):407–425, doi:10.1037/a0021524 (abstract opened).
- [V] Wagenmakers, Wetzels, Borsboom & van der Maas 2011: the analysis was partly exploratory, one-sided p values overstate evidence; default Bayesian t tests show the evidence for psi is "weak to nonexistent". — JPSP 100:426–432, doi:10.1037/a0022790.
- STUDY Bem 2011 precognition — original: 9 experiments, N > 1,000, mean d = 0.22. Later evidence: Ritchie, Wiseman & French 2012 (PLoS ONE 7:e33423): three pre-registered exact replications of "retroactive facilitation of recall", combined n = 150, combined p = .83 (one-tailed); Galak, LeBoeuf, Nelson & Simmons 2012 (JPSP 103:933–948): 7 experiments, N = 3,289, meta-analytic d = 0.04 (≈ 0). Bem, Tressoldi, Rabeyron & Duggan 2016 (F1000Research 4:1188) pooled 90 experiments from 33 labs/14 countries: Hedges g = 0.09 (z = 6.40), g = 0.06 without Bem's own — contested (inclusion choices, no mechanism, the same flexibility the lesson is about). GRADE D: failed pre-registered replications; teach as the case that showed ordinary p < .05 methods could "confirm" an implausible claim. — doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0033423; doi:10.1037/a0029709.
- [V] Stapel fraud 造假: Levelt, Noort & Drenth Committees, final report "Flawed science" (28 Nov 2012): "a total of fifty-five publications in which fraud has been established" (Levelt Committee 34 publications + chapters in 3 dissertations; Noort Committee 21 publications + 7 dissertations, plus evidence of fraud in 3 more); co-authors and PhD students were not involved. Also "sloppy science" (verification bias, missing replications, statistical flaws) tolerated by reviewers/peers. Retraction Watch counted 57 retractions by Oct 2015. — gwern.net/doc/statistics/bias/2012-levelt.pdf (report text opened); retractionwatch.com/2015/10/20/diederik-stapel-retraction-count-updated-to-57 (title).
- [V] The report's forensic yardstick: meta-analyses put the mean two-group effect in psychology at d ≈ 0.5 (Meyer et al. 2001; Richard, Bond & Stokes-Zoota 2003). The committee's own audit of 158 between-subjects studies in EJSP 2011 and JPSP 2007–2011 found mean d = 0.69; 71% had d < 0.8; mean explained variance 22%, and < 35% in 80% of studies — so Stapel's 85–95% explained variance (and 90%+ after reliability correction, "regularly") was "extremely rare" and a red flag. Lesson: fabricated effects were too clean and too large; the field's real effects are small and noisy. — same report (text opened).

### A2. Open Science Collaboration 2015 (Reproducibility Project: Psychology)
- [V] Design: 100 replications of studies from the 2008 volumes of Psychological Science (PSCI), JPSP and JEP:LMC; high-power designs, original materials where available; funnel: 488 articles in the 2008 issues → 158 (32%) eligible during Nov 2011–Dec 2014 → 111 claimed by a team (113 replications) → 100 completed (PSCI 40, JPSP 32, JEP:LMC 28; coded cognitive n = 43 / social-personality n = 57). One effect per article (the final, "key" effect) was replicated — the sample is quasi-random from three journals, not a random sample of psychology. — Science 349:aac4716, doi:10.1126/science.aac4716 (authors' manuscript opened at pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/21474290/RPP.pdf).
- ★[V] Headline numbers: 97 of 100 originals "positive" (4 had p just above .05 — .0508/.0514/.0516/.0567 — but were counted as positive); **replications significant in the original direction: 35 of 97 = 36%** (about 89 expected had every original effect been true and exactly estimated; mean replication power .92). **Mean effect size r: original .403 (SD .188) → replication .197 (SD .257)**, Wilcoxon W = 7137, p < .001; original larger in 82 of 99 pairs. Original effect size inside the replication 95% CI: 47%; teams' subjective "did it replicate?": 39%; original + replication combined (meta-analytic) significant: 68%.
- [V] By subfield: cognitive 21 of 42 = 50% significant vs social-personality 14 of 55 = 25%; by journal×subfield: JPSP-social 7/31 = 23%, PSCI-social 7/24 = 29%, JEP:LMC-cognitive 13/27 = 48%, PSCI-cognitive 8/15 = 53%. Effect-size shrinkage was similar in all cells (χ²(3) = 2.45, p = .48). Authors' reading: weaker original effects in social psychology + more powerful within-subject designs in cognitive.
- [V] Predictors: replication success correlated with the original p value (ρ = −.33) and original effect size (ρ = +.30), "better predicted by the strength of original evidence" than by characteristics of the original or replication teams; "surprising" originals replicated less (ρ = −.24). Among significant originals: main/simple effects replicated 23 of 49 (47%) but interaction effects only 8 of 37 (22%); by original p value: p < .02 → 26 of 63 (41%) replicated, .02 < p < .04 → 6 of 23 (26%), p > .04 → 2 of 11 (18%). — same manuscript.
- [V] Critiques/reanalyses: Gilbert, King, Pettigrew & Wilson 2016 (Science 351:1037) argued that the OSC estimate contained three statistical errors and that the data are consistent with high reproducibility; the reply (Anderson et al. 2016) called that a "very optimistic assessment" resting on statistical misconceptions and said neither optimistic nor pessimistic conclusions are yet warranted. Patil, Peng & Leek 2016 (Perspect Psychol Sci 11:539–544): 77% of replication effect sizes fall inside the 95% prediction interval from the original — consistent with sampling error given imprecise originals. Etz & Vandekerckhove 2016 (PLoS ONE 11:e0149794; 72 studies, Bayes factors): 75% gave qualitatively similar evidence, but 64% gave no strong evidence either way in original or replication; failures are, they argue, adequately explained by overestimated effect sizes from small samples and publication bias. — doi:10.1126/science.aad7243; aad9163; 10.1177/1745691616646366; 10.1371/journal.pone.0149794.
- STUDY Psychology replication rate 心理学可重复率 — claim graded: published findings replicate at roughly 36–50% and shrink to about half. original: Open Science Collaboration 2015 (100 studies from the 2008 volumes; 36% significant, r .40 → .20; doi:10.1126/science.aac4716). Later evidence: Many Labs 2 (54% at p < .05, median d 0.60 → 0.15), Camerer 2016 (61%, effect 66% of original), Camerer 2018 (62%, ≈ 50% of original). GRADE A (for the general statement that a large share of classic findings fail to replicate at original size and replicated effects are typically about half the original); not a verdict on any single finding, and the exact percentage depends on the criterion (36%/47%/39%/68% in the same project).

### A3. Many Labs and other large replication efforts
- [V] Many Labs 1 多实验室一: Klein et al. 2014 — 13 effects, 36 samples/sites, N = 6,344 (25 US + 11 international samples; 27 lab + 9 online). "10 effects replicated consistently"; imagined contact "weak support" (significant in 4 of 36 samples); flag priming (Carter et al. 2011) and currency priming (Caruso et al. 2013) "did not replicate" (one p < .05 each; the flag-priming one in the wrong direction; aggregate ≈ 0). Variation across samples/labs was small compared with variation across effects: "replicability is more dependent on the effect itself than on the sample and setting". — Social Psychology 45(3):142–152, doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000178 (abstract + author manuscript, Table 2, opened).
- [V] ML1 effect sizes (Table 2, Cohen's d; original → replication median): explicit-anchor items (Jacowitz & Kahneman 1995) original d = 0.93 → babies born per day 2.43, height of Mt Everest 2.00, Chicago population 1.88, distance SF–NYC 1.18 (every one of 36 samples significant, N ≈ 5,300–5,600 per item); Asian-disease gain/loss framing original d = 1.13 → 0.58 (median; 86% of samples significant, N = 6,271); allowed/forbidden 0.65 → 1.88; retrospective gambler's fallacy 0.69 → 0.61; low-vs-high scales 0.50 → 0.50; implicit math-gender sex difference 1.01 → 0.59. So replication is not "failure only": some effects came back bigger.
- ★[V] Many Labs 2 多实验室二: Klein et al. 2018 — 28 findings, preregistered with peer-reviewed protocols, ≈ half of 125 samples per protocol, 15,305 participants, 36 countries/territories. Same direction and p < .05: 15 of 28 (54%); at p < .0001: 14 (50%); 7 (25%) larger than original, 21 (75%) smaller; **median d: original 0.60 → replication 0.15**; 16 (57%) replications d < 0.20; 9 (32%) opposite direction. Heterogeneity across samples: Q significant for 11 (39%), mostly the biggest effects; only 1 effect with τ > .20; 8 others τ ≈ .10; WEIRD vs less-WEIRD differences small — variability "attributable more to the effect being studied than to the sample or setting". — Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 1(4):443–490, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (abstract via Crossref).
- [~] Many Labs 3 Ebersole et al. 2016 (J Exp Soc Psychol 67:68–82): 20 university participant pools (N = 2,696) + online (N = 737), 10 effects; the point was time-of-semester (no real moderation; "weak" effects on data quality, conscientiousness, mood, stress). Only "a subset" of effects reproduced; per a secondary review of the pre-publication manuscript 7 of 10 did not (Stroop did; Tversky–Kahneman 1973 availability study came back at d = 0.09 vs original 0.82) — I could not open the final text, so quote only "a subset", not the 3/10 count. — doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2015.10.012 (abstract via Semantic Scholar).
- [V] Camerer et al. 2016: 18 lab experiments in economics (American Economic Review, QJE, 2011–2014), all with predefined plans and ≥ 90% power: 11 (61%) significant in the same direction; mean replicated effect size = 66% of original; other indicators 67–78%. — Science 351:1433–1436, doi:10.1126/science.aaf0918.

- [V] Registered Replication Reports (headline only): ego depletion — 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15] (Hagger et al. 2016, Perspect Psychol Sci 11:546–573). ### A4. How p < .05 gets manufactured: researcher degrees of freedom 研究者自由度, QRPs, file drawer 文件抽屉
- ★[V] Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011 "False-positive psychology": 15,000 simulated samples per row, baseline two-condition design with 20 observations per cell, true effect = 0. Probability that at least one analysis is significant at p < .05 (p < .10 / p < .01): A) two correlated (r = .50) dependent variables: **9.5%** (17.8% / 2.2%); B) add 10 more observations per cell if not significant: **7.7%** (14.5% / 1.6%); C) control for gender or gender×treatment: **11.7%** (21.6% / 2.7%); D) drop (or not) one of three conditions: **12.6%** (23.2% / 2.8%); A+B: 14.4%; A+B+C: 30.9%; **A+B+C+D: 60.7%** (81.5% at .10; 21.5% at .01). The paper rounds this to "a stunning 61%". — Psychological Science 22(11):1359–1366, doi:10.1177/0956797611417632 (full text opened).
- [V] The demonstration: Study 1 — 30 UPenn undergraduates heard "Hot Potato" (The Wiggles) or a control song ("Kalimba"); controlling for father's age, they "felt older" after the children's song (adjusted M = 2.54 vs 2.06 on a 5-point scale), F(1,27) = 5.06, p = .033. Study 2 — 20 undergraduates, "When I'm Sixty-Four" (The Beatles) vs "Kalimba", ANCOVA on birth date with father's age as covariate: people were "nearly a year-and-a-half younger" (adjusted M = 20.1 vs 21.5 years), F(1,17) = 4.92, p = .040 — a logically impossible result obtained with "legitimate statistical analyses" and reported truthfully (the third condition was dropped, covariates and stopping rule chosen after looking). Remedy: six author requirements, four reviewer guidelines (e.g. fix the stopping rule in advance, ≥ 20 observations per cell, report all variables and conditions). — same paper.
- [V] John, Loewenstein & Prelec 2012: e-mail survey of 5,964 academic psychologists at major US universities, 2,155 responded (36%); anonymous; control vs "Bayesian truth serum" (BTS, honesty-incentive) condition. Self-admission rates (control / BTS), "have you ever...": failing to report all dependent measures **63.4% / 66.5%**; deciding whether to collect more data after looking at significance **55.9% / 58.0%**; selectively reporting studies that "worked" **45.8% / 50.0%**; deciding to exclude data after seeing the effect on results **38.2% / 43.4%**; reporting an unexpected finding as predicted from the start (HARKing) **27.0% / 35.0%**; failing to report all conditions 27.7% / 27.4%; rounding off a p value 22.0% / 23.3%; stopping data collection early because the result appeared 15.6% / 22.5%; claiming results unaffected by demographics when unsure 3.0% / 4.5%; **falsifying data 0.6% / 1.7%**. From self-report + two impersonal estimates (prevalence among colleagues; admission rate among those who did it) the authors inferred, via geometric means in the BTS group, "actual estimates [that] approached 100%" for some practices and "nearly 1 in 10 research psychologists has introduced false data into the scientific record" — a model-based inference, NOT an observed rate (observed falsification self-admission 0.6% / 1.7%). — Psychological Science 23(5):524–532, doi:10.1177/0956797611430953 (full text opened at cmu.edu copy).
- [~] Critique: Fiedler & Schwarz 2016 (Soc Psychol Personal Sci 7:45–52) — "ever done it" ≠ "how often"; splitting prevalence into (proportion of scientists who ever did X) × (share of studies in which they did), their German survey gave estimates "lower by order of magnitudes"; inflated estimates can create a descriptive norm "QRP is normal". So: use the self-admission column ("ever, at least once"), not "X% of studies are p-hacked". — doi:10.1177/1948550615612150.
- [V] File drawer 文件抽屉: Franco, Malhotra & Simonovits 2014 — the whole population of 249 TESS survey experiments (2002–2012; analysed 221 with known outcome): strong results were 40 percentage points more likely to be published and 60 points more likely to be written up than null results; only 10 of 48 null results were published vs 56 of 91 strong results; 65% of null results were never even written up; once written up, publication odds no longer depended on result strength (the bias sits in the authors' desk). — Science 345:1502–1505, doi:10.1126/science.1255484 (paper text opened via gwern.net copy). Franco, Malhotra & Simonovits 2016 (SPPS 7(1):8–12, online 2015): ≈ 40% of psychology experiments in the same registry failed to report all conditions, ≈ 70% did not report all outcome variables; reported effect sizes ≈ 2× unreported ones and ≈ 3× as likely to be significant. — doi:10.1177/1948550615598377.
- [V] Rosenthal 1979 file-drawer problem: his abstract states the extreme view: journals filled with the 5% of studies that show Type I errors, file drawers with the 95% that show nonsignificant results (an extreme case, not a measurement); he proposed a "tolerance for null results" (later "fail-safe N") calculation, now regarded as a weak method. — Psychological Bulletin 86(3):638–641, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.86.3.638 (abstract text via forrt.org/curated_resources/the-file-drawer-problem-and-tolerance-fo/).
- [V] Ioannidis 2005 "Why most published research findings are false": positive predictive value PPV = (1−β)R / (R − βR + α) (before bias), where R = pre-study odds that a probed relationship is true. His Table 4 (α = .05): adequately powered RCT, R = 1:1, little bias → PPV 0.85; confirmatory meta-analysis of good RCTs 0.85; meta-analysis of small inconclusive studies 0.41; underpowered but well-performed phase I/II RCT (power .20, R = 1:5) 0.23; adequately powered exploratory epidemiology (R = 1:10) 0.20; underpowered exploratory epidemiology 0.12; discovery-oriented massive testing (power .20, R = 1:1,000, high bias) 0.0010. — PLoS Med 2(8):e124, doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.0020124 (Table 4 opened). {B}: the framework is logically sound; the claim "most" depends on assumed R and bias, which are field-specific.
- [derived] My arithmetic from Ioannidis's formula with no bias: if 1 in 10 tested hypotheses is true (R = 1:9) PPV = 64% at power 80%, 53% at power 50%, 31% at power 20% (CORRECTION, lead 2026-10-14: this line earlier read 61% / 50% / 29% — that is R = 1:10, i.e. 1 in 11 true; the lesson's 64 / 53 / 31 is right for 1 in 10); if 1 in 2 is true (R = 1:1) PPV = 94% / 91% / 80%.
- ★[derived] Bet: probability of at least one false positive in k independent tests at α = .05 is 1 − 0.95^k: k = 3 → 14.3%; 5 → 22.6%; 10 → 40.1%; 14 → 51.2% (the break-even point); **20 → 64.2%**; 30 → 78.5%; 60 → 95.4%; 100 → 99.4%. Bonferroni threshold for 20 tests = .05/20 = .0025 (Šidák .00256).

### A5. Statistical power 统计功效 and the winner's curse 赢家诅咒
- [def] Power = probability of detecting a true effect of a given size; conventional target 80% (Cohen). For a two-group t test with d = 0.5 you need ≈ 64 per group (derived: power .81); for d = 0.2 ≈ 394 per group (power .80); for d = 0.8 ≈ 26 per group.
- [V] Button et al. 2013 (Nat Rev Neurosci 14:365–376): median power of neuroscience studies "between ~8% and ~31%" (Key Points); the overall median is 21% across 49 meta-analyses/730 studies (as restated in Nord et al. 2017, J Neurosci 37:8051–61, which also shows power is very heterogeneous — extremely low in meta-analyses with null results and in candidate-gene studies; "low power is far from a universal problem"). — doi:10.1038/nrn3475; 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3592-16.2017.
- [V] Szucs & Ioannidis 2017: 26,841 statistical records from 3,801 cognitive-neuroscience and psychology papers; median effect size D = 0.93 (IQR 0.64–1.46) among nominally significant results vs D = 0.24 (0.11–0.42) among non-significant; median power to detect small/medium/large effects (d = 0.2/0.5/0.8) = **0.12 / 0.46 / 0.78** (CORRECTED by the authors' 2021 *PLoS Biol* correction, PMC7935231, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3001151: a (df+2)/2 vs df+2 slip in the t-test power formula; the 2017 paper originally printed 0.12 / 0.44 / 0.73 — do not quote those), "no improvement through the past half-century"; corrected means 0.18 / 0.52 / 0.76; corrected false report probability: best case (odds 1:1, no bias) 13.0%, 10% bias 22%; for odds 1–5 with 10–30% bias FRP 22–70% (median 50%); for more exploratory research 59–90% (median 75%); false report probability "likely to exceed 50%" under realistic priors. — PLoS Biol 15(3):e2000797, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.2000797.
- [V] Maxwell, Lau & Howard 2015: a single non-significant replication is weak evidence of "no effect" because single replications are often underpowered; multiple replications/meta-analysis are needed. — American Psychologist 70(6):487–498, doi:10.1037/a0039400.
- ★[V] Winner's curse: when only significant results are published and studies are underpowered, published effects are necessarily inflated (Ioannidis 2008, Epidemiology 19:640–648). Gelman & Carlin 2014 formalised "Type S" (wrong sign) and "Type M" (exaggeration ratio) errors; their worked example (hypothetical true effect 2 percentage points, SE 8.1): power 0.06, 24% chance a significant estimate has the wrong sign, expected exaggeration factor 9.7. — Perspect Psychol Sci 9:641–651, doi:10.1177/1745691614551642; doi:10.1097/EDE.0b013e31818131e7.
- [derived] Normal-approximation simulation by me (two-group design, SE(d) ≈ √(2/n)): true d = 0.3, n = 20/group → power 16%; the average significant estimate is d ≈ 0.78 (2.6× too big). True d = 0.5, n = 20 → power 35%, average significant d ≈ 0.83 (1.7×). True d = 0.5, n = 64 → power 81%, average significant d ≈ 0.56 (1.1×). True d = 0.2, n = 100 → power 29%, average significant d ≈ 0.37 (1.8×). (Tell writers these are my arithmetic, not from a paper.)
- [def] Winner's-curse in plain words: every study estimates the truth with noise; if the journal only prints the lucky draws that cleared p < .05, the printed average is pulled upward — a replication, which is not filtered, regresses back down.


### B. Effect sizes you can SEE 效应量
- [def] Cohen's d = (M₁ − M₂) / pooled SD (the mean difference in standard-deviation units). r is the correlation; for two equal-sized groups r = d/√(d²+4) and d = 2r/√(1−r²) [derived: r .10 ↔ d .20; r .21 ↔ d .43; r .30 ↔ d .63; r .50 ↔ d 1.15; d .5 ↔ r .24; d .8 ↔ r .37].
- [~] Cohen's (1988) conventions: small / medium / large = d .2 / .5 / .8 (r .1 / .3 / .5); Cohen himself stressed that small, medium and large are relative to each other and to the area of behavioural science and the specific content and research method (Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences, 2nd ed., p. 25 — paraphrased from secondary sources; book not opened).
- ★[V] What effects are actually like: Richard, Bond & Stokes-Zoota 2003 compiled > 25,000 studies of 8 million people (322 meta-analyses): the typical social-psychological effect is **r = .21**, with a standard deviation of r across studies of .15. — Review of General Psychology 7:331–363, doi:10.1037/1089-2680.7.4.331.
- [~] Gignac & Szodorai 2016 (708 meta-analytic correlations in individual-differences research): 25th / 50th / 75th percentiles r = .11 / .19 / .29, so they propose r = .10 / .20 / .30 as "relatively small / typical / relatively large"; fewer than 3% of correlations reach .50, so Cohen's "large" is rare. — Personality and Individual Differences 102:74–78, doi:10.1016/j.paid.2016.06.069 (numbers via secondary summaries; abstract not opened).
- [V] Funder & Ozer 2019: judge an effect against concrete benchmarks and consequences, not labels; when reliably estimated, r = .05 is very small for single events but can matter over time, .10 small, .20 medium and "of some explanatory and practical use even in the short run", .30 large; a very large effect (r = .40 or greater) in psychological research is "likely to be a gross overestimate" that will rarely be found in a large sample or in a replication. Don't square r to "explain" it away. — AMPPS 2(2):156–168, doi:10.1177/2515245919847202.
- [~] A small r can be important: Physicians' Health Study (22,071 US male physicians; low-dose aspirin vs placebo, mean 60.2 months): myocardial infarction risk −44% (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.45–0.70; 254.8 vs 439.7 per 100,000 per year) — yet Rosnow & Rosenthal 1989 computed this as r ≈ .034 (r² ≈ .001). — NEJM 321:129–135, doi:10.1056/NEJM198907203210301 (abstract opened); r = .034 via a secondary summary of Rosnow & Rosenthal, Am Psychol 44:1276–84.
- [V] Published effects are inflated: Schäfer & Schwarz 2019 compared 900 randomly drawn effects from non-preregistered publications (median **r = .36**) with 93 effects from preregistered publications (median **r = .16**) — ≈ 2× inflation, and sub-discipline differences too large for any global benchmark. — Frontiers in Psychology 10:813, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00813.
- ★[derived] **Conversions for equal-variance normal distributions** (formulas: U3 = Φ(d); overlap OVL = 2Φ(−d/2); common-language effect size / probability of superiority CL = Φ(d/√2), McGraw & Wong 1992, Psychol Bull 111:361–365, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.111.2.361; U1–U3 from Cohen 1988). Computed by me, rounded:
  | d | % of treated group above the control mean (U3) | probability a random treated person beats a random control (CL) | overlap of the two distributions (OVL) | r |
  | 0.2 | 57.9% | 55.6% | 92.0% | .10 |
  | 0.5 | 69.1% | 63.8% | 80.3% | .24 |
  | 0.8 | 78.8% | 71.4% | 68.9% | .37 |
  | 1.0 | 84.1% | 76.0% | 61.7% | .45 |
  | 1.5 | 93.3% | 85.6% | 45.3% | .60 |
  (Cohen's non-overlap U1 for d = .2/.5/.8/1.0 is 14.7–14.8% / 33.0% / 47.4% / 55.4%.) Reading: at the typical d = 0.43 (r = .21) a random treated person beats a random control 62% of the time (derived). Assumes normality and equal SDs — real distributions differ.
- [def] What a p value is and is not 什么是 p 值 (ASA statement, Wasserstein & Lazar 2016, The American Statistician 70:129–133, doi:10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108; six principles as released by the ASA on 7 Mar 2016, amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf): informally, the probability — computed assuming a specified statistical model — of obtaining a data summary (such as a difference between two group means) at least as extreme as the one observed. The six principles, paraphrased except where quoted: (1) p-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model (verbatim: "P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model."); (2) they do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or that the data were produced by chance alone; (3) conclusions and policy decisions should not rest only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold; (4) "Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency."; (5) a p-value or statistical significance does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result; (6) by itself a p-value is not a good measure of evidence about a model or hypothesis. (The informal definition is my paraphrase of the statement text as repeated in secondary sources; the six principles were read in the ASA release.)
- [def] Distribution of p values (p-curve): if the true effect is zero, p is uniform on 0–1, so significant p's are spread evenly between 0 and .05; if the effect is real, p is right-skewed (many very small p's). — Simonsohn, Nelson & Simmons 2014, J Exp Psychol Gen 143:534–547, doi:10.1037/a0033242. [derived] Normal approximation: with no effect 20% of significant p's lie below .01 and 20% between .04 and .05; with a true d = 0.5 at 80% power (64 per group) ≈ 74% are below .01 and only ≈ 3% between .04 and .05; at 35% power (n = 20) ≈ 45% and ≈ 10%. A pile of p's just under .05 is a fingerprint of selective reporting.
- [def] Regression to the mean 均值回归: when two measures are imperfectly correlated, units selected for extreme scores on the first are on average less extreme on the second, with no cause needed; with standardised scores E[z₂ | z₁] = r · z₁. Barnett et al. 2005: RTM "can make natural variation in repeated data look like real change", larger with more measurement error and when follow-up is restricted to a sub-sample selected on baseline. — Int J Epidemiol 34:215–220, doi:10.1093/ije/dyh299.
- [V] Galton 1886 "Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature" (J Anthropological Institute 15:246–263): the mean filial regression towards mediocrity was proportional to the parental deviation; for human stature he gives the ratio "from 1 to ⅔" (children of very tall parents average about two-thirds as far above the mean as their parents). He first saw it in sweet-pea seeds. — galton.org/essays/1880-1889/galton-1886-jaigi-regression-stature.pdf (OCR text opened).
- [V] Kahneman's flight-instructor story: Tversky & Kahneman 1974 (Science 185:1124–1131): experienced flight instructors observed that praise for a very smooth landing was typically followed by a poorer one next time, and harsh criticism after a rough landing by an improvement, and concluded that reward hurts and punishment helps — a conclusion the authors call "unwarranted because of the presence of regression toward the mean" (wording checked in the paper text). It is an anecdote used to teach, not a measured data set.
- [derived] Worked example: test–retest r = .5; pick the top 5% on test 1 (mean z ≈ +2.06); with no intervention their expected retest score is +1.03 SD; at r = .3 → +0.62; r = .7 → +1.44. Real consequences: "punishment works / praise backfires", "the jinx", treatments tested on the worst-off.

### C. Inference traps
- [V] Observational vs randomised, hormone therapy: 16 prospective studies etc. pooled by Stampfer & Colditz 1991 gave relative risk of coronary heart disease 0.56 (95% CI 0.50–0.61) in users of postmenopausal estrogen (0.50, 0.43–0.56 in the best prospective/angiographic studies); they judged the benefit "unlikely to be explained by confounding". The Women's Health Initiative randomised trial (16,608 women aged 50–79, estrogen+progestin 8,506 vs placebo 8,102, stopped after mean 5.2 years) found hazard ratios: coronary heart disease **1.29 (1.02–1.63)**, breast cancer 1.26 (1.00–1.59), stroke 1.41 (1.07–1.85). — Prev Med 20:47–63 (doi:10.1016/0091-7435(91)90006-P); JAMA 288:321–333 (doi:10.1001/jama.288.3.321).
- [V] Nuance — do not tell this as pure "confounding": Hernán et al. 2008 re-analysed the Nurses' Health Study as an emulated trial and found intention-to-treat HRs of 1.42 (0.92–2.20) in the first 2 years, 0.96 overall, and 0.84 in women within 10 years of menopause vs 1.12 in others, concluding the NHS–WHI discrepancy "could be largely explained" by differences in the distribution of time since menopause and length of follow-up. — Epidemiology 19:766–779, doi:10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181875e61.
- [~] A spurious ecological correlation: Messerli 2012 (NEJM 367:1562–64) correlated national chocolate consumption with Nobel laureates per 10 million people, r = 0.791 (p < 0.0001) in 23 countries (0.862 without Sweden); he cautioned it shows no causation (numbers via secondary reports; letter not opened).
- [def] Simpson's paradox 辛普森悖论 (Simpson 1951; named by Blyth 1972, JASA 67:364–366): an association present in a pooled table can shrink, vanish or reverse within every subgroup, because group sizes/mix differ.
- ★[V] Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell 1975, Berkeley fall 1973 graduate applications: pooled, 8,442 men applied and 44% were admitted vs 4,321 women and 35% (men 3,738 admitted/4,704 denied; women 1,494/2,827; χ² = 111.25, 1 df) — a "clear but misleading pattern of bias against female applicants". Department by department "few decision-making units ... show statistically significant departures", about as many favour women as men, and properly pooled there is "a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women"; the aggregate gap arose because women applied more to departments that are hard for everyone to enter. — Science 187:398–404, doi:10.1126/science.187.4175.398 (abstract opened); campus totals from Selvitella 2017 (J Stat Distrib Appl 4:2, doi:10.1186/s40488-017-0056-5) and Kazdan (Penn) — secondary.
- [V] Six largest departments (R `UCBAdmissions`, from the same data; totals 2,691 men / 1,198 admitted = 44.5% and 1,835 women / 557 admitted = 30.4%): men's vs women's admission rate (applicants): A 62% (825) vs 82% (108); B 63% (560) vs 68% (25); C 37% (325) vs 34% (593); D 33% (417) vs 35% (375); E 28% (191) vs 24% (393); F 6% (373) vs 7% (341). Women were 5.9% of the six-department applicants to A (an easy one, 64% overall admission) but 32% to C and 21% to E (35% and 25% overall). [derived] If women's department rates are weighted by men's application mix, women's expected admission rate is 51.6% vs men's 44.5%. (Note: Bickel's paper is about whether departments discriminated; why women applied to harder departments is a separate causal question the paradox itself cannot answer.) — stat.ethz.ch/R-manual `UCBAdmissions` doc (totals); percentages from Kazdan page and checked against counts.
- ★[V] Base rates 基率 and Bayes: Eddy 1982 mammography problem (as adapted by Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995): prevalence 1%, hit rate 80%, false-alarm rate 9.6% → P(cancer | positive) = .01×.80 / (.01×.80 + .99×.096) = **7.8%**; Eddy reported that **95 of 100 physicians estimated 70–80%**. In natural frequencies: of 1,000 women 10 have cancer, 8 of those test positive, 95 of 990 without cancer also test positive, so 8 of 103 positives (≈ 8%) are real. — Psychological Review 102:684–704, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684 (full text opened at pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/GG_How_1995.pdf).
- [V] Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 Study 1: 15 problems, probability vs frequency formats, standard vs short menu; Bayesian reasoning (strict joint criterion of process and outcome) in **46% (standard menu) and 50% (short menu) with natural frequencies vs 16% and 28% with probabilities**; identifiable algorithms 84% overall. Physicians: Hoffrage & Gigerenzer 1998 — 48 physicians in Munich/Düsseldorf, 4 diagnostic tests: correct positive predictive value in **10%** of cases with probabilities vs **46%** with natural frequencies (Academic Medicine 73:538–540, doi:10.1097/00001888-199805000-00024). Gigerenzer (APS Observer, Nov 2007): the most common answer of 160 gynecologists to a mammography PPV question (true ≈ 10%) was 90%; after a one-hour lecture to 150 gynecologists 87% understood vs 21% beforehand [~ author's own account]. Same article: mammography screening "reduces the risk of dying of breast cancer by 25%" = out of 1,000 women, 4 die without screening vs 3 with it, an absolute reduction of 1 in 1,000 (0.1%). — psychologicalscience.org/observer/helping-physicians-understand-screening-tests-will-improve-health-care; Gigerenzer et al. 2007, PSPI 8:53–96, doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2008.00033.x (abstract opened).
- STUDY Natural frequencies help Bayes — original: Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 (16% → 46% standard menu; students), Hoffrage & Gigerenzer 1998 (10% → 46%, 48 physicians). Later evidence: McDowell & Jacobs 2017 meta-analysis (35 articles, 226 performance estimates, Psychol Bull 143:1273–1312): the natural-frequency facilitation effect is real, but short-menu formats and visual aids also help conditional-probability formats ("some of the strongest moderation effects"), and methodological factors (e.g. exposure to both formats) matter. GRADE B: direction replicated across many labs; the size depends on format details, and the "mind is Bayesian once given frequencies" claim is contested (nested-sets accounts). — doi:10.1037/bul0000126.

### D. Who was studied: WEIRD samples
- ★[V] Arnett 2008: in six premier APA journals (2003–2007) US samples were ~68% of participants and 96% were from "Western industrialized" countries; Americans are < 5% of the world's population. Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010 build on this: 96% of participants came from countries holding 12% of the world's population; 73% of first authors at US universities, 99% at Western ones; in JPSP (premier social-psychology journal) 67% of American samples and 80% of other-country samples were psychology undergraduates, so a randomly selected American undergraduate is "more than 4,000 times more likely to be a research participant" than a randomly selected person from outside the West. — Am Psychol 63:602–614 (doi:10.1037/0003-066X.63.7.602); BBS 33:61–83 (doi:10.1017/S0140525X0999152X; text opened at www2.psych.ubc.ca/~henrich/pdfs/WeirdPeople.pdf). (The 96%/68% figures are Arnett's; Henrich et al. re-use them.)
- [V] Henrich et al. examples of WEIRD people as outliers: Müller–Lyer illusion (Segall, Campbell & Herskovits 1966, 16 societies): American undergraduates needed line "a" about a fifth (≈ 20%) longer than "b" to see them as equal, the San foragers of the Kalahari showed no illusion (PSE not distinguishable from zero); also fairness/ultimatum-game offers, spatial reasoning, categorisation, moral reasoning. {B}: variation across populations is documented for several tasks; the "carpentered-world" explanation for Müller–Lyer is a hypothesis.
- [V] Update: Thalmayer, Toscanelli & Arnett 2021 re-audited the same six APA journals for 2014–2018 (3,447 articles): first authors 64% US-based (73% in 2004–2008); samples **62% US** (68% in 2003–2007), 14% other English-speaking, 17% Europe (13% before), 4% Asia; Africa/Middle East/Latin America/Israel ≤ 1%; "11% of the world's population is now represented ... 89% ... neglected"; JPSP undergraduate share of samples fell to 42% (from 67%/80%). — Am Psychol 76:116–129, doi:10.1037/amp0000622 (author manuscript opened).
- [V] Rad, Martingano & Ginges 2018 (PNAS 115:11401–05): almost all research in Psychological Science relies on Western samples and uses the data "in an unreflective way"; ≈ 74% of 2017 samples were from Western countries (US 51.1% of 94 samples; 23.4% unknown origin). — doi:10.1073/pnas.1721165115 (abstract opened; sample breakdown via secondary summaries).
- [V] Latest I found (as of Oct 2026): Petrutiu, Birney, Cooke & Stewart 2026 (Int J Psychol; five British Psychological Society journals, Jan 2021–Dec 2023; 661 articles, 1,293 samples): Latin America, Eastern Europe, Middle East and Africa together made up 8.7% of samples; reporting of gender/race/SES/education was inconsistent. — eprints.staffs.ac.uk/9611 (abstract opened).
- [V] Counter-evidence for balance — ML2 tested whether effects differ across 125 samples in 36 countries: heterogeneity was small and "WEIRD vs less-WEIRD" comparisons showed little difference; "variability ... attributable more to the effect being studied than to the sample or setting". So WEIRD sampling matters a lot for WHICH effects (visual illusions, fairness norms, analytic vs holistic reasoning, IQ heritability) and little for many robust cognitive/judgment effects. — Klein et al. 2018, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225.
- STUDY WEIRD sample dominance — original: Arnett 2008/Henrich 2010 (96% Western, 68% US, 12% of humanity). Later evidence: Thalmayer 2021 (62% US samples 2014–2018), Rad 2018 (≈ 74% Western, 2017), Petrutiu 2026 (8.7% from four regions). Whether WEIRD samples mislead: ML2 found little cross-sample heterogeneity for 28 effects. GRADE A for the descriptive claim "psychology's participants are overwhelmingly WEIRD, only slowly diversifying"; B for "WEIRD people are frequent outliers on tasks" (task-dependent).

### E. Habits that help (meta-science 元科学)
- [V] Preregistration 预注册: Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor 2018 — defining questions and analysis plan before seeing outcomes separates prediction from postdiction and counters hindsight bias. — PNAS 115:2600–2606, doi:10.1073/pnas.1708274114.
- [V] Registered Reports' quality: Soderberg et al. 2021 — 353 researchers reviewed 29 RRs and 57 matched comparison papers: RRs scored higher on all 19 criteria (mean difference 0.46 on a −4 to +4 scale), with sizeable gains in rigour of methodology (0.99, CI 0.62–1.35), analysis (0.97) and overall quality (0.66), and no loss in novelty (0.13, CI −0.24–0.49) or creativity (0.22). — Nat Hum Behav 5:990–997, doi:10.1038/s41562-021-01142-4. {B}: blind-ish reviewer ratings, small sample of RRs.
- [V] Multi-lab replication and adversarial collaboration 对抗性合作: Mellers, Hertwig & Kahneman 2001 (Psychol Sci 12:269–275) — Hertwig and Kahneman agreed on tests with Mellers as arbiter for the Linda conjunction effect: frequency formats alone did not eliminate conjunction effects; with filler items removed they disappeared with Hertwig's "who are" phrasing (they still disagreed on interpretation). Pointer: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2023 (PNAS 120:e2208661120, doi:10.1073/pnas.2208661120): adversarial collaboration resolved the income–happiness conflict — happiness rises with log income for most people, the "plateau" appears only for the least happy group; details in the well-being lesson.
- [V] Cumulative picture of residual bias: Stanley, Carter & Doucouliagos 2018 (200 meta-analyses, 12,065 effect sizes, ≈ 8,000 papers): median-of-median power ≈ 36%, ≈ 8% of studies adequately powered; median heterogeneity I² = 74%; "only a small amount of average residual reporting bias" — i.e. low power + heterogeneity alone can explain much of the replication difficulty. — Psychol Bull 144:1325–1346, doi:10.1037/bul0000169.
- [V] The false-discovery-rate counter-view: Jager & Leek 2014 estimated that 14% (SD 1%) of reported p-values in 5,322 abstracts from 77,430 papers in five top medical journals (2000–2010) are false discoveries — "contrary to previous claims" of Ioannidis 2005 for medicine (the method itself is contested). — Biostatistics 15:1–12, doi:10.1093/biostatistics/kxt007.
- [V] Fraud vs QRP base rates: Fanelli 2009 meta-analysis of 21 surveys: pooled 1.97% (95% CI 0.86–4.45) of scientists admit to having fabricated, falsified or modified data at least once and up to 33.7% admit other QRPs; 14.12% report colleagues' falsification and up to 72% colleagues' other QRPs. — PLoS ONE 4:e5738, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0005738.

### F. Graded studies for lesson 02 (badge source)
- STUDY Researcher degrees of freedom — original: Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011, 15,000 simulated samples per scenario, n = 20/cell, true effect 0: four ordinary flexibilities together give 60.7% "significant" at p < .05 (single ones 7.7–12.6%); two experiments "showed" that a Beatles song made people 1.5 years younger. Later evidence: John et al. 2012 (QRP admissions up to 63.4%), Franco et al. 2016 (≈ 40% of studies omit conditions, ≈ 70% omit outcomes), Schäfer & Schwarz 2019 (non-preregistered median r .36 vs .16), Scheel 2021 (96% vs 44% positive). GRADE A: the inflation is arithmetic given the stated assumptions; empirically, such flexibility is common — the exact rate in any given paper is unknowable.
- STUDY QRP admission survey 可疑研究实践 — original: John, Loewenstein & Prelec 2012, 2,155 psychologists (36% of 5,964): 63.4% ever failed to report all dependent measures, 45.8% selectively reported studies that "worked", 27.0% reported a surprise as predicted, 0.6–1.7% admitted falsifying data. Later evidence: Fanelli 2009 meta-analysis (33.7% admit QRPs; 1.97% falsification); Fiedler & Schwarz 2016 (frequency-based estimates "lower by orders of magnitude"). GRADE B: QRPs are common across surveys (direction robust), but "% who ever did X" is not "% of studies with X" and wording changes the number.
- STUDY File drawer in social science 文件抽屉 — original: Franco, Malhotra & Simonovits 2014, 221 TESS survey experiments (population of studies with known fate): strong results 40 pp more likely published, 60 pp more likely written up; 10/48 nulls vs 56/91 strong results published; 65% of nulls never written. Later evidence: Franco et al. 2016 (underreporting inside studies), Scheel et al. 2021 (96% vs 44%), Allen & Mehler 2019 (nulls "substantially more likely" to be published as registered reports). GRADE A: direction replicated by independent designs; the size is specific to TESS (survey experiments), not a universal constant.
- STUDY Why most findings are false 阳性预测值 — original: Ioannidis 2005, a model (no data): PPV depends on power, pre-study odds R, bias; Table 4 gives 0.85 (well-powered RCT) down to 0.001 (massive-testing discovery research). Later evidence: Jager & Leek 2014 (14% false discoveries in top medical abstracts), Szucs & Ioannidis 2017 (false report probability > 50% in cognitive neuroscience/psychology under assumed priors), OSC 2015 (36%). GRADE B: the logic is exact, but "most" depends on assumed priors and bias, which differ by field; psychology's replication data are consistent with it, top medical journals less so.
- STUDY Typical power is low — original: Szucs & Ioannidis 2017, 26,841 records from 3,801 papers: median power 0.12 / 0.46 / 0.78 for small/medium/large true effects (as corrected in 2021; originally printed 0.12 / 0.44 / 0.73); significant results' median D = 0.93 vs non-significant 0.24. Later evidence: Stanley et al. 2018 (200 meta-analyses: median power ≈ 36%, ≈ 8% of studies ≥ 80%), Button et al. 2013 (neuroscience median 21%), Cohen 1962-style audits. GRADE A for "typical power is well below the 80% convention"; the exact number depends on the assumed true effect size.
- STUDY Power failure in neuroscience — original: Button et al. 2013, 49 meta-analyses/730 studies, median power ≈ 21% (range of subfield medians ~8–31%). Later evidence: Nord et al. 2017 (same data re-modelled: power is bimodal/heterogeneous — very low in null-result meta-analyses and candidate-gene studies, "acceptable or even exemplary" in many others); Szucs & Ioannidis 2017 (power lower in cognitive neuroscience than psychology). GRADE B: low power is real and common but not uniform; true power also depends on meta-analytic effects that are themselves inflated.
- STUDY Many Labs 1 (13 effects) — original: Klein et al. 2014, 36 samples, N = 6,344: 10 of 13 replicated, imagined contact weak, flag and currency priming null; explicit-anchor effects came back 1.3–2.6× larger than the original d 0.93 (d 1.18–2.43); Asian-disease framing d 1.13 → 0.58. Later evidence: ML2 (28 effects, 125 samples, N = 15,305) and ML3 reached the same conclusion about sites. GRADE A for "differences between effects dominate differences between labs/samples" (three Many Labs projects agree); the "10 of 13" rate is not a base rate (hand-picked, short online-friendly effects).
- STUDY Many Labs 2 (28 effects) — original: Klein et al. 2018: 54% significant in the original direction (50% at p < .0001), median d 0.60 → 0.15, 32% reversed in sign, tiny between-sample heterogeneity (only 1 effect with τ > .20). Later evidence: consistent with OSC 2015 (36–50%), Camerer 2016/2018 (61–62%) — different effect sets, same ballpark. GRADE A: large, preregistered, peer-reviewed-protocol, multi-country; applies to the 28 selected effects, not "psychology as a whole".
- STUDY Registered Reports vs standard papers — original: Scheel, Schijen & Lakens 2021: 71 RRs vs 152 standard papers (first hypothesis): 44% vs 96% positive. Later evidence: Allen & Mehler 2019 (exploratory: nulls much likelier to appear as RRs); Soderberg et al. 2021 (RRs rated higher on 19/19 criteria). GRADE B: large, consistent difference, but RR authors and hypotheses are self-selected, so not all of the 52-point gap is bias removal.
- STUDY Ego-depletion RRR — claim graded: the original sequential-task "willpower is a depletable resource" effect exists as a medium-sized effect. original: Baumeister et al. 1998 (medium effect; a 2010 meta-analysis d ≈ 0.6). Later evidence: Hagger et al. 2016 RRR — 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]; Vohs et al. 2021 — 36 labs, N = 3,531, preregistered d = 0.06 (n.s.; Bayes factor ≈ 4:1 for the null vs an informed alternative), exploratory full-sample d = 0.08. GRADE D: two large preregistered multi-lab tests found effects near zero; teach as a caution (the broader idea of self-control fatigue is separate). — doi:10.1177/1745691616652873; doi:10.1177/0956797621989733.
- STUDY Pen-in-mouth funniness effect — claim graded: holding a pen in the teeth (smile) makes cartoons funnier than holding it in the lips. original: Strack, Martin & Stepper 1988, difference 0.82 rating units (10-pt scale). Later evidence: Wagenmakers et al. 2016 RRR — 17 labs, 0.03 units, CI [−0.11, 0.16]; Many Smiles (Coles et al. 2022, N = 3,878, 19 countries): pen-in-mouth "less conclusive". GRADE D for the pen-in-mouth effect. (Separate claim — "facial expressions can modulate emotion": Coles et al. 2019 meta-analysis of 286 effects/138 studies: "significant but small" and variable; Many Smiles: mimicry and voluntary facial-action tasks amplified/initiated happiness. - STUDY Replication audits in economics and Nature/Science — original: Camerer et al. 2016 (18 economics lab experiments; 61% replicated; replicated effect = 66% of original) and 2018 (21 Nature/Science social-science experiments; 62%; effect ≈ 50% of original; replications ≈ 5× larger samples; prediction markets did well). ## 06 · judgment 判断 — the price of approximating 近似的代价 (bounded rationality 有限理性 · what replicates · why "good enough" can be optimal)

Reading order: A the programme and the 1974 paper; B each classic demonstration graded by its CURRENT evidence (including the ADDED hot-hand, loss-aversion and dual-process items); C the derivation that makes "good enough" an optimum (bias–variance, take-the-best, 1/N, resource rationality) and its limits; D debiasing pointer; widget constants and bets last. Net message: several named biases are large, replicated effects (explicit anchors, framing, the conjunction effect under its original wording, ease of retrieval within limits); several famous "fast thinking does X" demonstrations did not survive (incidental anchors, behavioural priming); and approximating is not a defect in itself — whether it costs accuracy depends on the environment and on how much data the decision-maker has.

### A. Bounded rationality 有限理性 and the 1974 paper
- [V] Simon 1955, "A behavioral model of rational choice", Quarterly Journal of Economics 69(1):99–118, doi:10.2307/1884852, and Simon 1956, "Rational choice and the structure of the environment", Psychological Review 63(2):129–138, doi:10.1037/h0042769: the founding papers of bounded rationality (Crossref bibliographic data checked; I did not open either text). [def] Satisficing 满意化: go through the alternatives one at a time and stop at the first one that meets an aspiration level 期望水平, instead of maximising over all of them; Artinger, Gigerenzer & Jacobs date the notion to Simon 1955. — Todd & Gigerenzer 2000, BBS 23(5):727–741, doi:10.1017/S0140525X00003447 (précis opened); Artinger, Gigerenzer & Jacobs 2022, J Econ Lit 60(2):598–635, doi:10.1257/jel.20201396 (abstract opened).
- ★[V] Modern verdict on satisficing (Artinger, Gigerenzer & Jacobs 2022, abstract): under risk (known options and probabilities) satisficing is typically inferior to optimisation strategies; under uncertainty, satisficing strategies are often derived empirically and can be highly effective; the paper states the conditions under which satisficing can be rational. This is the §06 thesis in one sentence: "good enough" loses in a fully specified problem and can win when the future is uncertain and data are scarce. — same source.
- [V] Scissors metaphor 剪刀比喻: behaviour is shaped by two blades — the structure of the task environment and the computational capabilities of the actor — so a theory about one blade alone cannot explain it. The earliest wording I could document is Newell & Simon 1972, *Human Problem Solving*, p. 55 (as quoted in Gray, Neth & Schoelles 2006, OUP, ch. 8, p. 100); Simon 1990 restates it (Annual Review of Psychology 41:1–20, "Invariants of human behavior", doi:10.1146/annurev.ps.41.020190.000245; its wording not opened). Do not attach it to Simon 1955/1956 and do not quote it from this file.
- [V] Tversky & Kahneman 1974, "Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases", Science 185(4157):1124–1131 (27 Sep 1974), doi:10.1126/science.185.4157.1124 (text opened): a review of three heuristics — representativeness 代表性, availability 可得性, adjustment and anchoring 锚定与调整 — and the biases attached to each. Its own framing, verbatim: "these heuristics are quite useful, but sometimes they lead to severe and systematic errors." Most demonstrations are reported as medians or percentages without N, SD or a test statistic, so the numbers in the next bullets are what the paper states, not independent measurements.
- [V] 1974 anchoring by a wheel of fortune 幸运轮: a number between 0 and 100 was fixed by spinning a wheel in the subjects' presence; subjects first said whether the percentage of African countries in the UN was higher or lower than that number, then estimated it. Median estimates **25 vs 45** for the groups that received **10 vs 65** (derived slope: 20/55 = 0.36 estimate-points per anchor-point). The paper adds that payoffs for accuracy did not reduce the effect. N and spread are not reported. — same paper (text opened).
- [V] 1974 adjustment from a computed starting point: two groups of high-school students had 5 seconds to estimate 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1 or 1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8; medians **2,250 (descending) vs 512 (ascending)**; the correct answer is **40,320** — both far too low (by factors of 18 and 79, derived). — same paper.
- [V] 1974 base-rate neglect: personality descriptions of engineers and lawyers with stated base rates 70/30 or 30/70. Bayes' rule says the odds in the two conditions should differ by (.7/.3)² = 5.44, yet the judgments were "essentially the same"; for the deliberately uninformative description of "Dick" subjects said .5 in both conditions, while with no description they used the base rates (.7 and .3). The experiment itself is Kahneman & Tversky 1973, Psychological Review 80(4):237–251, doi:10.1037/h0034747 (bibliographic data checked; the 1974 text only summarises it).
- [V] 1974 insensitivity to sample size, the hospital problem: a large hospital (about 45 births a day) and a small one (about 15); which recorded more days with more than 60% boys? Of 95 undergraduates, 21 chose the larger, 21 the smaller, 53 "about the same" (within 5%). Correct: the smaller. [derived] binomial with p = .5: P(more than 60% boys) = 15.1% for n = 15 (at least 10 boys) vs 6.8% for n = 45 (at least 28 boys) — the small hospital has 2.2× as many such days.
- [V] 1974 imaginability: median estimates of the number of distinct committees were 70 for 2-member and 20 for 8-member committees (the true number is 45 for both). Calibration: in most earlier studies the true value fell outside the subjects' 98% interval for about 30 percent of the problems instead of 2%, for naive and sophisticated subjects alike, and not fixed by proper scoring rules. (Only the 1974 summary figure — I did not research the overconfidence literature.) — same paper.
- [V] The fair-fight frame: Stanovich & West 2000 (BBS 23(5):645–665, doi:10.1017/S0140525X00003435, abstract opened) list four ways to read the gap between normative models and actual answers — performance errors, computational limitations, the wrong norm applied by the experimenter, a different construal of the task by the subject. From individual-difference data on the classic tasks they conclude that performance errors are a minor factor, that computational limitations underlie non-normative answers on several tasks (especially those needing cognitive decontextualisation), and that covariance patterns can flag a wrong norm or a legitimate alternative construal. Use it to say that "error" is not one thing.
- [V] The 1996 exchange: Kahneman & Tversky, "On the reality of cognitive illusions", Psychological Review 103(3):582–591 (doi:10.1037/0033-295X.103.3.582), answers Gigerenzer, "On narrow norms and vague heuristics", 103(3):592–596 (doi:10.1037/0033-295X.103.3.592). Titles and bibliographic data checked; I could not retrieve abstracts, so I do not summarise either side beyond the titles.
- [V] Kahneman & Klein 2009, "Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree", American Psychologist 64(6):515–526, doi:10.1037/a0016755 (abstract opened): judging the likely quality of an intuition requires assessing how predictable the environment is and whether the person had an opportunity to learn its regularities; subjective confidence is not a reliable indicator of accuracy. The heuristics-and-biases and naturalistic-decision-making camps agree on this boundary.

### B1. Anchoring 锚定效应
- ★[V] Explicit comparative anchors (two-step design: say whether the quantity is higher or lower than a given number, then estimate it): Jacowitz & Kahneman 1995, "Measures of anchoring in estimation tasks", Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 21(11):1161–1166, doi:10.1177/01461672952111004 — anchors set at the calibration group's 15th and 85th percentiles; [def] anchoring index = (median estimate of the high-anchor group − median of the low-anchor group) ÷ (high anchor − low anchor), so 1 means estimates move one-for-one with the anchor and 0 means no effect; the paper's mean index of 0.49 is second-hand (via arXiv 1911.12275) [~]. Many Labs 1 re-ran four such items in 36 samples (N ≈ 5,300–5,600 per item): d = 2.43 (babies born per day), 2.00 (Everest), 1.88 (Chicago population), 1.18 (distance SF–NYC) against an original d = 0.93, significant in every sample (§02-A3). — Klein et al. 2014, doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000178.
- [V] Schley & Weingarten 2026, "Fifty Years of Anchoring Effects: A Theoretical Reintegration and Meta-Analysis", Management Science (online 19 May 2026), doi:10.1287/mnsc.2023.03238 (abstract opened; as of Oct 2026 the largest anchoring meta-analysis I found): 2,601 effect sizes (1,280 comparing high with low anchors); Hedges' g 95% CI [0.765, 0.884] — a large effect that "remains large even after accounting for extensive publication bias". Reduced or null effects are associated with incidental anchoring (numeric priming), anchors from a different dimension, random-number anchors, incentives, debiasing interventions, and whether the anchor gives directional information.
- [V] Caution that applies to every meta-analytic number in this section: Kvarven, Strømland & Johannesson 2020 (Nature Human Behaviour 4(4):423–434, doi:10.1038/s41562-019-0787-z; abstract opened; an Author Correction, 4(6):659–663, exists and was not read) compared 15 meta-analyses with 15 matching preregistered multi-lab replications: effect sizes differ significantly in 12 of 15 pairs, meta-analytic effects are on average almost three times as large, and three bias-correction methods do not substantively fix it.
- [V] Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec 2003, "Coherent arbitrariness", QJE 118(1):73–106, doi:10.1162/00335530360535153: 55 MBA students wrote down the last two digits of their Social Security number, said whether they would pay that many dollars for each product, then stated a willingness to pay; top vs bottom SSN quintile: cordless keyboard $55.64 vs $16.09, trackball $26.18 vs $8.64; SSN–WTP correlations .32–.52; students with above-median SSNs paid 57–107% more.
- [V] The arbitrary-number WTP result came back much weaker: Fudenberg, Levine & Maniadis 2012 (AEJ: Microeconomics 4(2):131–145, doi:10.1257/mic.4.2.131; abstract opened) re-ran the Ariely et al. manipulation on common market goods and found "very weak" anchoring effects, and none at all for binary lotteries. Maniadis, Tufano & List 2014 (AER 104(1):277–290, doi:10.1257/aer.104.1.277; abstract opened) add new evidence on the strength of certain anchoring results and a model showing that the false-positive rate depends on power, priors and the number of scholars testing a question — a few independent replications sharply raise the chance an original finding is true.
- [V] Brewer & Chapman 2002, "The fragile basic anchoring effect", JBDM 15(1):65–77, doi:10.1002/bdm.403 (abstract opened): three experiments (N = 881, 205, 117) on "basic anchoring" — merely being shown or writing an irrelevant number, Wilson, Houston, Etling & Brekke 1996, JEP: General 125(4):387–402, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.125.4.387 — found a weak effect limited to the exact manipulations of Wilson et al.; trivial changes such as reordering the anchor numbers or using different ones eliminated the bias.
- ★[V] Incidental environmental anchors: Critcher & Gilovich 2008 (JBDM 21(3):241–251, doi:10.1002/bdm.586; abstract opened; studies on a jersey number, a product model number, and a restaurant called "Studio 17" vs "Studio 97"). Study 2 (N = 207): estimated share of a new phone's sales in the US 58.1% (SD 19.6) for the "P97" vs 51.9% (SD 21.7) for the "P17", d = 0.30, 95% CI [0.02, 0.58]. Many Labs 2 replicated this item in 59 samples: pooled d = 0.04 [−0.01, 0.09]; only 5.08% of samples significantly positive (3.39% significantly negative) — Klein et al. 2018, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (table opened). Shanks, Barbieri-Hermitte & Vadillo 2020 (Collabra: Psychology 6(1), doi:10.1525/collabra.310; abstract opened): three studies with the Critcher–Gilovich method found no statistically significant incidental anchoring but robust standard (two-step) anchoring, and conclude that anchoring is limited to situations requiring explicit thinking about the anchor.
- [~] Englich, Mussweiler & Strack 2006, "Playing dice with criminal sentences" (PSPB 32(2):188–200, doi:10.1177/0146167205282152): in Study 3 legal experts' sentences stayed pulled towards a demand determined by dice. I did not open the paper, found no large independent replication, and cannot give N or effect size — use only as "reported, not independently replicated at scale".
- [V] Why anchoring is not just a bug: Lieder, Griffiths, Huys & Goodman 2018, "The anchoring bias reflects rational use of cognitive resources", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 25(1):322–349, doi:10.3758/s13423-017-1286-8 (abstract opened): a resource-rational model (adjustment from an anchor under a limited time budget) gives a unifying explanation for ten anchoring phenomena, including the different effect of accuracy motivation on provided versus self-generated anchors. A model that fits known phenomena is not yet a test; see C for the critiques.
- STUDY Explicit comparative anchors — original: Jacowitz & Kahneman 1995 (index ≈ .49 [~]); ML1 d 1.18–2.43 vs original 0.93 in 36 samples, N ≈ 5,500 per item. ### B2. Availability 可得性
- [def] Availability heuristic 可得性启发式: judge how frequent or probable something is by how easily instances come to mind. — Tversky & Kahneman 1973, "Availability: A heuristic for judging frequency and probability", Cognitive Psychology 5(2):207–232, doi:10.1016/0010-0285(73)90033-9 (bibliographic data checked; text not opened).
- [V] Lichtenstein, Slovic, Fischhoff, Layman & Combs 1978, "Judged frequency of lethal events", JEP: Human Learning and Memory 4(6):551–578, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.551 (open copy opened): 41 causes of death, 106 pairs; 111 University of Oregon students and 77 League of Women Voters members judged which cause is more frequent and by what ratio. Against 1970s US rates (deaths per 100 million people per year): stroke 102,000 vs all accidents 55,000 (true ratio 1.85), yet only 20% (students) and 23% (LWV) said stroke is more likely (the students' geometric-mean ratio was .04 — they put accidents 25× above stroke); all disease vs all accidents (true ratio 15.4): judged about equally likely by the students; motor-vehicle accidents vs diabetes (true ratio 1.42): 99% and 96% picked the right one but their geometric-mean ratios were 356× and 99.6×; suicide (12,000) vs homicide (9,200), true ratio 1.30: only 32% and 30% picked suicide. The two groups' judgments correlated .93 across the 106 pairs. Pattern: ranks roughly right, ratios compressed, rare dramatic causes overrated and common quiet causes underrated.
- [V] The mechanism is not the media: Pachur, Hertwig & Steinmann 2012, "How do people judge risks: Availability heuristic, affect heuristic, or both?", JEP: Applied 18(3):314–330, doi:10.1037/a0028279 (abstract opened; student samples): availability-by-recall — the direct experience of risk occurrences in one's own social network — conformed to people's responses best; affect mattered more for value-of-a-statistical-life and perceived-risk judgments than for frequency judgments; encounters with risks in the media "played a negligible role"; some evidence for mechanisms that combine both. Earlier tests: Hertwig, Pachur & Kurzenhäuser 2005 (JEP: LMC 31(4):621–642, doi:10.1037/0278-7393.31.4.621; abstract opened): four candidate mechanisms tested in three risk environments — availability by recall and regressed frequency conformed best, and financial incentives did not improve accuracy.
- [V] Ease of retrieval 提取容易度: Weingarten & Hutchinson 2018, "Does ease mediate the ease-of-retrieval effect? A meta-analysis", Psychological Bulletin 144(3):227–283, doi:10.1037/bul0000122 (abstract opened): 142 papers, 263 studies, 582 effect sizes; the standard few-versus-many manipulation shows a medium-sized effect; about a third to a half of it is mediated by subjective ease; publication bias reduces the standard effect by up to a third; moderator manipulations that differ from the standard one give smaller, often reversed effects. The classic demonstration is Schwarz et al. 1991 (JPSP 61(2):195–202, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.61.2.195): recalling 6 (easy) vs 12 (hard) examples of one's own assertive behaviour — fewer examples led to higher self-rated assertiveness [~: design and cell sizes second-hand, paper not opened].
- [V] Real risk statistics for the "plane vs car" talking point (do not turn them into per-trip odds — one is a population death count, the other a per-flight rate, so exposure differs): WHO fact sheet "Road traffic injuries" (page dated 20 July 2026 when I read it): about 1.16 million road deaths a year, the leading cause of death for ages 5–29, 92% in low- and middle-income countries. IATA 2025 Safety Report (press release 9 March 2026): all-accident rate 1.32 per million flights (51 accidents, 38.7 million flights), 8 fatal accidents with 394 on-board fatalities, fatality risk 0.17 (IATA's own measure), one fatal accident per 5.6 million flights over 2021–2025. — who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries; iata.org press release.
- STUDY Ease-of-retrieval effect — original: Schwarz et al. 1991 (fewer recalled examples → higher self-rating). Later evidence: Weingarten & Hutchinson 2018 (582 effect sizes: medium standard effect, ease mediates a third to a half, publication bias up to a third, many moderators reverse it). GRADE B: direction replicated across many labs, but the size is inflated by bias, only partly mediated by ease, and it flips under common moderators. — doi:10.1037/bul0000122.
- STUDY Misjudging causes of death — original: Lichtenstein et al. 1978 (41 causes; stroke vs accidents 20–23% correct; two groups r = .93). Later evidence: Hertwig et al. 2005 and Pachur et al. 2012 reproduce systematic misjudgment and tie it to recalled personal experience, not media exposure. GRADE B: the distortion pattern is robust; the media-availability explanation is not supported, and the data are US 1970s rates and student samples. — doi:10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.551; 10.1037/a0028279.

### B3. Framing 框架效应
- ★[V] Tversky & Kahneman 1981, "The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice", Science 211(4481):453–458, doi:10.1126/science.7455683 (text opened). Asian disease problem 亚洲疾病问题 (600 people expected to die): gain frame (200 people saved for sure vs a 1/3 chance that all 600 are saved) — 72% chose the sure option (N = 152); loss frame (400 people die for sure vs a 1/3 chance that nobody dies) — 22% chose the sure option (N = 155). Problem 10 (a $5 saving on a $15 calculator vs on a $125 jacket): 68% vs 29% would make the trip (N = 181 in total, per ML2).
- [V] Replication in Many Labs: ML1 (Klein et al. 2014): Asian-disease gain/loss framing original d = 1.13 → 0.58 (median; 86% of 36 samples significant; N = 6,271). ML2 (Klein et al. 2018) replicated the calculator/jacket item — NOT the Asian disease: original d = 1.08 [0.71, 1.45] → pooled 0.40 [0.35, 0.45], 45.45% of the samples individually significant, none significantly reversed (table opened). So the effect is real but replicates at roughly half to a third of the original size (§02-A3).
- [~] Aggregate size: Kühberger 1998 (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 75(1):23–55, doi:10.1006/obhd.1998.2781) meta-analysis of risky-choice framing: 136 papers, 230 effect sizes, mean d ≈ .31 (figures second-hand via Best & Charness 2015; paper not opened). As of Oct 2026 a newer meta-analysis (J Behav Exp Econ 116, 2025, "Meta-analysis examining the relationship between framing effect and risky decisions", RePEc:eee:soceco:v:116:y:2025:i:c:s2214804325000187; abstract level only): 40 studies, N = 17,416, pooled OR = 2.467, stronger for life-death problems, adolescents more susceptible, culture not a moderator.
- [V] Interpretation: Kühberger 2023, "A systematic review of risky-choice framing effects", EXCLI Journal 22:1012–1031 (19 Sep 2023), doi:10.17179/excli2023-6169 (abstract opened): framing effects violate description invariance, and the author's central point is that their existence points to the adaptiveness of the processes underlying judgment and choice rather than simply showing human irrationality. Typology: risky-choice, attribute and goal framing — Levin, Schneider & Gaeth 1998, OBHDP 76(2):149–188, doi:10.1006/obhd.1998.2804 [def; title and bibliographic data checked].
- [~] Attribute framing: ground beef described as "75% lean" vs "25% fat" is rated more favourably in the lean frame, and the difference shrinks after the product is tasted — Levin & Gaeth 1988, J Consumer Research 15(3):374–378, doi:10.1086/209174 (title and bibliographic data checked; effect sizes not opened).
- STUDY Risky-choice framing — original: Tversky & Kahneman 1981 (72% vs 22%, N = 152/155; 68% vs 29% in the calculator/jacket pair). Later evidence: ML1 d 1.13 → 0.58 (36 samples); ML2 d 1.08 → 0.40 (calculator/jacket); meta-analyses d ≈ .31 (1998, second-hand) and OR 2.47 (2025, abstract level). GRADE A: independent large multi-lab replications and meta-analyses agree on direction; the effect is about half to a third of the original size and depends on problem type (stronger in life-death problems). — doi:10.1126/science.7455683; 10.1177/2515245918810225.

### B4. Conjunction fallacy 合取谬误
- [V] Tversky & Kahneman 1983, "Extensional versus intuitive reasoning: The conjunction fallacy in probability judgment", Psychological Review 90(4):293–315, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.90.4.293 (open PDF opened). Linda: "31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright", philosophy major, concerned with discrimination and social justice. Direct test (rank 8 statements incl. "bank teller" T and "bank teller and active in the feminist movement" T&F): violation rates for Linda 89% (naive, N = 88), 90% (informed, N = 53), 85% (sophisticated doctoral students in decision science, N = 32); for the "Bill" problem 92%, 86%, 83% — "no effect of statistical sophistication" in the ranking test.
- [V] Making it transparent (same paper): 142 UBC undergraduates choosing between T and T&F — 85% picked T&F (order inverted for half, no effect); a new group (N = 119) rating on a 9-point scale — mean 3.5 for T vs 5.6 for T&F, 82% rated T&F higher; 65% (n = 58) chose the invalid "resemblance" argument over the valid extensional one; betting task (win $10): 56% (n = 60) still chose T&F; the "T* = bank teller whether or not active in the feminist movement" version: 57% (n = 75) rated T&F higher; 64 social-science graduate students with several statistics courses, rating-scale version: mean 3.5 for T&F vs 3.8 for T, only 36% fallacy — "the first time" in the series the mean pointed the right way.
- ★[V] Not as universal as the headline: Charness, Karni & Levin 2010, "On the conjunction fallacy in probability judgment: New experimental evidence regarding Linda", Games and Economic Behavior 68(2):551–556, doi:10.1016/j.geb.2009.09.003 (paper text opened): UCSB students deciding alone made the error 58.1% (50/86) without incentives and 33.0% (31/94) with incentives, against 85.2% (121/142) in Tversky & Kahneman's UBC sample; deciding in pairs 48.2% (27/56) without and 13.2% (5/38) with incentives; they state they were "unable to replicate" the 85% rate. Footnote: in sessions where the word "single" was deleted from Linda's description the error rate fell sharply.
- [V] The wording debate: Hertwig & Gigerenzer 1999, "The 'conjunction fallacy' revisited: How intelligent inferences look like reasoning errors", JBDM 12(4):275–305, doi:10.1002/(SICI)1099-0771(199912)12:4<275::AID-BDM323>3.0.CO;2-M (text opened): in a paraphrasing study with 18 participants, 15 (83%) violated the conjunction rule in the Linda problem, and only 7 of the 39 paraphrases they gave of "probability" (18%) were mathematical — the word is polysemous (18 different interpretations); asking in frequency terms ("how many of 100 people like Linda ...") cues the mathematical reading and reduces violations. Tentori & Crupi 2012 (Cognition 122(2):123–134, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2011.09.002; abstract opened) answer Hertwig, Benz & Krauss 2008 (Cognition 108(3):740–753, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2008.06.008): they question the claim that responses reflect "reasonable pragmatic and semantic inferences" because "and" is not always ∧, and report two new experiments that "overtly contradict" the HBK data. The adversarial Mellers–Hertwig–Kahneman test is in §02-E.
- STUDY Conjunction fallacy (Linda) — original: Tversky & Kahneman 1983 (85–90% of N = 88–142 undergraduates; 36% in statistically trained graduates on the rating version). Later evidence: Charness et al. 2010 (58% alone, 33% with incentives, 13% in incentivised pairs); Hertwig & Gigerenzer 1999 (wording/polysemy); Tentori & Crupi 2012 (fallacy survives control of "and"); Mellers et al. 2001 (§02-E). GRADE B: the effect is reproduced across labs and persists under many controls, but its size ranges from about 13% to 90% with incentives, wording and format, and "people cannot reason about probability" is an overreach. — doi:10.1037/0033-295X.90.4.293; 10.1016/j.geb.2009.09.003.

### B5. Base rates 基率
- [~] Cab problem (Bar-Hillel 1980, "The base-rate fallacy in probability judgments", Acta Psychologica 44(3):211–233, doi:10.1016/0001-6918(80)90046-3; metadata checked, text not opened): 85% of a city's cabs are Green and 15% Blue; a witness who is 80% reliable says the cab was Blue. Bayes: .15×.8 ÷ (.15×.8 + .85×.2) = 41% (derived). Typical answers near the witness's 80% [~, second-hand]; Bar-Hillel's point, as I know it from the literature, is that base rates are used more when they look causally relevant [~: not checked in the paper].
- [V] Koehler 1996, "The base rate fallacy reconsidered: Descriptive, normative, and methodological challenges", BBS 19(1):1–17, doi:10.1017/S0140525X00041157 (abstract opened earlier): base rates are almost always used, and how much they are used depends on task structure and representation — so "people ignore base rates completely" is overstated; the engineer–lawyer result concerns the case where a worthless description is present. Natural-frequency formats help with Bayes problems (§02-C: 16% → 46% in Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995).
- STUDY Base-rate neglect — original: Kahneman & Tversky 1973 / T&K 1974 (70/30 vs 30/70 base rates, same judgments with a description; .5 for the worthless description). Later evidence: Koehler 1996 review (base rates almost always used, degree depends on task); Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 and McDowell & Jacobs 2017 meta-analysis (format changes accuracy; §02-C). GRADE B: neglect or underuse is reliable when a vivid description is present, but "complete neglect" is [✗] overstated and the size depends on format and causal framing. — doi:10.1017/S0140525X00041157.

### B6. Priming 启动效应 (behavioural priming collapse)
- ★[V] Bargh, Chen & Burrows 1996 (JPSP 71(2):230–244, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.71.2.230): participants unwittingly exposed to the elderly stereotype (scrambled-sentence task) walked more slowly down a hallway afterwards. Doyen et al. describe the original as 60 participants in total [~; 1996 paper not opened]. Doyen, Klein, Pichon & Cleeremans 2012 (PLoS ONE 7(1):e29081, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0029081; text/abstract opened): Experiment 1 (120 undergraduates, 4 experimenters, infrared timing): primed M = 6.27 s (SD 2.15) vs unprimed 6.39 s (SD 1.11), F < 1, η² = .01 — no priming effect. Experiment 2 (50 participants, 10 experimenters): a walking-speed difference appeared only when experimenters had been led to expect slower walking.
- [V] Shanks et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE 8(4):e56515, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0056515; abstract opened): 9 experiments, N = 475, trying to reproduce "intelligence priming" (primes such as "professor" or "soccer hooligan" changing general-knowledge accuracy) — none obtained the effect, a Bayesian analysis gave considerable support for the null, and financial incentives did raise performance.
- [V] The aggregate view: Weingarten et al. 2016 (Psychological Bulletin 142(5):472–497, doi:10.1037/bul0000030; abstract opened) meta-analysed 352 effect sizes from 133 studies of incidental word primes on behaviour: small effect (d = .332 fixed-effects, .352 random-effects), "only minimally biased" by publication bias in the authors' analysis. Read against Kvarven 2020 (meta-analyses run about three times too high) and against the multi-lab nulls below, this average is not evidence that any specific famous priming result is real.
- [V] Multi-lab results: ML1 flag priming and currency priming "did not replicate" (§02-A3); ML2: "priming heat → belief in global warming" original d = 0.31 [0.03, 0.59] → −0.03 [−0.09, 0.03], and "structure promotes goal pursuit" 0.49 → −0.02 [−0.07, 0.03] (table opened).
- [V] Kahneman's letter of 26 September 2012 to priming researchers ("A proposal to deal with questions about priming effects") warned of "a train wreck looming" for social priming and proposed a daisy-chain of replications across labs (paraphrase; source: Nature news by Yong, 3 Oct 2012, doi:10.1038/nature.2012.11535, and the letter as supplementary PDF at nature.com/news/polopoly_fs/7.6716.1349271308!/suppinfoFile/Kahneman Letter.pdf).
- STUDY Elderly-prime slows walking — original: Bargh et al. 1996 (about 60 participants in total per Doyen). Later evidence: Doyen et al. 2012 (N = 120 with infrared timing: 6.27 vs 6.39 s; effect only under experimenter expectation); ML1/ML2 other priming nulls. GRADE D: the direct replication failed and the one positive pattern tracked experimenter expectations; teach as a caution about small-sample, subtle-measure findings. — doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0029081.
- STUDY Word primes change behaviour — original: Bargh et al. 1996 and similar studies. Later evidence: Weingarten et al. 2016 (d ≈ .35 overall, moderated by value of the goal); Shanks et al. 2013 and ML2 nulls for specific effects; Kvarven 2020 shows meta-analytic effects overshoot replications. GRADE C: the aggregate is positive but the best-known individual effects failed, and the average is probably inflated; do not treat as a conclusion. — doi:10.1037/bul0000030; 10.1371/journal.pone.0056515.

### B7. Dual-process 双过程 and the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) — includes ADDED items (De Neys 2023, Bago & De Neys 2019, Meyer & Frederick 2023, and the contrast-vs-two-systems grading)
- [~] The labels: "System 1 / System 2" are usually credited to Stanovich & West 2000 (§A; I did not open the text to find the first use), and Kahneman & Frederick 2002 ("Representativeness revisited: Attribute substitution in intuitive judgment", in *Heuristics and Biases*, CUP, pp. 49–81, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511808098.004) describe attribute substitution — answering an easier question than the one asked. Kahneman 2003, "A perspective on judgment and choice: Mapping bounded rationality", American Psychologist 58(9):697–720, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.58.9.697 (bibliographic data checked; texts not opened).
- [V] Evans & Stanovich 2013, "Dual-process theories of higher cognition: Advancing the debate", Perspectives on Psychological Science 8(3):223–241, doi:10.1177/1745691612460685 (abstract opened): answers five lines of criticism, argues they are overstated, and prefers a version in which rapid autonomous Type 1 processes yield default responses unless intervened on by Type 2 reasoning; what defines the difference is that Type 2 supports hypothetical thinking and loads heavily on working memory.
- [V] Melnikoff & Bargh 2018, "The Mythical Number Two", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 22(4):280–293, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2018.02.001 (abstract opened): the common typology — one type of process intentional, controllable, conscious and inefficient, the other unintentional, uncontrollable, unconscious and efficient — "lacks empirical support, contradicts well-established findings, and is internally incoherent" (the authors' summary of what critics point out); they argue the assumption that phenomena divide into two types thwarts progress. Four comment/reply items appeared in the same journal in 2018 (PubMed "comment in" list).
- [V] De Neys 2023, "Advancing theorizing about fast-and-slow thinking", BBS 46:e111 (online 2 Sep 2022), doi:10.1017/S0140525X2200142X (abstract opened): critiques two features of fast-and-slow models — exclusivity (intuition and deliberation produce unique responses, so some answers are "beyond" the fast mode) and the switch mechanism between them; reviews empirical evidence and finds no solid ground for exclusivity, and argues the switch accounts are conceptually problematic because they presuppose exclusivity.
- [V] Bago & De Neys 2019, *The Smart System 1: evidence for the intuitive nature of correct responding on the bat-and-ball problem* (title), Thinking & Reasoning 25(3):257–299, doi:10.1080/13546783.2018.1507949 (abstract opened): seven studies with a two-response paradigm (first an answer under time pressure and load, then a free answer) found that many people who finally answer correctly already gave the correct answer in the quick first response — correct answers need not be "corrections" of an intuition. Meyer & Frederick 2023 ("The formation and revision of intuitions", Cognition 240:105380, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105380; abstract opened) report 59 new studies (N = 72,310), mostly on the bat-and-ball problem: the erroneous intuition often survives whatever reflection can be induced, especially when the intuition is approximately correct. So the data cut both ways: intuition can be right, and reflection often fails to correct a wrong intuition.
- [V] Frederick 2005, "Cognitive reflection and decision making", Journal of Economic Perspectives 19(4):25–42, doi:10.1257/089533005775196732 (text read via a mirror copy of the article; Crossref confirms the bibliographic data): three items — bat-and-ball (ball = 5 cents; the intuitive 10 cents makes the total $1.20), 5 machines/5 minutes/100 machines (5 minutes), lily pads (47 days). N = 3,428 in 35 studies collected over 26 months from January 2003; mean 1.24 correct; 0/1/2/3 correct = 33/28/23/17%; MIT students 48% all three correct, Toledo students 5%; people who answered 10 cents estimated 92% of others would get it right, people who answered 5 cents estimated 62%.
- [V] A large general-population sample: Sinayev & Peters 2015, "Cognitive reflection vs. calculation in decision making", Frontiers in Psychology 6:532, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00532 (Study 1, text opened): Understanding America Study, internet panel recruited by address-based sampling, N = 1,413 (31 May–22 Oct 2014): bat-and-ball — 78% intuitive error (10 cents), 14% correct (5 cents), 8% other; lily pads — 54% intuitive error (24 days), 29% correct (47), 17% other.
- [V] Disfluency as a "System 2 trigger" did not replicate: ML2 item "Disfluency engages analytic processing" (Alter, Oppenheimer, Epley & Eyre 2007, Study 4, JEP: General 136(4):569–576, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.136.4.569; 41 participants): original d = 0.63 [−0.004, 1.25] → pooled d = −0.03 [−0.08, 0.01]; N = 6,935; syllogisms answered correctly M = 1.03 (hard-to-read font) vs 1.06 (easy font). — Klein et al. 2018 (text and table opened).
- STUDY Fast vs slow as contrast — claim graded: processing differs in speed, effort and working-memory load, and these differences are useful for describing judgment tasks. original: the fast/slow contrast as popularised by Stanovich & West 2000 and Kahneman & Frederick 2002 (first use not traced). Later evidence: Evans & Stanovich 2013 (Type 1/Type 2 defined by autonomy vs working-memory load); Bago & De Neys 2019 (two-response paradigm finds distinguishable fast and slow responses); Meyer & Frederick 2023. GRADE B: useful and supported as a contrast of response types, but the features do not cluster neatly (Melnikoff & Bargh 2018), and "fast = wrong, slow = right" is not what the data show. — doi:10.1177/1745691612460685; 10.1080/13546783.2018.1507949.
- STUDY Two literal systems — claim graded: the mind contains two separate systems, one producing errors and the other correcting them. original: the popular reading of the System 1 / System 2 labels (no single source). Later evidence: De Neys 2023 (no solid ground for exclusivity; switch accounts problematic); Bago & De Neys 2019 (correct answers often already intuitive); Melnikoff & Bargh 2018; ML2 disfluency d 0.63 → −0.03. GRADE D as a literal two-system architecture (supporters such as Evans & Stanovich 2013 keep a weaker Type 1/Type 2 distinction, graded B above); teach "System 1/2" as a metaphor, not a mechanism. — doi:10.1017/S0140525X2200142X; 10.1016/j.tics.2018.02.001.
- STUDY Cognitive Reflection Test — claim graded: most people give the intuitive wrong answer to bat-and-ball-type items, and CRT scores differ widely across people. original: Frederick 2005 (N = 3,428; 33% scored 0; MIT 48% vs Toledo 5% all correct). Later evidence: Sinayev & Peters 2015 (N = 1,413 US panel; 78% answered 10 cents); Meyer & Frederick 2023 (59 studies, N = 72,310; intuition often survives reflection). GRADE A for "the intuitive error is common and the pattern replicates across large, varied samples"; the interpretation (System 1 vs System 2) is graded separately above. — doi:10.1257/089533005775196732; 10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00532.

### B8. ADDED — the hot hand 热手
- [V] Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky 1985, "The hot hand in basketball: On the misperception of random sequences", Cognitive Psychology 17(3):295–314, doi:10.1016/0010-0285(85)90010-6: 76ers field goals 1980–81 (9 players, 48 home games), Celtics free throws (9 players), and a controlled experiment with 26 Cornell players (100 shots each from a distance set to about 50% difficulty); they found no evidence of streak shooting beyond chance and concluded the belief in the hot hand is a cognitive illusion. (Sample sizes as described by Miller & Sanjurjo; the often-quoted share of fans who believe in streak shooting is [~] unverified — do not use.)
- ★[V] The estimator problem: Miller & Sanjurjo 2018, "Surprised by the Hot Hand Fallacy? A Truth in the Law of Small Numbers", Econometrica 86(6):2019–2047, doi:10.3982/ECTA14943 (arXiv 1902.01265; text opened): in a finite sequence of fair coin flips the expected proportion of heads on flips that follow a head is below 50% — for three flips it is 5/12 = 41.7% (my enumeration over the 6 sequences with a flip after a head: (1 + ½ + 0 + 0 + 1 + 0)/6 confirms it). The usual estimates — the hit rate after a streak of hits, or its difference from the hit rate after a streak of misses — are therefore biased downward in finite sequences, and a truly random shooter shows a negative expected difference. [derived] My simulation (100,000 sequences of 100 fair flips, streak length 3): average hit rate after three hits 46.1%, average difference between "after three hits" and "after three misses" −7.9 points. For GVT's controlled experiment they report a measured difference of about +3 to +4 percentage points, an expected difference of about −8 points for a Bernoulli (no-hot-hand) shooter, and a bias-corrected difference of about +12 to +13 points (significant, p < .01, SE ≈ 4.7 points). They conclude that the belief in the hot hand is not a fallacy in the sense GVT claimed, while noting that beliefs may still be too strong or too weak.
- [~] Bocskocsky, Ezekowitz & Stein 2014 (MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference, "The hot hand: A new approach to an old 'fallacy'"): controlling partly for defence and shot location, the hot-hand pattern is positive; known to me only via Miller & Sanjurjo's citation, not opened — do not quote numbers.
- [V] Green & Zwiebel 2018, "The Hot-Hand Fallacy: Cognitive Mistakes or Equilibrium Adjustments? Evidence from Major League Baseball", Management Science 64(11):5315–5348, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2017.2804 (abstract opened): panel data show strong evidence of a hot hand in all 10 statistical categories, with "hot" corresponding to one-half to one standard deviation of player ability; the authors attribute the basketball nulls to endogenous defensive responses (defence can equalise shooting probabilities in basketball but not in baseball); teams respond about right to hot opponents except for overreacting to the last five attempts.
- [V] 2020s evidence (as of Oct 2026; abstracts only, found by a DOI-based citation lookup): Ritzwoller & Romano 2022 (Review of Economic Studies 89(2):976–1007, doi:10.1093/restud/rdab020) show that permutation tests of randomness in controlled shooting experiments need substantially larger data sets for an informative measurement, and in the one data set they could obtain find that one shooter shows a pattern significantly inconsistent with randomness while evidence for the others is lacking. Lantis & Nesson 2024 (Journal of Sports Economics 25(3):283–321, doi:10.1177/15270025231222630) study the 1986–2019 NBA 3-Point Contests and report that a hot hand exists, but only for shots taken from the same location. No newer synthesis was opened.
- STUDY Hot-hand fallacy — claim graded: there is no hot hand and the belief is an illusion. original: GVT 1985 (26 players × 100 shots; measured difference ≈ +3/+4 points, not significant). Later evidence: Miller & Sanjurjo 2018 (bias in the estimator; corrected ≈ +12/+13 points, p < .01); Green & Zwiebel 2018 (strong hot hand in MLB); Lantis & Nesson 2024 (hot hand in same-location shots); Ritzwoller & Romano 2022 (low power). GRADE D for "there is no hot hand in sports and belief in it is a fallacy" — substantially revised by a statistical correction and by field data. GRADE C for "a large hot hand exists in basketball shooting" — size and generality are contested (defence, location, power). — doi:10.3982/ECTA14943.

### B9. ADDED — loss aversion 损失厌恶
- [def] Loss aversion 损失厌恶 in prospect theory 前景理论: outcomes are valued as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and the value function is steeper for losses; the coefficient λ scales the loss branch against the gain branch (for equal curvature, the ratio of the slopes). — Kahneman & Tversky 1979, Econometrica 47(2):263–291, doi:10.2307/1914185 (bibliographic data checked; text not opened). [~] In the cumulative version (Tversky & Kahneman 1992, J Risk Uncertainty 5(4):297–323, doi:10.1007/BF00122574) the median λ was 2.25 for 25 graduate students in three unincentivised sessions, with curvature α = β = 0.88 (figures as reported by Brown et al. below; paper not opened).
- [V] Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer 2024, "Meta-analysis of empirical estimates of loss aversion", Journal of Economic Literature 62(2):485–516, doi:10.1257/jel.20221698 (abstract and open PDF opened): 607 estimates of λ from 150 articles in economics, psychology, neuroscience and other fields (1992–2017); mean λ = 1.955, 95% probability interval [1.820, 2.102]; raw median 1.69, raw mean 1.97; 93.9% of the estimates exceed 1; of the 419 estimates with standard errors, 398 (95%) lie above 1 and 76.6% of those are significantly above 1; few study-design characteristics are substantially correlated with the mean.
- [derived] What λ ≈ 1.955 means (linear value function, no probability weighting): a 50/50 gamble that risks $100 needs a prize of about $195.5 to be a break-even offer (0.5·G = 0.5·1.955·100); with 2.25 it is $225, with the raw median 1.69 it is $169.
- [V] The sceptical review: Gal & Rucker 2018, "The Loss of Loss Aversion: Will It Loom Larger Than Its Gain?", Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3):497–516, doi:10.1002/jcpy.1047 (abstract opened): review of the evidence concluding that it does not support losses, on balance, being any more impactful than gains, and asking why the belief persists. Reply: Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter & Herrmann 2020, "Moderating loss aversion: Loss aversion has moderators, but reports of its death are greatly exaggerated", J Consumer Psychology 30(3):407–428, doi:10.1002/jcpy.1156 (abstract opened): five samples (N = 17,720); more domain knowledge and experience went with lower loss aversion though people at all levels were loss averse; older consumers were more loss averse; they cast doubt on claims that loss aversion is a fallacy or fully explained by status-quo bias, risk aversion or student samples.
- [V] Context dependence: Walasek & Stewart 2015 (JEP: General 144(1):7–11, doi:10.1037/xge0000039; abstract opened): in four experiments, changing the range of gains and losses people saw while their loss aversion was elicited produced loss aversion, loss neutrality and even the reverse of loss aversion (a decision-by-sampling account). Cross-country check: Ruggeri et al. 2020 (Nature Human Behaviour 4(6):622–633, doi:10.1038/s41562-020-0886-x; abstract opened) directly re-tested Kahneman & Tversky's 1979 risky-choice items (adjusting only currencies; every participant answered every item) with 4,098 participants in 19 countries and 13 languages: the results replicated for 94% of items, with some attenuation, and 12 of 13 theoretical contrasts replicated; this supports the 1979 risky-choice patterns generally, not a particular λ.
- STUDY Losses loom larger (λ > 1) — claim graded: losses outweigh equal gains in mixed monetary gambles. original: Kahneman & Tversky 1979; T&K 1992 (median λ = 2.25, 25 students). Later evidence: Brown et al. 2024 (607 estimates, 150 articles: mean 1.955 [1.820, 2.102]; 93.9% of estimates > 1); Mrkva et al. 2020 (N = 17,720, loss averse at every knowledge level). GRADE A: many independent samples and methods agree on direction and rough size in monetary risky choice. — doi:10.1257/jel.20221698.
- STUDY Loss aversion as universal law — claim graded: losses weigh more than gains in every domain. original: the prospect-theory tradition (Kahneman & Tversky 1979; Tversky & Kahneman 1992). Later evidence: Gal & Rucker 2018 (review: not supported on balance); Mrkva et al. 2020 (supported, with moderators: knowledge, age); Walasek & Stewart 2015 (can be made to vanish or reverse by changing the range of outcomes). GRADE B: reliable as a tendency in monetary risky choice and moderated by context; "a universal law" is not supported. — doi:10.1002/jcpy.1047; 10.1002/jcpy.1156; 10.1037/xge0000039.
- STUDY λ ≈ 2 as typical — claim graded: λ ≈ 2 is a typical value. original: Tversky & Kahneman 1992 (median λ = 2.25, 25 graduate students; via Brown et al.). Later evidence: Brown et al. 2024 mean 1.955 [1.820, 2.102] vs raw median 1.69. GRADE B: a fair summary of the literature's centre, but estimates depend on elicitation method and sample. And "λ = 2.25 is a constant of human nature" — GRADE D: a single median from 25 unincentivised graduate students in 1992 (via Brown et al.), ABOVE (not below — CORRECTION, lead 2026-10-14: 2.25 > 1.955) the later literature's mean and far from a fixed constant. — doi:10.1257/jel.20221698.

### C. "Good enough" as an optimum: the derivation
- ★[def] Bias–variance decomposition 偏差–方差分解: for squared-error prediction, total error = (bias)² + variance + noise. Bias = systematic gap between the model's average prediction (over training samples) and the true function; variance = how much the fitted model changes from one finite sample to another; noise = irreducible. A flexible model has low bias but, with few noisy observations, high variance — the bias–variance dilemma. — Gigerenzer & Brighton 2009, "Homo heuristicus: Why biased minds make better inferences", Topics in Cognitive Science 1(1):107–143, doi:10.1111/j.1756-8765.2008.01006.x (text opened at library.mpib-berlin.mpg.de/ft/gg/GG_Homo_2009.pdf); derivation in Geman, Bienenstock & Doursat 1992, "Neural networks and the bias/variance dilemma", Neural Computation 4(1):1–58, doi:10.1162/neco.1992.4.1.1.
- [V] Worked example 1 (G&B 2009, Fig. 3): London's mean daily temperature in 2000; polynomials fitted to random samples of 30 observations. The more the degree grows, the lower the error in fitting the sample; the error in predicting new observations is U-shaped, and the best-predicting polynomial has degree 4 (they also draw degree 3 and degree 12). Fit is not prediction.
- [V] Worked example 2 (G&B 2009, Fig. 4): a fictional temperature pattern that is exactly a degree-3 polynomial plus noise, samples of 30: the degree-3 model has the lowest prediction error, but a degree-2 polynomial (biased) predicts better than a degree-10 polynomial (unbiased on average) — a biased model can beat an unbiased one when samples are small.
- [def] Heuristics used below: take-the-best 择优而行 — order cues by validity, compare the options on the first cue, decide by the first cue that discriminates, ignore the rest; Minimalist — same with random cue order; Dawes's rule / tallying 计数法 — count the cues favouring each option with equal weights (a unit-weight linear model); 1/N — split money equally across N assets. — Gigerenzer & Goldstein 1996 (Psychological Review 103(4):650–669, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.103.4.650); Todd & Gigerenzer 2000.
- ★[V] Czerlinski, Gigerenzer & Goldstein 1999 (ch. 6 of *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart*, OUP, pp. 97–118) cross-validated heuristics and regression on 20 real data sets, all paired comparisons (e.g. which of two Chicago high schools has the higher dropout rate, from cues such as writing score and the proportion of Hispanic students); 10 of the 20 sets come from Weisberg's 1985 regression textbook; both kinds of model were cross-validated, correcting an imbalance in earlier Dawes–Corrigan comparisons (G&B 2009). Averages across the 20 sets (Todd & Gigerenzer 2000 précis, Table 1; text opened at library.mpib-berlin.mpg.de/ft/pt/PT_Precis_2000.pdf):
  | Strategy | cues looked up (frugality) | accuracy fitting the data, % | accuracy predicting new data, % |
  | Minimalist | 2.2 | 69 | 65 |
  | Take the Best | 2.4 | 75 | 71 |
  | Dawes's rule (tallying) | 7.7 | 73 | 69 |
  | Multiple regression | 7.7 | 77 | 68 |
  Regression fits best (77 vs 75) but predicts worse than Take the Best (68 vs 71): the crossover is the overfitting signature; the précis states Take the Best beat all three other algorithms in generalisation by at least two percentage points. The mean number of predictors available was 7.7.
- [V] Replication caveat — heuristica R package vignette (Czerlinski Whitmore 2021, CRAN: cran.r-project.org/web/packages/heuristica/vignettes/reproducing-results.html; opened): on the city-population data (83 German cities, 9 binary cues) the book's fitting accuracy was 74% for regression, Dawes and Take the Best and 70% for Minimalist; in the vignette's 50/50 cross-validation (200 repetitions, whereas the book used 10,000) fitting → prediction was regression 75.2 → 70.8, Take the Best 74.9 → 71.0, unit weight 73.6 → 70.2, Minimalist 70.4 → 67.0 — regression and Take the Best are within 0.2 points, and the vignette itself says a few hundred repetitions are not enough for stable results. On the Chicago high-school dropout data (63 schools, 23 real-valued cues, 100 repetitions) the vignette reports Take the Best and the unit-weight model predicting better than regression (shown in a plot only, no numbers). So the statement that simple rules match or slightly beat regression out of sample with a fraction of the cues is supported; "heuristics crush statistics" is not.
- [V] The less-is-more effect 少即是多: Gigerenzer & Goldstein 1996 simulated 500 individuals per condition on the 83 German cities with more than 100,000 inhabitants (9 binary cues), each tested on all 3,403 city pairs; the maximum accuracy, about 77%, was reached not with full knowledge but with partial knowledge (about 60 of the cities recognised); mechanism: with a recognition validity of 80%, recognition decides about half the pairs (80% correct) and guessing the other half (50%), giving 65% at zero cue knowledge. G&B 2009 add that Take the Best is unbiased in non-compensatory environments (analytic results) and that a heuristic's advantage depends on the environment and sample size, so "it avoids overfitting" is only shorthand.
- ★[V] 1/N 等权配置: DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal 2009, "Optimal versus naive diversification: How inefficient is the 1/N portfolio strategy?", Review of Financial Studies 22(5):1915–1953, doi:10.1093/rfs/hhm075 (abstract opened at ideas.repec.org and london.edu): of 14 models on seven empirical datasets none is consistently better than 1/N in Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return or turnover, meaning the gain from optimal diversification is more than offset by estimation error; with parameters calibrated to the US equity market the estimation window needed for sample-based mean–variance to outperform 1/N is around 3,000 months for 25 assets and about 6,000 months for 50 assets (≈ 250 and 500 years, derived).
- [V] Counter-evidence: Kirby & Ostdiek 2012, "It's all in the timing: Simple active portfolio strategies that outperform naïve diversification", J Financial and Quantitative Analysis 47(2):437–467, doi:10.1017/S0022109012000117 (abstract opened): DeMiguel et al.'s result is largely due to their research design (portfolios with high estimation risk and extreme turnover); mean–variance optimisation often beats 1/N, but turnover can erode the advantage under transaction costs; two simple timing strategies beat 1/N even with high costs. Another field: Wübben & von Wangenheim 2008 (J Marketing 72(3):82–93, doi:10.1509/jmkg.72.3.082; abstract opened): the simple heuristics firms use performed at least as well as the stochastic Pareto/NBD-type models for managerially relevant tasks, except for predicting purchases at the overall customer-base level.
- [V] Resource rationality 资源理性: Lieder & Griffiths 2020, "Resource-rational analysis: Understanding human cognition as the optimal use of limited computational resources", BBS 43:e1 (online 4 Feb 2019), doi:10.1017/S0140525X1900061X (abstract opened): identifies the rational use of limited resources as a unifying principle and proposes a modelling paradigm in which cognitive mechanisms are derived as optimal under realistic cognitive constraints, claiming it can reconcile impressive cognitive skills with apparent irrationality; the target article drew 26 open peer commentaries in the same volume (PubMed "comment in" list). Griffiths, Lieder & Goodman 2015 (Topics in Cognitive Science 7(2):217–229, doi:10.1111/tops.12142; abstract opened): define levels of analysis between Marr's computational and algorithmic levels and give a recipe — derive optimal algorithms for increasingly realistic abstract computational architectures. Gershman, Horvitz & Tenenbaum 2015 (Science 349(6245):273–278, doi:10.1126/science.aac6076; abstract opened): "computational rationality" — choose the decision with highest expected utility while accounting for the costs of computation in problems where calculations can only be approximated.
