## 12 · 读心与社会影响 (reading minds & social influence)
Story hooks for the lesson: the hardest thing to predict is another predictor (心理理论 theory of mind); other people's judgments are evidence (从众 conformity/服从 obedience); several famous social-psychology "facts" need correction (below). Development detail of ToM lives in T3b; only the claims needed here are given.

### A1 · 心理理论 theory of mind (ToM) — definition, false-belief task, autism claim
- [def] ★ 心理理论 theory of mind: "an individual has a theory of mind if he imputes mental states to himself and others" — the working definition in Premack & Woodruff 1978; they tested an adult chimpanzee with videotaped problems and photographs of solutions and read its consistent correct choices as evidence it understood the actor's purpose (open peer commentary in the same BBS issue — e.g., Dennett 1978, BBS 1:568–570, doi:10.1017/S0140525X00076664 — is where the false-belief test was proposed; commentary not opened). — *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 1(4):515–526, doi:10.1017/S0140525X00076512 (abstract read, OA)
- [def] 错误信念任务 false-belief task: child must predict where an actor will search for an object that was moved without the actor's knowledge (unexpected-transfer paradigm: Wimmer & Perner 1983, "Maxi and the chocolate"; Sally–Anne puppet version used by Baron-Cohen et al. 1985). Passing = pointing to where the character BELIEVES the object is, not where it is. — Wimmer & Perner 1983, *Cognition* 13:103–128, doi:10.1016/0010-0277(83)90004-5 (citation verified; paper not opened)
- [V] ★ Wellman, Cross & Watson 2001 meta-analysis: **178 separate studies**; false-belief performance clusters by age, country and four task factors (combined model multiple R = .74, R² = .55); preschoolers go **from below-chance to above-chance** performance, a consistent pattern across countries and task variations; this is inconsistent with "it's only a task artifact" and fits genuine conceptual change in the preschool years. (The abstract does not give the median age of success; the textbook "about 4" is the usual summary — see T3b for the age curve.) — *Child Development* 72(3):655–684, doi:10.1111/1467-8624.00304 (abstract read)
- GRADE A: STUDY false-belief task as a marker of a developmental change — original: Wimmer & Perner 1983 (3–9 year-olds). Later evidence: Wellman et al. 2001 (178 studies, R² = .55; cross-cultural), plus the language-ToM link (Milligan et al. 2007 below). Task-version details (verbal vs implicit) are disputed — that is T3b territory. — sources above
- [V] Language and false belief go together: meta-analysis of **104 studies (N = 8,891)** of children under 7 found a moderate-to-large correlation between language ability and false-belief understanding that remains significant with age controlled; stronger from earlier language to later false belief than the reverse; receptive vocabulary related more weakly than general language. — Milligan, Astington & Dack 2007, *Child Development* 78(2):622–646, doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.01018.x (abstract read)
- [~] ★ Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith 1985 (the autism result): Sally–Anne task, three groups — 20 autistic children (mean CA 11;11, mean verbal MA 5;5), 14 children with Down syndrome (CA 10;11, vMA 2;11), 27 typically developing 4-year-olds (CA 4;5). On the belief question **23/27 (85%) typical, 12/14 (86%) Down syndrome, 4/20 (20%) autistic** answered correctly; 16 of the 20 autistic children pointed to where the marble really was. Numbers from a teaching summary (holah.co.uk) and a news-style summary that agree with each other; I could not open the *Cognition* PDF itself. — *Cognition* 21(1):37–46, doi:10.1016/0010-0277(85)90022-8
- [V] ★ The strong reading ("autistic people lack a theory of mind") is not supported: Gernsbacher & Yergeau 2019 review evidence that autistic people are not uniquely impaired on ToM tasks (children with specific language impairment, Down syndrome, Williams syndrome, cerebral palsy, Fragile X and others also fail false-belief tasks), that some original findings failed to replicate, that ToM tasks correlate poorly with each other and with autistic traits/empathy, and that vocabulary predicts false-belief performance more strongly than age or diagnosis; they call the claim "empirically questionable and societally harmful". They note Baron-Cohen himself wrote (2009, 2010) that a ToM lack "may not be specific" to autism. — *Archives of Scientific Psychology* 7(1):102–118, doi:10.1037/arc0000067 (full text read via PMC6959478)
- [V] Meta-analytic basis for a *deficit-on-tasks* claim (not a "lack"): Yirmiya et al. 1998, 3 meta-analyses (autism vs mental retardation vs typical development): individuals with autism and with intellectual disability both show impaired ToM task performance; etiology (e.g., Down syndrome), CA and verbal mental age moderate results. — *Psychological Bulletin* 124(3):283–307, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.124.3.283 (abstract read)
- [def] 双重共情问题 double empathy problem: communication breakdowns between autistic and non-autistic people are a two-way, mismatch-of-neurotype problem, not one-sided "mindblindness". — Milton 2012, *Disability & Society* 27(6):883–887, doi:10.1080/09687599.2012.710008
- [V] Test of double empathy: Crompton et al. 2020 ran 8-person "diffusion chains" (n = 72; a story retold from person to person) with all-autistic, all-non-autistic and mixed chains: detail retention declined **faster in mixed chains**; all-autistic chains did not differ from all-non-autistic chains; rapport ratings were lowest in mixed chains. Small single-lab study → GRADE C {n = 72; I found no independent replication of the information-transfer result}. — *Autism* 24(7):1704–1712, doi:10.1177/1362361320919286 (abstract read)
- GRADE D for "autistic people lack ToM" as a blanket claim; GRADE B for the narrower claim "a subset of autistic children pass standard false-belief tasks later than typical peers, and language ability accounts for much of the gap" {Yirmiya 1998; Milligan 2007; Gernsbacher & Yergeau 2019}.

### A2 · 归因 attribution — fundamental attribution error / correspondence bias, culture, actor–observer
- [def] Heider 1958 (*The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations*): people explain behavior as coming from the person (dispositions) or the situation — the founding distinction of attribution theory. 基本归因错误 fundamental attribution error was named by Ross 1977 (in Ross's chapter "The intuitive psychologist and his shortcomings", *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 10) after the quiz-game demonstration (Ross, Amabile & Steinmetz 1977, *JPSP* 35:485–494: randomly assigned "questioners" were rated more knowledgeable than "contestants"). [Citations verified in Crossref — doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(08)60357-3 and doi:10.1037/0022-3514.35.7.485; neither paper opened, so the 'questioner rated more knowledgeable' description is the standard textbook account.]
- [V] Gilbert & Malone 1995 define the broader 对应偏差 correspondence bias — drawing inferences about a person's enduring dispositions from behavior that the situation fully explains — and list four mechanisms (lack of awareness, unrealistic expectations, inflated categorizations, incomplete corrections). — *Psychological Bulletin* 117(1):21–38, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.117.1.21
- [V] Gawronski 2004 argues the phenomenon is real but the explanation "perceivers ubiquitously consider situational factors to have little impact" has no supporting evidence; the correspondence bias results from several processes tied to perceivers' causal theories ("the FAE is dead, long live the correspondence bias"). — *European Review of Social Psychology* 15:183–217, doi:10.1080/10463280440000026 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Jones & Harris 1967** essay-attribution paradigm ("assigned" essay on Castro/the death penalty, observers still infer a matching attitude) is the basic test; the paradigm replicated at very large scale in **Many Labs 2** (Klein et al. 2018): 7,197 participants, assigned pro- vs anti-death-penalty essay, controlling perceived constraint → perceived attitude differed by **d = 1.82** (95% CI 1.76–1.87). — Klein et al. 2018, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 1(4):443–490, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (full text read, OA); Jones & Harris 1967 *J Exp Soc Psychol* 3:1–24, doi:10.1016/0022-1031(67)90034-0 (not opened)
- GRADE A: STUDY correspondence bias (essay paradigm) — original: Jones & Harris 1967; Miyamoto & Kitayama 2002 Study 1 (49 Japanese, 58 US undergraduates: US d = 2.47, Japan d = 0.74 n.s.). Later evidence: Many Labs 2 N = 7,197, d = 1.82 overall. GRADE for the *effect*: A. — Klein et al. 2018
- [V] ★ **But the cultural difference in it did not replicate**: in Many Labs 2 the effect was equally large in WEIRD samples (median d = 1.78; pooled d = 1.81 [1.75, 1.88]) and less-WEIRD samples (median d = 1.86; pooled d = 1.84 [1.74, 1.94]) — i.e., the original "Americans show more correspondence bias than Japanese" was not borne out. GRADE C for "East Asians are less prone to the correspondence bias" in this paradigm. — Klein et al. 2018, Table 2 (full text read)
- [V] Culture and everyday explanation: Miller 1984 studied Indian (Hindu) and American adults and children aged 8, 11, 15; at older ages Americans referred more to general dispositions and less to contextual factors than Hindus did, and references to dispositions rose much more with age among Americans; the differences were not explained by cognitive, experiential or informational differences. Sample sizes not in the abstract. — Miller 1984, *JPSP* 46(5):961–978, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.961 (abstract read)
- [V] Morris & Peng 1994 tested the hypothesis that dispositionalism in attribution reflects an implicit theory more widespread in individualist than collectivist cultures (American vs Chinese attributions for social and physical events). Only the first lines of the abstract were retrievable; sample details not verified. — *JPSP* 67(6):949–971, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.67.6.949
- [V] ★ Choi, Nisbett & Norenzayan 1999 review: East Asians are *less likely to show the correspondence bias*, but the difference is **not** an absence of dispositional thinking — dispositionism is widespread across cultures; East Asians hold dispositions to be more malleable and the person as situated in a broad social context, and they avoid the bias especially "as long as situational constraints are salient". — *Psychological Bulletin* 125(1):47–63, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.125.1.47 (abstract read)
- GRADE B: STUDY culture × attribution — original: Miller 1984; Morris & Peng 1994. Later evidence: Choi et al. 1999 review supports a modest, context-dependent difference; Many Labs 2 (2018) failed to reproduce the US–Japan gap in the essay paradigm. Direction plausible, size and generality uncertain. — sources above
- [V] ★ **Actor–observer asymmetry** (Jones & Nisbett 1971: actors explain their own behavior by the situation, observers by the person) is largely not supported: Malle 2006 meta-analysis of **173 published studies** found average effect sizes between d = −0.016 and d = 0.095; the asymmetry held only for idiosyncratic actors, hypothetical events, intimates, or free-response coding, and reversed for positive events (a self-serving pattern); "across valence, no actor-observer asymmetry exists". — *Psychological Bulletin* 132(6):895–919, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.895 (abstract read)
- GRADE D: actor–observer asymmetry as a general law {Malle 2006: 173 studies, mean d ≈ 0}.
- [V] False consensus (Ross, Greene & House 1977) replicated robustly in Many Labs 2: supermarket scenario original d = 0.99 → pooled d = 1.18 [1.13, 1.23]; traffic-ticket scenario original d = 0.80 → pooled d = 0.95 [0.90, 1.00]. GRADE A. — Klein et al. 2018 (full text read)

### B1 · 阿希从众实验 Asch line-judgment conformity ★
- [V] ★ Design as Asch described it: a group of 7–9 male college students in a "visual judgment" task; one is the naive subject (seated near the end), the rest are instructed confederates ("six to eight opponents" in the 123-subject series). Cards: one standard line, three comparison lines (one equal; the wrong ones differ by ¾ to 1¾ inches); answers spoken aloud in seating order; **18 trials per series, on 12 of them ("critical trials") the confederates answer wrongly and unanimously**; confederates sometimes answer correctly on the other trials; sessions where the subject saw through the ruse were discarded. The reported series = **123 subjects from three institutions (not Swarthmore)**. — Asch 1955, *Scientific American* 193(5):31–35 (full text read: archive.org scan https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)
- [V] ★ Headline result (same source): judged alone, people err "less than 1 per cent" of the time; under the unanimous wrong majority subjects gave the majority's wrong answer on **36.8% of the critical selections**; "about one quarter" of subjects never yielded (the figure caption says 75% agreed with the majority at least in some trials). Individuals were highly consistent: independents stayed independent, yielders kept yielding. — Asch 1955 (full text)
- [~] Textbooks also print "32%" and "37%". 36.8% is the 123-subject figure in Asch 1955 (re-cited as Asch 1956 by Franzen & Mader 2023, who write "36.8% of the 123 real subjects"); "~32%" is attached in teaching sources to a smaller 50-subject series (Asch 1951). I did not open the 1951 chapter or the 1956 *Psychological Monographs* text, so quote 36.8% only together with "123 subjects", and "about a third" otherwise. — Asch 1955; Franzen & Mader 2023, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0294325
- [V] ★ **Conformity vs size of the majority — the full published series** (percent of critical trials answered with the majority's wrong line), printed in the Asch 1955 figure caption: **1 opponent 3.6% · 2 opponents 13.6% · 3 opponents 31.8% · 4 opponents 35.1% · 6 opponents 35.2% · 7 opponents 37.1% · 9 opponents 35.1% · 15 opponents 31.2%**. The text: with one opponent the subject "was swayed little"; two → 13.6%; three → 31.8%; "further increases … did not increase the weight of the pressure substantially" (the effect saturates at about 3–4). The task-file list "8, 10–15" is NOT what the caption prints (it prints 7, 9, 15; no 5 or 8). — Asch 1955 (full text)
- [V] ★ Unanimity matters more than size: with a "truthful partner" (a second naive subject, or a confederate told to answer correctly) errors fell to **one fourth** of the unanimous-majority level; a moderate dissenter cut conformity by about one third; an extremist dissenter (choosing an even worse line than the majority) cut errors to **9%**. — Asch 1955 (full text)
- [V] Partner variations: the partner answers correctly for 6 trials then joins the majority → errors jump back to about the unanimous-majority level (while supported, 18 of 27 subjects had been fully independent); the partner simply leaves → errors rise less; a majority that dissolves gradually leaves the lone subject conforming abruptly once alone. — Asch 1955 (full text)
- [V] Even with a 7-inch line difference some subjects still yielded; interviews found independents (confident, or doubtful but feeling obliged to report what they saw), yielders who doubted their own eyes, yielders who went along "not to spoil the results", and yielders who hid a felt deficiency; all yielding subjects underestimated how often they conformed. Asch's own closing stress: independence is possible and subjects valued it. — Asch 1955 (full text; paraphrase)
- [V] Meta-analysis, **Bond & Smith 1996**: 133 Asch-type line-judgment studies from 17 countries; conformity in US studies has **declined since the 1950s**; collectivism scores (3 independent surveys) were significantly related to conformity — collectivist countries showed higher conformity on average. (Franzen & Mader 2023 report Bond & Smith's average error rate over 44 strict replications as 25% — second-hand, I did not see that table.) — Bond & Smith 1996, *Psychological Bulletin* 119(1):111–137, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.1.111 (abstract read via OpenAlex)
- [V] Group-size meta-analysis, **Bond 2005**: 125 Asch-type studies; the size–conformity function differs for public vs private responses and Asch vs Crutchfield paradigms — i.e., there is no single universal curve (normative influence stronger with public face-to-face answers, informational influence with private ones). — Bond 2005, *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations* 8:331–354, doi:10.1177/1368430205056464
- [V] UK replication, **Perrin & Spencer 1981**: contemporary British student subjects (procedural replication) complied on only **1 of 396 critical trials**; but similar-to-Asch levels appeared in samples where the cost of dissent was high (youths on probation with probation officers as confederates; alienated Black youths with a white experimenter). Authors read Asch's high US 1950s rate as partly historical/cultural. — doi:10.1111/j.2044-8309.1981.tb00533.x (abstract read). [Task file lists "1980/1981"; the full paper I could confirm is 1981, *BJSP* 20:205–209.]
- [V] **Nicholson, Cole & Rocklin 1985**: British and US university students did not differ; error responses were significantly fewer than Asch's 1951–56 rates but significantly above zero — "the Asch effect has weakened … it remains an observable minority behaviour". — *BJSP* 24:59–63, doi:10.1111/j.2044-8309.1985.tb00660.x (abstract read)
- [V] **Mori & Arai 2010** (no confederates; polarizing-filter "fMORI" trick so one of four participants sees different lines): 104 Japanese undergraduates (40 men, 64 women), same-sex groups of four; women conformed, men did not; conformity was about the same whether the majority was unanimous or not (unlike Asch). — *Int J Psychology* 45:390–397, doi:10.1080/00207591003774485 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Franzen & Mader 2023** (the "N = 210" recent replication — dated 2023, not 2018): 210 students recruited on the University of Bern campus (61% female, mean age 22.6), fieldwork March–April 2021, 5 confederates + 1 naive subject, 6 critical trials; **error rate 33%** in the standard line task (matches Asch's 1951–56 values); 25% when correct answers were paid; 38% conformity on political-opinion items; of personality traits only openness related to conformity. Other recent replications they cite: Takano & Sogon (Japan, 40 male students, 6–9 confederates: 25%); Usto et al. (Bosnia-Herzegovina, 95 students, 5 confederates: 35%). — *PLoS ONE* 18(11):e0294325, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0294325 (full text read)
- GRADE B: STUDY Asch line conformity — original: 123 men, 36.8% of critical answers wrong (1955). Later evidence: Bond & Smith 1996 (133 studies, 17 countries; lower and variable rates, collectivism and era matter), Perrin & Spencer 1981 (UK ~0.3%), Nicholson 1985, Franzen & Mader 2023 (33%, N=210), Mori & Arai 2010. Direction replicates in several countries, but size depends on era, culture, task, group composition. — sources above
- [def] 信息性影响 informational influence (treating others' judgments as evidence about reality) vs 规范性影响 normative influence (going along to be accepted); Deutsch & Gerard 1955 introduced the split and ran Asch variants (face-to-face, anonymous, group-with-commitment). — *J Abnormal & Social Psychology* 51:629–636, doi:10.1037/h0046408 (abstract only; the numbers inside were not available to me)
- [def] Sherif 1935 autokinetic effect: judgments of a physically stationary light's "movement" converge in groups (an informational-influence paradigm with no objectively correct answer). — Sherif 1935, *Archives of Psychology* 27(187) (classic; not opened)
- [V] Berns et al. 2005 fMRI mental-rotation conformity: conformity was associated with changes in an occipital–parietal network, independence with amygdala and caudate activity; the authors read this as evidence that conformity can involve perception, not only decision. Small-sample fMRI, no independent replication found by me → treat as {C}. — *Biological Psychiatry* 58:245–253, doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2005.04.012 (abstract read; N and conformity rate NOT confirmed)
- [V] Gender: in a review of influenceability, Eagly's (1978) review of influenceability found that women were more susceptible than men in only one domain, Asch-type group-pressure studies, and even there only 34% of studies showed women significantly more conforming (reported via Blass 1999); Mori & Arai 2010 found women but not men conformed; no consistent sex difference should be taught as a rule. — Blass 1999 (full text), doi:10.1111/j.1559-1816.1999.tb00134.x

### B2 · 米尔格拉姆服从实验 Milgram obedience studies ★
- [V] ★ Design (Milgram 1963): **40 men aged 20–50**, New Haven area, recruited by newspaper ad and direct mail for a "study of memory and learning at Yale"; paid $4.50 "for coming" whatever happened; teacher/learner assigned by rigged draw; the learner (a trained confederate) is strapped in an adjacent room; a simulated generator has **30 switches, 15 to 450 V** (labels "Slight Shock" to "Danger: Severe Shock"); at Level 20 (300 V) the victim pounds on the wall and stops answering; the experimenter uses standardized prods. — Milgram 1963, *J Abnormal & Social Psychology* 67(4):371–378, doi:10.1037/h0040525 (full text read; PDF at psy.miami.edu)
- [V] ★ Result: **26 of 40 (65%) went all the way to 450 V; 14 (35%) broke off** — but **all 40 went at least to 300 V**: 5 stopped right after 300 V, 4 after one more shock (315), 2 at 330, 1 each at 345, 360 and 375 V. 14-point scale "how painful were the last shocks?": modal answer 14 ("extremely painful"), mean 13.42. 14 of 40 showed definite nervous laughter/smiling; 3 had full-blown uncontrollable seizures; one session was halted for a violent seizure. 43 unpaid Yale undergraduates (footnote) gave "very similar" results. — Milgram 1963 (full text)
- [V] ★ **What people predicted (the "~1%" claim, checked)**: (a) in the 1963 paper, **14 Yale senior psychology majors** estimated how many of 100 hypothetical adults would reach 450 V: range **0–3%, class mean 1.2%**. (b) In the 1974 book (ch. 3), psychiatrists (the table lists n = 38 psychiatrists; the text says 110 respondents in all with 31 college students and 40 middle-class adults), graduate students/faculty, sophomores and adults all predicted for "one hundred Americans" that only "a pathological fringe, not exceeding one or two per cent" would go to the end; **the psychiatrists' distribution: most would stop by level 10 (150 V), about 4% would reach level 20 (300 V), "about one subject in a thousand" would give 450 V**. (c) Blass 1999 gives the psychiatrists' figure as 0.125% and quotes Milgram's 24 Sept 1962 letter to E. P. Hollander (40 Yale psychiatrists "wrong by a factor of 500"). — Milgram 1963; Milgram 1974 *Obedience to Authority* ch. 3 (read in an archive.org OCR scan, tables checked against known values); Blass 1999 (full text), doi:10.1111/j.1559-1816.1999.tb00134.x
- [✗] "Psychiatrists predicted ~1% would go to the end." Psychiatrists predicted about **0.1%** (one in a thousand; Blass: 0.125%); ~1% (1.2%) is the *Yale undergraduates'* mean; all groups said "not exceeding one or two per cent". (When asked about *themselves*, 100% of the 110 respondents said they would stop; mean maximum shock they predicted was level 8.20 for psychiatrists, 9.35 for students, 9.15 for adults, out of 30.) — Milgram 1974 ch. 3
- [V] Later prediction studies are less extreme: told only the procedure, participants predicted full obedience of 33.5% (naive women) to 44.3% (naive men) in Mixon 1971; 27% in Kaufmann & Kooman 1967; 23.9% in Canadian officer candidates (Guimond et al. 1994) — still below 65%, but not "1%". — Blass 1999 (full text)
- [V] ★ **The variations (Milgram 1974; n = 40 per condition unless marked)** — percent going to 450 V: **Exp 1 Remote victim (in next room, silent) 26/40 = 65.0%** (mean max level 27.0) · **Exp 2 Voice-feedback 25/40 = 62.5%** · **Exp 3 Proximity (same room, a few feet) 16/40 = 40.0%** · **Exp 4 Touch-proximity (must force the victim's hand on the plate) 12/40 = 30.0%** · **Exp 5 "new baseline" (Voice-feedback plus remarks about a heart problem; moved to a plain basement lab after the Yale Interaction Laboratory) 26/40 = 65.0%** · Exp 6 Change of personnel 20/40 = 50.0% · **Exp 7 Experimenter absent (orders by telephone) 9/40** [arithmetic 22.5%; the printed table reads "20.5%" in the scan I read, and many secondary tables repeat 20.5% — say "about one in five"; subjects who stayed also sometimes gave weaker shocks than ordered and said they obeyed] · Exp 8 Women as subjects 26/40 = 65.0% · Exp 9 Victim's limited contract (learner agrees to take part only on condition he is released when he says so; the experimenter ignores this at 150 V) 16/40 = 40.0% · Exp 10 Institutional context (run-down Bridgeport office, no Yale) 19/40 = 47.5% (book text: "48 percent … versus 65 percent … at Yale") · **Exp 11 Subject free to choose any shock level on any trial: 1/40 = 2.5% used 450 V; overall mean shock level 3.6 of 30** · Exp 12 Learner demands to be shocked, experimenter forbids: 0 (none complied with the learner) · Exp 13 Ordinary man gives the orders (n = 20): 16 of 20 broke with him → 4/20 = 20% · Exp 14 Authority as victim, ordinary man commands (n = 20): all stopped at the experimenter's first protest = 0% · Exp 15 Two authorities, contradictory commands (n = 20): 1 broke off before the disagreement, 18 stopped at the disagreement, 1 one step later → 0 reached 450 V · Exp 16 Two authorities, one as victim: subjects treated him like any ordinary victim (percent not in the scan; secondary tables say 65% → [~]) · **Exp 17 Two peers rebel (confederate teacher 1 quits at the 150-V protest, teacher 2 at 210 V; the naive subject is teacher 3 and is left alone at the generator) 4/40 = 10.0%** (36 of 40 defied vs 14 in the baseline) · **Exp 18 A peer administers the shocks (subject does a subtask) 37/40 = 92.5%**. — Milgram 1974 Tables 2–5 and text (OCR scan read; percentages recomputed from counts)
- [V] Haslam & Reicher: Milgram ran "30 or so" variants in all; the share going to 450 V ranged from 0% to nearly 100% and, **across the studies as a whole, a majority of participants did not go that far**; the "baseline" is not typical. When the experimenter gave a *direct order* ("You have no other choice, you must go on") participants typically refused; the science-justified prod ("The experiment requires that you continue") worked. — Haslam & Reicher 2012, *PLoS Biology* 10(11):e1001426, doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1001426 (full text read, OA)
- [V] Cross-national and over-time replications (Blass 1999 review of Milgram-type studies 1963–1985): full-obedience rates in standard conditions ranged **from 28% (Kilham & Mann 1974, Australia) to 91% (Ring et al. 1970)**; the Spearman correlation between year of study and obedience rate was **rs = .002** (14 methodological replications + Milgram's baseline-type conditions; −.008 when proximity studies were added, 24 conditions) → no sign of a trend over 22 years and no support for an "enlightenment effect"; 8 of 9 replications with both sexes found no sex difference (exception: Kilham & Mann, men 40% vs women 16%). — Blass 1999 (full text)
- [V] ★ **Burger 2009** partial replication (ethical-board approved; stops at the 150-V switch, where Milgram's subjects first heard the learner protest; two-step clinical screening; $50): **70 adults** (29 men, 41 women; ages 20–81, mean 42.9); base condition (n = 40): **28 (70.0%) continued past 150 V** vs 12 (30.0%) who stopped; Milgram's comparable Exp 5: 33/40 = 82.5% — difference not significant (χ²(1, N = 80) = 1.10, p = .29); modeled-refusal condition (n = 30): 19 (63.3%) continued, not different from base; base-condition men 66.7% vs women 72.7%. The 150-V stopping rule rests on Milgram's data: 79% of those who passed 150 V went to 450 V. — Burger 2009, *American Psychologist* 64(1):1–11, doi:10.1037/a0010932 (full text read)
- [~] **Doliński et al. 2017** (Poland, 2015; Burger-style truncated procedure): 80 participants (40 men, 40 women, ages 18–69), about **90%** went through to the highest level of the shortened scale; the authors call the obedience "similarly high" to Milgram's; sex of the learner effects "do not allow us to unequivocally accept or reject" the hypotheses. Details beyond the abstract come from the authors' press release (SPSP), not the paper. — *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 8(8):927–933, doi:10.1177/1948550617693060 (abstract read)
- [V] **A 2025 variation on the "teacher" role (truncated procedure, stop rule at the 150-V switch)**: in the "examiner" condition (the participant only checks the learner's answers) **4 of 40 (10%) refused to press all ten switches, 90% reaching 150 V**; in conditions where participants first taught the learner, **9 of 40 (22.5%) refused, 77.5% reaching 150 V** — a difference the authors report as **not statistically significant**; experimenter prompts were more frequent when participants had first taught the learner (median 0.5 vs 0; this difference significant). Small, single-lab, ethics-truncated: a hint that role engagement moderates compliance, not a result to build on. — Grzyb & Doliński 2025, *Frontiers in Psychology* 16:1613507, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1613507 (abstract read)
- [V] Packer 2008 meta-analysis of 8 of Milgram's experiments: in every study **disobedience peaked at 150 V** (the learner's first request to be released); across studies obedience rates covaried with disobedience at 150 V but at no other point; disobedience was not related to the learner's escalating pain. — *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 3(4):301–304, doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00080.x (abstract read)
- [V] **Belief matters**: Perry, Brannigan, Wanner & Stam 2020 re-analysed an unpublished study by Milgram's assistant Taketo Murata: in **18 of 23 variations**, mean shock levels were *lower* among subjects who fully believed they were hurting the learner than among those who did not; i.e., skepticism inflated obedience and perceived pain inflated defiance. — *Social Psychology Quarterly* 83(1):88–106, doi:10.1177/0190272519861952 (abstract read). [Perry 2012/2013 *Behind the Shock Machine* reports that "only half" fully believed and that of those about two thirds disobeyed — book and Wikipedia-relayed; I did not open the book → [~].]
- [V] Alternative reading, "engaged followership": participants do not obey as automata; they identify with the scientific enterprise (Haslam, Reicher & Millard & McDonald 2015: the Yale archive shows many participants were "happy to have been of service"; Reicher, Haslam & Smith 2012: the willingness to give 450 V changes predictably with identification with the experimenter vs the learner). — *BJSP* 54:55–83, doi:10.1111/bjso.12074; *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 7:315–324, doi:10.1177/1745691612448482 (abstracts read)
- GRADE B: STUDY Milgram obedience — original: 40 men, 65% to 450 V (1963); 18 variations (1974). Later evidence: Blass 1999 (rates 28–91% across ~24 conditions; no time trend), Burger 2009 (70% past 150 V vs 82.5%, N = 70), Doliński 2017 (≈90% on a truncated scale, N = 80). A majority of ordinary adults will continue to harm a protesting stranger under insistent, polite, scientific-authority pressure — but the rate swings from 0% to 93% with the situation, and belief in the setup matters (Perry 2020).
- GRADE C: the interpretation "blind obedience" / "Nazi-style banality of evil" — the "agentic state" reading is disputed by identification-based accounts (Haslam & Reicher; Reicher, Haslam & Smith 2012), by Packer 2008 (systematic disobedience at 150 V) and by Perry 2020 (belief). Teach the behavior, hedge the explanation.
- [V] Sociological caveat for lesson use: nearly all Milgram data are from men (Exp 8 is the only all-female condition, n = 40), from the US northeast in 1961–62, paid volunteers; replications with ethics-limited truncated procedures (Burger, Doliński) test the 150-V decision point, not 450 V. — Milgram 1963; Burger 2009; Blass 1999

### B3 · 斯坦福监狱实验 Stanford prison experiment (SPE) ★
- [V] ★ Original design (Haney, Banks & Zimbardo 1973, *Int J Criminology & Penology* 1:69–97; read from a scanned PDF via OCR): a mock prison in the basement of Stanford's psychology building; newspaper ad for a "psychological study of prison life" at **$15 a day for up to two weeks**; **75 respondents** completed questionnaires/interviews; **24** judged most stable/mature/least anti-social were selected (the paper's Subjects paragraph says "the 21 subjects who participated"; Le Texier counts 11 guards and 11 prisoners — the counts in the sources do not match, so quote "24 selected from 75"); roles assigned at random; prisoners lived in the prison 24 hours a day, three to a cell (others on stand-by); guards worked three-man 8-hour shifts and went home. Terminated "prematurely after only six days". — doi not assigned (journal pre-DOI era); Haney, Banks & Zimbardo 1973 (OCR of Stanford-hosted scan https://www.prisonexp.org/pdf/ijcp1973.pdf)
- [V] What the 1973 paper itself reports: **five prisoners** were released early — four with acute distress beginning as early as day 2, the fifth after treatment for a psychosomatic rash; the abstract says half the prisoners reacted with acute emotional disturbance "severe enough to warrant early release" and that "at least a third" of the guards became far more aggressive than expected; the text adds that not all guards were hostile: some were tough but fair, some went far beyond their roles into inventive cruelty, a few were passive. The abstract also says neither group received specific training for the roles. The paper admits selective sampling, incomplete data per measure and a small N. — Haney, Banks & Zimbardo 1973 (OCR)
- [V] ★ **Le Texier 2019** (first systematic reading of the SPE archives: 16 boxes at Stanford + 1 box at Akron, hundreds of documents, 6 hours of film, 48 hours of audio, plus phone interviews with 15 participants, 2017): the paper argues that (1) the SPE borrowed its rules, daily schedule and procedures from a **student mock-prison exercise at Toyon Hall, 15–16 May 1971**, run by an undergraduate who then served as "warden" (11 of the SPE's 17 rules were copied from it, the other 6 adapted); (2) the **guards were briefed on the goal and told what to do** at the orientation on Saturday 14 August 1971 (Zimbardo told them the aim was to create boredom, frustration, fear, arbitrariness, loss of privacy and powerlessness, and that "we have total power in the situation"), were handed a suggested daily schedule, and were pushed to act tough (on day 3 the warden told a soft guard that every guard had to be a "tough guard"); (3) the guards were **not told they were subjects**; (4) prisoners were meant not to be able to quit: the protocol Zimbardo submitted on 31 July 1971 said they would be led to believe they could not leave except for emergencies and would be discouraged from quitting; (5) participants were mostly aware it was an experiment; (6) data were incomplete (**6 h of video + 15 h of audio ≈ 21 of ~150 h, under 15%**; no data on day 3; the video was filmed for its drama, as the project's own Haney memo admits; Zimbardo says 12 h of video were shot but only 6 h survive in the archive); (7) conclusions about prison reform were prepared in advance (press releases on days 2 and 6). — Le Texier 2019, *American Psychologist* 74(7):823–839, doi:10.1037/amp0000401 (full text read)
- [V] The "breakdown": the best-known prisoner (8612-1) planned to act mad to get out — in a debriefing recording on the last day he says that, after being refused release, he chose to act mad or upset as the least harmful way out, built up his distress in the solitary "Hole" and let it out in front of the warden so he would be released; Zimbardo's 2018 online reply says the breakdown was real and that the participant's story has changed. Le Texier counts 5 early releases in all and concludes that of the 11 prisoners only two really exhibited symptoms of a breakdown, so "half of them … released prematurely for psychological disturbance" (Zimbardo 1978) is exaggerated. — Le Texier 2019 (full text); contested by Zimbardo 2018 (reply on prisonexp.org, not opened in full)
- [V] Demand characteristics: Banuazizi & Movahedi 1975 gave **150 college students** only a description of the SPE procedure and the recruitment ad; **81%** worked out the experimenters' hypothesis and **90%** predicted the guards would be "oppressive, hostile, aggressive, humiliating". The SPE's guards also had pay incentives (paid at the end, so staying in the study paid) and several said afterwards they were acting (Guard 4, the most abusive night-shift guard, told the warden at the debriefing that he was an actor the whole time and was "hamming it up"). — *American Psychologist* 30(2):152–160, doi:10.1037/h0076835 (as reported by Le Texier 2019, full text read)
- [V] Self-selection: students answering the SPE-style "psychological study of prison life" ad scored significantly higher than controls (identical ad minus the words "prison life") on aggressiveness, authoritarianism, Machiavellianism, narcissism and social dominance and lower on empathy and altruism — so the SPE sample may not have been "typical". Authors conclude person–situation interaction, not strict situationism. — Carnahan & McFarland 2007, *Personality & Social Psychology Bulletin* 33(5):603–614, doi:10.1177/0146167206292689 (abstract read)
- [V] The BBC Prison Study: a filmed "experimental case study" in which men were randomly divided into prisoners and guards in a purpose-built institution **for 8 days**; unlike the SPE the researchers took no leadership role; the **guards failed to identify with their role**, were reluctant to use authority and were overcome by the prisoners; an egalitarian system followed, then proved unsustainable and later a tyrannical regime was proposed. Authors: tyranny follows from the failure of groups and powerlessness, not from role assignment alone. — Reicher & Haslam 2006, *BJSP* 45:1–40, doi:10.1348/014466605x48998 (abstract read; participant N not in abstract)
- [V] Haslam, Reicher & Van Bavel 2019, reading the SPE archive with the identity-leadership framework, report comprehensive evidence that experimenters directly encouraged guards to adopt roles and act tough, rather than guards conforming to the role on their own. — *American Psychologist* 74(7):809–822, doi:10.1037/amp0000443 (abstract read, OA)
- [~] **Retraction debate (as of March 2025; opinion, not a finding)**: a guest post by sociologist Augustine Brannigan on Retraction Watch (10 March 2025), summarized in *Nature*'s Briefing of 13 March 2025, asks "should the study be retracted?" on the strength of Le Texier's archival work (his book: French 2018, English translation 2024, *Investigating the Stanford Prison Experiment: History of a Lie*). Brannigan's own answer: for a classic this heavily criticized "official retraction, while desirable, becomes redundant"; the journal that published the 1973 paper ended publication in 1978, so "a retraction in the IJCP is not even possible", and "arguably the best outcome we can expect is more responsible reporting in contemporary textbooks". No source I opened reports a retraction notice. — https://retractionwatch.com/2025/03/10/guest-post-should-zimbardos-stanford-prison-experiment-be-retracted/ (full text read); Nature Briefing, doi:10.1038/d41586-025-00815-8 (read)
- GRADE D: STUDY SPE — original: 24 men selected (21 participated), 6 days, 1971; "ordinary people placed in a guard role automatically become cruel". Later evidence: Le Texier 2019 (archives: coached guards, unrealistic situation, incomplete data), Haslam, Reicher & Van Bavel 2019, Banuazizi & Movahedi 1975 (demand characteristics), Carnahan & McFarland 2007 (self-selection), BBC Prison Study 2006 (no automatic role conformity). No independent replication reproduces the 1971 outcome. Teach as a caution about demonstrations and demand characteristics, not as evidence for "the situation makes monsters".
- GRADE C: the weaker claim that *leaders who set a toxic goal and legitimize cruelty can induce it in ordinary people* (identity-leadership account) — supported by archival reading and by Milgram-variation data, but archival re-analyses are interpretive.
- [✗] "Guards spontaneously invented the abuses, with no instructions" — the archive shows a briefing, a script drawn from Toyon Hall, a coaching of soft guards and a prisoner-quitting rule (Le Texier 2019). [✗] "All guards became sadistic" — even the 1973 paper says a third at most and describes tough-but-fair and passive guards. [✗] "Half the prisoners had breakdowns" — five were released early (four distress, one rash), and Le Texier argues only two were genuine.

### B4 · 旁观者效应 bystander effect & the Kitty Genovese story ★
- [V] ★ **What actually happened to Kitty Genovese** (Manning, Levine & Collins 2007): murdered early on **13 March 1964** in Kew Gardens, Queens; the "38 witnesses" story was built by journalists Martin Gansberg and A. M. Rosenthal — Gansberg's front-page *New York Times* article of **27 March 1964** ("37 Who Saw Murder Didn't Call the Police") said 38 respectable citizens watched a killer stalk and stab a woman in **three** attacks for more than half an hour and none phoned the police; Rosenthal's 1964 book *Thirty-Eight Witnesses* followed. The trial record of Winston Moseley shows **five** apartment residents testified (Mozer, Picq, Frost, Koshkin, Farrar), of whom **three** saw Genovese and Moseley together; none saw the stabbing; one (Mozer) shouted at Moseley and scared him off after the first attack; there were **two** attacks, not three, and the second took place inside a stairwell no trial witness could see; assistant DA Charles Skoller said investigators "only found about half a dozen that saw what was going on, that we could use" (quoted from Rasenberger 2004); **no list of the 38 has ever been produced**; a former police officer's affidavit and 1995 press reports say calls to police were made, and a friend (Sophie Farrar) phoned the police and went to Genovese's side. All **10 popular textbooks** the authors checked told the 38-witness version. — Manning, Levine & Collins 2007, *American Psychologist* 62(6):555–562, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.62.6.555 (full text of the author manuscript read)
- [~] The *New York Times* itself corrected the story in Winston Moseley's obituary (R. D. McFadden, April 2016): it calls the "38 witnesses" account erroneous and a gross exaggeration of the number of witnesses and what they perceived — none saw the attack in its entirety; two attacks, not three; two people did call the police afterwards. I could not open nytimes.com; wording relayed by secondary summaries. — NYT obituary, 4–5 April 2016
- [V] Manning et al. separate the robust lab tradition from the news story: the 38-witness story "prompted" Latané & Darley's research, but the experiments do not depend on it; the story itself became a "parable" of group apathy and shaped textbooks. — Manning et al. 2007 (full text)
- [V] ★ **Darley & Latané 1968** (epileptic-seizure study): students in separate cubicles discussing personal problems over an intercom overheard a "fellow student" have a seizure; they believed they were alone with the victim, or that **1 or 4** others also heard; the presence of others lowered the speed of reporting (p < .01); sex of the bystander did not matter; personality/background measures did not predict helping; authors suggest the explanation lies "more in the bystander's response to other observers than in his indifference to the victim". — *JPSP* 8(4, Pt.1):377–383, doi:10.1037/h0025589 (abstract read)
- [~] ★ The usual numbers for that study (2-person / 3-person / 6-person groups): **85% / 62% / 31%** reported the seizure before the victim's voice was cut off; mean response times about **52 s / 93 s (some sources say 96 s) / 166 s**. These come from teaching summaries — the original table was not available to me, so quote them as "about" and flag in the lesson as 2nd-hand. — Darley & Latané 1968, via secondary summaries
- [V] ★ **Latané & Nida 1981** review of the decade after the seminal studies: "the social inhibition of helping is a remarkably consistent phenomenon", but boundary conditions can weaken or eliminate it. — *Psychological Bulletin* 89(2):308–324, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.89.2.308 (abstract read)
- [~] Counts commonly attached to it: 56 studies, ≥ 6,000 participants, people alone helped more in 48 of 56 comparisons, help rate about 75% alone vs under 53% in groups (FORRT open textbook; I did not see the original table). — Latané & Nida 1981 via https://forrt.org/open-social-psychology/chapter13.html
- [V] Field experiment: in the New York subway (8th Avenue Independent line, 4 teams of student victims, models and observers), an apparently ill person was helped more than an apparently drunk one, and the expected slower helping with more bystanders ("diffusion of responsibility") **did not occur** in that real setting. — Piliavin, Rodin & Piliavin 1969, *JPSP* 13(4):289–299, doi:10.1037/h0028433 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Fischer et al. 2011 meta-analysis**: data from **over 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect sizes** → overall **g = −0.35** (fixed-effects; presence of others reduces helping). The effect was *attenuated* when the situation was perceived as dangerous (vs non-dangerous), when the perpetrator was present, and when intervention costs were physical; the authors propose an arousal–cost–reward account and also found "non-negative bystander effects" in certain dangerous emergencies; the effect was reduced when bystanders were exclusively male, were naive rather than passive confederates, or were only virtually present, and when bystanders were not strangers. (A secondary summary says the 105 effect sizes come from 53 studies — I did not see the full text.) — Fischer, Krueger, Greitemeyer, Vogrincic, Kastenmüller, Frey, Heene, Wicher & Kainbacher 2011, *Psychological Bulletin* 137(4):517–537, doi:10.1037/a0023304 (abstract read)
- [V] Group identity changes the sign: in four experiments (street-violence scenario; a real-life measure in Study 4) increasing group size **inhibited** intervention among strangers but **encouraged** it among friends or shared-category members (e.g., women helping a female victim); "the bystander effect is not a generic consequence of increasing group size". — Levine & Crowther 2008, *JPSP* 95(6):1429–1439, doi:10.1037/a0012634 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Philpot, Liebst, Levine, Bernasco & Lindegaard 2020** — the largest real-world study: **219 CCTV clips of aggressive public conflicts** (Amsterdam n = 63, Cape Town n = 61, Lancaster n = 95; inner-city entertainment areas; raw pool 1,225 clips; clips where police/paramedics were present at the start excluded). **At least one bystander intervened in 90.9% of conflicts** (mean 3.76 interveners per clip, SD 3.01; mean 16.29 bystanders present, SD 13.16; mean conflict duration 3.27 min); no national difference (Dutch OR = 0.61, UK OR = 1.59 vs South Africa; BF01 = 17.27 for "no association"); each extra bystander raised the odds that someone intervenes (**OR = 1.10 per bystander, 95% CI 1.03–1.18**; standardized OR = 12.72, CI 1.96–82.50 — wide). Intervention = pacifying gestures, calming touches, blocking, pulling an aggressor away, consoling or practically helping the victim; coder reliability Krippendorff α = .85 (number of attentive bystanders) and .87 (number of interveners). Older estimates quoted in the paper: 10.8% (Felson & Steadman 1983 case files), 73.8% (Heinskou & Liebst 2017 police files), 26.2–39.5% (barroom observations). — *American Psychologist* 75(1):66–75, doi:10.1037/amp0000469 (accepted manuscript read, OSF postprint https://osf.io/nqscj)
- [V] Follow-ups with the same method: Liebst et al. 2021 (93 CCTV recordings; 417 interveners and 636 non-interveners): interveners were physically harmed in **3.6%**, non-interveners virtually never; Lindegaard et al. 2022 (80 conflicts): danger present → bystanders **19×** more likely to intervene, but graded danger level unrelated; Levine, Taylor & Best 2011 (42 CCTV incidents, 312 people): third parties were more often conciliatory than escalating, and more so as group size rose. — *Psychology of Violence* 11(1):11–18, doi:10.1037/vio0000299; *SPPS* 13(4):795–802, doi:10.1177/19485506211042683; *Psychological Science* 22(3):406–412, doi:10.1177/0956797611398495 (abstracts read)
- GRADE B: STUDY bystander effect (individual-level inhibition) — original: Darley & Latané 1968 (lab, intercom; 85/62/31% reporting). Later evidence: Latané & Nida 1981 (consistent across ~56 comparisons), Fischer et al. 2011 (g = −0.35 overall, attenuated or absent when dangerous, perpetrator present, physical costs, friends or a shared group), Piliavin 1969 (no effect in a subway field test). Reliable in non-dangerous, ambiguous situations with strangers; boundary conditions are real.
- GRADE C: "in an emergency in public, nobody helps" (generalizing the lab effect or the Genovese story to real violence) — the best naturalistic evidence (Philpot 2020: ~9 in 10 conflicts get help; more people present → more likely someone helps) points the other way, and the Genovese facts were wrong. Philpot et al. measure whether SOMEONE intervenes, not each person's likelihood; both can be true (Latané himself noted the "mechanical potential" of more potential helpers).
- [✗] "38 people watched for 30 minutes and did nothing" — see Manning et al. 2007 above. [✗] "The bystander effect means people are apathetic/cruel" — Darley & Latané argued the opposite (responsibility diffusion and ambiguity, not indifference).

### C · 偏见、群体与道德 prejudice, groups & moral psychology (best-evidenced items only)

#### C1 · 群际接触 intergroup contact
- [def] 接触假说 contact hypothesis (Allport 1954, *The Nature of Prejudice*, Addison-Wesley): contact between groups can reduce prejudice when it involves **equal status, common goals, intergroup cooperation and support of authorities, law or custom** (the four conditions as listed by Paluck et al. 2019 from Pettigrew & Tropp 2006 p. 752). — Allport 1954 (book, not opened); Paluck, Green & Green 2019, doi:10.1017/bpp.2018.25 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Pettigrew & Tropp 2006**: **713 independent samples from 515 studies**; contact "typically reduces intergroup prejudice"; the result appears not to come from participant selection or publication bias; more rigorous studies give *larger* effects; effects generalize to the whole outgroup and to non-ethnic targets; Allport's conditions help but "are not essential". — *JPSP* 90(5):751–783, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.90.5.751 (abstract read)
- [V] Numbers from the authors' own later summary: the 515 studies involve "a quarter of a million participants in 38 nations"; mean effect size **r = .21 in magnitude (higher contact ↔ lower prejudice)**; **94%** of studies show an inverse contact–prejudice relationship; the more rigorous experimental studies average **r = .33**. (The minus sign is lost in the PDF text I extracted; the direction is stated in words.) — Pettigrew & Tropp 2008, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 38:922–934, doi:10.1002/ejsp.504 (full text read). The exact N = 250,493 sometimes quoted was NOT found by me → write "about a quarter of a million".
- [V] How contact works (same 2008 meta-analysis): reduced intergroup anxiety (60 samples, 13,343 people) and increased empathy/perspective taking (14 samples, 2,362 people) mediate more strongly than increased knowledge about the outgroup (17 samples, 2,543 people). — Pettigrew & Tropp 2008 (full text read; Table 1, random-effects estimates). The same paper's introduction, citing other work (Tropp & Pettigrew 2005; Tropp 2007), says contact effects are significantly stronger for majority-group than for minority-status-group members and for affective than for cognitive (stereotype) measures → [~] second-hand.
- [V] ★ **Paluck, Green & Green 2019** (what the *randomized* evidence says): only **27 experimental comparisons** with random assignment and delayed outcomes (about two thirds published after 2006); 24 of 27 show positive effects, average **0.39 SD** reduction in measured prejudice; but for **ethnic/racial/religious/immigrant targets the pooled effect is 0.25 SD (95% CI 0.08–0.42)** and four of the five largest studies fall below it; contact works best for disability-related prejudice; **no randomized study of ethnic or racial contact includes adults over 25**; Allport's conditions are almost untested in experiments. — *Behavioural Public Policy* 3(2):129–158, doi:10.1017/bpp.2018.25 (full text read, OA)
- [V] Prejudice-reduction field in general: 418 experiments in 309 manuscripts (2007–2019); **76%** test "light-touch" interventions of unknown durability; the commonest intervention is perspective-taking; signs of publication bias that "may exaggerate effects"; the landmark studies "often find limited effects"; the authors conclude much of this work is ill-suited to give actionable advice. — Paluck, Porat, Clark & Green 2021, *Annual Review of Psychology* 72:533–560, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-071620-030619 (abstract read)
- [V] **2023–24 additions**: (a) a pre-registered meta-analysis of the "ironic" effect — **98 studies, 140 samples, 213,085 disadvantaged-group members** — found that contact was on average associated with *less* perceived injustice (**r = −.07**), collective action (**r = −.06**) and support for reparative policies (**r = −.07**), but "small, variable, and consistent with alternative explanations", with **25%–36% of studies finding positive associations** — Reimer & Sengupta 2023, *JPSP* 124(2):362–380, doi:10.1037/pspi0000404; (b) a natural experiment of random college roommate assignment: the policy raised the chance of a cross-race roommate, and a cross-race roommate was associated with more racial-outgroup friends and more positive verbal and nonverbal behavior in a cross-race interaction, with no difference in relationship quality (sample sizes not in the abstract) — Albuja, Gaither, Sanchez & Nixon 2024, *JPSP* 127(2):277–290, doi:10.1037/pspa0000393; (c) a field experiment with farmer and pastoralist communities in Nigeria: communities that received the contact program showed more positive intergroup attitudes, more feelings of physical security and more voluntary intergroup contact in observed market behavior; exploratory analyses suggest the spread to the wider community was "likely due to norm change" — Grady, Wolfe, Dawop & Inks 2023, *PNAS* 120(43):e2304882120, doi:10.1073/pnas.2304882120 (abstracts read).
- GRADE B: STUDY intergroup contact reduces prejudice — original: Allport 1954 (theory) / early desegregation studies. Later evidence: Pettigrew & Tropp 2006 (515 studies, r ≈ −.21, mostly correlational; rigorous studies larger), Paluck et al. 2019 (27 randomized, d ≈ 0.39 overall but 0.25 for racial/ethnic and no adult data). Direction reliable; size, durability and adult-racial-contact evidence limited.

#### C2 · 内隐联想测验 Implicit Association Test (IAT) and "implicit bias"
- [def] IAT: a speeded two-category sorting task — performance is faster when highly associated categories share a response key (flower + pleasant) than when less associated categories do (insect + pleasant); the difference "implicitly measures differential association". The 1998 paper reports 3 experiments (flower/insect vs pleasant/unpleasant; Japanese vs Korean names in Japanese and Korean participants; Black vs White names in self-described unprejudiced White participants, who nonetheless showed the association). — Greenwald, McGhee & Schwartz 1998, *JPSP* 74(6):1464–1480, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.74.6.1464 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Stability of IAT scores**: two longitudinal studies, three domains (self-concept, racial attitudes, political attitudes) — implicit measures were significantly less stable over time (**weighted average r = .53 after the authors' 2019 corrigendum**; the 2017 abstract printed .54 — do not quote .54) than the matching explicit measures (**r = .75**), despite comparable internal consistency. CORRECTION (lead, 2026-10-14; read in full on the publisher page): Corrigendum, *PSPB* 45(10), published online 11 March 2019, doi:10.1177/0146167219832334: the SPSS syntax for aggregating the IAT data in Studies 1a and 2b deviated from the Greenwald, Nosek & Banaji (2003) D-algorithm (means/SDs computed from correct trials only; 100% valid trials presupposed in each combined block; a typo omitted the 600-ms error penalty); corrected weighted average stability of implicit measures r = .53 (instead of .54), of implicit measures of racial attitudes r = .39 (instead of .41); the explicit-measure figure (.75) is not mentioned in the corrigendum. — Gawronski, Morrison, Phills & Galdi 2017, *PSPB* 43(3):300–312, doi:10.1177/0146167216684131 (abstract read). Widely repeated "test–retest ≈ .5" for the IAT is consistent with this but is not an IAT-only meta-analytic figure → [~].
- [V] ★ **Does the IAT predict discrimination?** Two meta-analyses, two answers: Oswald et al. 2013 (criterion studies of ethnic/racial discrimination): average IAT–criterion correlation **ρ = .14 overall (.15 race, .12 ethnicity)**, "poor predictors of every criterion category other than brain activity", and no better than simple explicit measures (explicit ρ = .12 overall, .10 race, .15 ethnicity); Greenwald, Poehlman, Uhlmann & Banaji 2009 (9 criterion domains; included only correlations the authors expected to be positive): **r = .236** for Black–White IATs vs **r = .117** for self-report behavior predictors. Greenwald, Banaji & Nosek 2015 attribute the gap mainly to different inclusion policies (Oswald et al. kept a substantial minority of correlations with no theoretical expectation of a relation) and argue that even r ≈ .15–.24 can matter at the scale of populations. — Oswald et al. 2013, *JPSP* 105(2):171–192, doi:10.1037/a0032734 (full text read); Greenwald, Banaji & Nosek 2015, *JPSP* 108(4):553–561, doi:10.1037/pspa0000016 (full text read)
- [V] Largest synthesis (217 research reports, N = 36,071): significant implicit– and explicit–criterion correlations; unique contributions in SEM **β = .14 (implicit)** and **β = .11 (explicit)**; heterogeneity is large — studies with a focus on the relation, standard IATs, high-polarity attributes, relative criteria and matched content (k = 13) reached **r = .37**, studies lacking these features (k = 6) **r = .02**; most studies were underpowered. — Kurdi et al. 2019, *American Psychologist* 74(5):569–586, doi:10.1037/amp0000364 (abstract of the OSF preprint read)
- [V] Can IAT scores be changed, and does behavior follow? Contest of 17 interventions (4 studies, total N = 17,021): 8 reduced implicit preference for Whites over Blacks right away, 9 did not; no intervention consistently changed explicit preferences. Follow-up with 9 interventions (N = 6,321): **all 9 changed the IAT immediately; none was effective after a delay of hours to days.** — Lai et al. 2014, *JEP:General* 143(4):1765–1785, doi:10.1037/a0036260; Lai et al. 2016, *JEP:General* 145(8):1001–1016, doi:10.1037/xge0000179 (abstracts read)
- [V] ★ Network meta-analysis of **492 studies (87,418 participants)** of procedures meant to change implicit measures: implicit measures can be changed but effects are small (|d| < .30), mostly single-session, probably inflated by bias; explicit measures changed less; behavior changed "trivially"; **changes in implicit measures did not mediate changes in explicit measures or behavior.** — Forscher et al. 2019, *JPSP* 117(3):522–559, doi:10.1037/pspa0000160 (abstract read, OA PMC6687518)
- [~] Project Implicit's own FAQ says a single IAT is unlikely to predict a single person's behavior, that predictive value is in the aggregate and "fairly small on average", and advises not reading an individual score as something permanent about a person. (Paraphrase of the FAQ page as returned by a fetch tool; check wording at https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/faqs.html before quoting.)
- GRADE C: STUDY IAT as a measure of individual "implicit bias" that predicts discriminatory behavior — original: Greenwald et al. 1998 (3 experiments). Later evidence: Oswald 2013 (ρ = .14), Greenwald 2009 (r = .24), Kurdi 2019 (β = .14), Gawronski 2017 (stability .53 after correction), Lai 2016 and Forscher 2019 (changes do not last or transfer). Group-level patterns exist; individual diagnosis and training-by-IAT claims are not supported.

#### C3 · 刻板印象威胁 stereotype threat
- [V] ★ **Steele & Aronson 1995**, original: stereotype threat = "being at risk of confirming, as self-characteristic, a negative stereotype about one's group". Study 1: **117 Black and White Stanford undergraduates recruited (114 analysed)**, 2 (race) × 3 (test description) design, $10 for 1 h; **30-min test of 30 verbal items (27 difficult GRE-guide items)** presented as diagnostic of ability or as a laboratory problem-solving task; performance adjusted for self-reported verbal SAT (Black mean 592, White 632). In Study 1 the race × condition interaction **did not reach conventional significance (p < .19)**; Study 2 (**20 Black + 20 White female Stanford undergraduates, 10 per cell**) gave Race × Test Description F(1,35) = 8.07, p < .01; Study 3 (cognition) and Study 4 (stereotype salience) followed. — *JPSP* 69(5):797–811, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.69.5.797 (full text read)
- [V] ★ A famous misreading: the 1995 scores were *statistically adjusted for prior SAT scores*, so the experiments show that, absent threat, the two groups differ about as much as their SAT scores predict; they do **not** show that eliminating threat eliminates the Black–White test gap — a widespread popular and scholarly misinterpretation. — Sackett, Hardison & Cullen 2004, *American Psychologist* 59(1):7–13, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.59.1.7 (abstract read)
- [V] Meta-analyses: overall |d| = .26 across minorities and women (k and N not in abstract; effects depend on test difficulty and cue type) — Nguyen & Ryan 2008, *J Applied Psychology* 93(6):1314–1334, doi:10.1037/a0012702; girls on math/science/spatial tests: **47 effect sizes, mean −0.22**, "several signs for the presence of publication bias" and a call for a large replication — Flore & Wicherts 2015, *J School Psychology* 53(1):25–44, doi:10.1016/j.jsp.2014.10.002; cognitive-ability tests under conditions that resemble real testing: **d = −.14 (k = 45, N = 3,532)**, operational-setting studies d from .00 to −.14, nontrivial publication bias, "negligible to small" in college-admission/employment settings — Shewach, Sackett & Quint 2019, *J Applied Psychology* 104(12):1514–1534, doi:10.1037/apl0000420 (abstracts read)
- [V] ★ **Direct replications with large samples**: Ganley et al. 2013 (3 studies, N = 931 school-age children): no evidence that girls' maths performance was hurt by threat (doi:10.1037/a0031412); Flore, Mulder & Wicherts 2018 (registered report, **N = 2,064 Dutch 13–14-year-olds**, 86 classrooms): neither an overall effect nor any moderated effects among girls (doi:10.1080/23743603.2018.1559647); **Registered Replication Report of Johns, Schmader & Martens 2005**: eight direct replications, **total N = 1,502**; no stereotype-threat × gender interaction; the effect among women was **d = −0.006 (95% CI −0.15 to 0.14; N = 822)** vs the original **d = −0.82 (N = 45)**; the ordinary gender gap on the test remained (d = −0.37). — PsyArXiv preprint, version 5 of December 2025, doi:10.31234/osf.io/qctkp_v5 (abstract read; as of December 2025 I found no journal version, so mark "preprint")
- [V] **Updates, 2024–26**: an updated meta-analysis of *stereotype lift* (a performance boost for the non-stereotyped group) found **d = 0.09 (SD_res = 0.19)** against Walton & Cohen's earlier **0.24**, and concluded that subgroup mean differences on cognitive tests "result largely from between-group effects rather than from the effects of ST and SL" — Priest, Griebie, Zhou, Tomeh & Sackett 2024, *J Applied Psychology* 109(9):1337–1354, doi:10.1037/apl0001185 (abstract read). A separate pre-registered meta-analysis of stereotype threat *at work* (k = 61 samples, N = 40,134) finds that self-reported threat correlates with exhaustion, turnover intentions, lower job satisfaction and so on — a correlational literature on felt threat, **not** evidence for the lab performance decrement — von Hippel, Kühner, Coundouris, Lim, Henry & Zacher 2026, *PSPB* 52(4):927–948, doi:10.1177/01461672241297884 (abstract read).
- GRADE C: STUDY stereotype threat (lab effect on performance) — original: 114 + 40 students, interaction n.s. in Study 1 (1995). Later evidence: Nguyen & Ryan 2008 (|d| = .26), Flore & Wicherts 2015 (−0.22 with bias signs), Shewach 2019 (−.14), three large null replications 2013–2025 (N = 931; 2,064; 1,502). Mixed to null in large replications; do not teach as an established cause of group gaps.
- GRADE D: "stereotype threat explains the Black–White (or male–female) test-score gap" — never shown by the original (Sackett 2004), and the best-powered replications find no effect on girls' maths.

#### C4 · 最简群体与内群体偏私 minimal groups & ingroup favoritism
- [V] ★ Tajfel's **minimal group paradigm**: people sorted into groups on trivial criteria still favored their own group when dividing real rewards, even when this gave no personal advantage; the clearest effect was an attempt to **maximize the difference between ingroup and outgroup** even at the cost of absolute joint profit. — Tajfel, Billig, Bundy & Flament 1971, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 1(2):149–178, doi:10.1002/ejsp.2420010202 (abstract read; schoolboy sample sizes quoted in teaching notes disagree — see COULD NOT VERIFY)
- [V] Even *explicitly random* assignment is enough once the notion of "group" is introduced (discrimination stronger than for similarity-based divisions without the group label); fairness also shaped decisions. — Billig & Tajfel 1973, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 3(1):27–52, doi:10.1002/ejsp.2420030103 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ Meta-analyses: 137 tests of ingroup bias — "highly significant and of moderate magnitude", stronger when the ingroup is salient (Mullen, Brown & Smith 1992, *Eur J Soc Psychol* 22:103–122, doi:10.1002/ejsp.2420220202); in cooperation tasks people are more cooperative with ingroup than outgroup members, **d = 0.32**, larger when interdependence exists (social dilemmas d = 0.42; no interdependence, e.g. dictator games, d = 0.19), and the pattern reflects **ingroup favoritism rather than outgroup derogation** (Balliet, Wu & De Dreu 2014, *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6):1556–1581, doi:10.1037/a0037737). (abstracts read)
- [V] Theoretical extension: Greenwald & Pettigrew argue ingroup favoritism is "plausibly more significant as a basis for discrimination in contemporary American society than is outgroup-directed hostility". — *American Psychologist* 69(7):669–684, doi:10.1037/a0036056 (abstract read; an argument, not a meta-analysis)
- [~] A cross-cultural test is under way: a Stage-1 **registered report** proposes > 200 participants in each of > 50 nations, comparing the minimal-group effect with bias toward a first cousin and a national ingroup member (Yang, Schulz, Schmidt et al. 2024, PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/5gpr4_v1; abstract read — design only, no results in what I read; check for the published results before teaching cross-national claims).
- GRADE A: STUDY minimal-group ingroup favoritism — original: Tajfel et al. 1971 (schoolboys, reward matrices). Later evidence: Mullen 1992 (137 tests), Balliet 2014 (d = 0.32 for cooperation, country of participants tested as a moderator). Robust, small-to-moderate; mostly Western lab samples; the *explanation* (social identity vs. norms vs. interdependence) is debated.

#### C5 · 罗伯斯山洞（强盗洞）实验 Robbers Cave
- [V] ★ Design as summarised by the Cummings Center archivist: Sherif ran **three** boys' summer-camp studies — **1949 Happy Valley Camp, Connecticut** (Red Devils vs Bulldogs; conflict *not* resolved), **1953 Camp Talualac, Middle Grove, New York** (Panthers vs Pythons; cut short when the boys discovered the experimental set-up) and **1954 Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma** (Eagles vs Rattlers; the only camp that reached the reconciliation stage); boys were 11–12-year-old, Protestant, white, middle-class, well-adjusted; staff played camp counsellors, Sherif played the caretaker "Mr. Mussey"; boys were never debriefed. — Bazar, "Journey to Robbers Cave", *Behavioral Scientist* (Jennifer L. Bazar, assistant director, Cummings Center for the History of Psychology, Univ. of Akron, which holds the Sherif papers), https://behavioralscientist.org/journey-to-robbers-cave/
- [V] Phases in the 1954 study: groups formed independently → competitive tournament (baseball, tug-of-war etc.; knives as prizes) produced name-calling, raids and flag burning → **mere contact** (shared meals, movie) did **not** reduce tension and sometimes worsened it → **superordinate goals** (water supply failure, pulling a stalled food truck, raising money for a movie) reduced friction; in Sherif's own 1958 summary, unfavorable stereotypes and social distance appeared under competition and fell with goals that required collaboration. — Bazar (as above); Sherif 1958, *American Journal of Sociology* 63(4):349–356, doi:10.1086/222258 (abstract read)
- [~] 22 boys (11 per group) took part in 1954 — from a book review of Perry 2018 (twenty-two boys; I could not open Sherif et al. 1961, *Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation: The Robbers Cave Experiment*, University of Oklahoma Book Exchange; the archive.org copy is lending-restricted). — Pastuzyk 2024 review, doi:10.29173/cjfy30012
- [V] ★ The reappraisal: Cherry's 1995 reading of Sherif's footnotes shows that the 1953 camp ended because the boys saw through the ruse, a reason Sherif later softened to "unfavorable conditions" and researchers' "errors in judgment"; textbook claims about group conflict and its resolution rest on **one** of the three camps; critics also note the conflict was engineered by the researchers (unlike *Lord of the Flies*). Perry's 2018 book *The Lost Boys*, based on archives and interviews with former campers, argues the camp was a "choreographed" set-up with staff sometimes acting as provocateurs (this characterization is from reviews of the book, which I did not open → [~]). — Bazar (as above); Cherry 1995 (book); Perry 2018 (book; Scribe) via Publishers Weekly/Pastuzyk review
- GRADE C: STUDY Robbers Cave — original: 22 boys, one camp (1954) of three. Later evidence: the earlier camps did not reproduce the reconciliation stage (1949 unresolved, 1953 aborted); no independent replication I could find; archival re-reading (Cherry 1995; Perry 2018). The *ideas* (competition breeds hostility; shared superordinate goals help; mere contact is not enough) are supported elsewhere (Pettigrew & Tropp 2006 for contact; Balliet 2014 for interdependence) but the camp itself is a weak single demonstration.

#### C6 · 道德心理 moral psychology
- [V] ★ **Trolley problems replicate**: the contrast between a "switch" type act (harm as a foreseen side effect) and a "push" type act (harm as the means) is huge — original (Hauser et al. 2007; N = 2,646) 89% vs 11% judged the act permissible; **Many Labs 2 (aggregate N = 6,842): 71% vs 17%, OR = 11.54, d = 1.35 (CI 1.28–1.41)**, about half the original d = 2.50; in a second pair of scenarios (Ned/Oscar) d = 0.25 vs original 0.34 (N = 7,923). Familiarity with trolley tasks reduced but did not remove the effect (d = 1.47 unfamiliar vs 1.20 familiar). — Klein et al. 2018, *AMPPS* 1(4):443–490, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (full text read, OA)
- [V] Greene's fMRI claim: in two fMRI studies, moral dilemmas "vary systematically in the extent to which they engage emotional processing and … these variations in emotional engagement influence moral judgment". — Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley & Cohen 2001, *Science* 293(5537):2105–2108, doi:10.1126/science.1062872 (abstract read; participant numbers not verified). Caution: people who give more "utilitarian" answers score higher on psychopathy, Machiavellianism and life meaninglessness, which questions treating those answers as the "error-free" benchmark — Bartels & Pizarro 2011, *Cognition* 121(1):154–161, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2011.05.010 (abstract read)
- [def] 社会直觉主义 social intuitionist model: moral judgment is mainly the product of quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions); moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction; it emphasizes social and cultural influence. — Haidt 2001, *Psychological Review* 108(4):814–834, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.108.4.814 (abstract read)

- [V] Cross-cultural status of the five-factor structure is mixed: the short Moral Foundations Questionnaire's five-factor structure was supported in a **30-society** dataset, though item loadings differ across WEIRD and non-WEIRD samples (Doğruyol, Alper & Yilmaz 2019, *Personality & Individual Differences* 151:109547, doi:10.1016/j.paid.2019.109547; abstract via Semantic Scholar); but across **27 countries** it is "difficult to specify" the items in a five-factor model that converges without problems (Iurino & Saucier 2020, *Assessment* 27(2):365–372, doi:10.1177/1073191118817916); Atari et al. 2023 (MFQ-2, 25 populations; Study 2 N = 3,902 in 19 populations) argue for **six** foundations — fairness splits into **equality** and **proportionality**, beside care, loyalty, authority and purity — and report that which foundations matter most in the network of moral variables "varied across cultural contexts" (*JPSP* 125(5):1157–1188, doi:10.1037/pspp0000470). (abstracts read)
- [V] ★ **Moral Machine** (autonomous-vehicle dilemmas): **40 million decisions in ten languages from millions of people in 233 countries and territories**; the authors report global preferences, demographic variation, "three major clusters of countries", and correlations of the differences with modern institutions and deep cultural traits. — Awad et al. 2018, *Nature* 563:59–64, doi:10.1038/s41586-018-0637-6 (abstract read; a published "Matters Arising" critique exists — Bigman & Gray 2020, "Life and death decisions of autonomous vehicles", *Nature* 579:E1–E2, doi:10.1038/s41586-020-1987-4 — content not read). [~] The three strongest preferences — spare humans over animals, spare more lives, spare younger lives — come from a CNRS summary of the paper, not from the paper text I could not open; the cluster names "Western/Eastern/Southern" are likewise from memory → do not print them. Self-selected online respondents, not representative samples.
- [V] Morality-as-cooperation: in the ethnographic records of **60 societies**, seven cooperative behaviors (helping kin, helping your group, reciprocating, bravery, deference to superiors, fair division, respect for prior possession) were **uniformly considered morally good**, and most were observed in most societies across all regions. — Curry, Mullins & Whitehouse 2019, *Current Anthropology* 60(1):47–69, doi:10.1086/701478 (abstract read)
- GRADE A: STUDY trolley "switch vs push" judgments (the double-effect contrast) — original: Hauser et al. 2007 (N = 2,646). Later evidence: Many Labs 2 (N = 6,842, d = 1.35). GRADE B: moral foundations differences by political orientation (direction replicates, d ≈ 0.27–0.29 in ML2, about half of the original). GRADE C: "five (or six) universal moral foundations" as a measurement structure (mixed cross-cultural invariance results above).

[✗] "The Moral Machine shows what 'the world' wants self-driving cars to do" — respondents were a self-selected internet sample (Awad et al. 2018).

### A · 先天还是习得 nativism vs usage-based — one balanced statement
- [def] 刺激贫乏论证 poverty-of-the-stimulus (POS) argument: children end up with syntactic generalizations for which their input offers little or no direct evidence, so some innate, language-specific inductive bias ("普遍语法 universal grammar", UG) must guide them. The textbook worked example is auxiliary fronting in English questions (Chomsky 1965, 1971, 1980; Crain & Nakayama 1987, as cited by Perfors et al.). — Perfors, Tenenbaum & Regier 2011, *Cognition* 118(3):306–338, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2010.11.001 (abstract read)
- [def] Historical hinge: Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (*Language* 35(1):26–58, doi:10.2307/411334) is the standard starting point of the argument against behaviorist (Chomsky: "empiricist") accounts of language; Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and Pinker's *The Language Instinct* (1994) are the standard nativist textbooks, Tomasello's *Constructing a Language* (Harvard UP, 2003) the standard usage-based one. — books/review not opened; [def] only
- [V] Empirical audit of POS: Pullum & Scholz 2002 re-examine four standard POS exemplars against actual language data and report that the arguments do not hold up as stated (the claimed absence of evidence in the input is not established). — *The Linguistic Review* 19(1–2):9–50, doi:10.1515/tlir.19.1-2.9 (abstract read)
- [V] Existence proof against needing innate hierarchy: a Bayesian grammar-induction model given typical child-directed speech plus "certain innate domain-general capacities" can prefer hierarchical phrase structure over linear rules without hierarchy being innately specified (shows learnability in principle, not what children do). — Perfors, Tenenbaum & Regier 2011, *Cognition* 118(3):306–338, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2010.11.001 (abstract read)
- [V] Synthesis position: learning is statistical inference *over* representations that UG supplies ("how nature meets nurture"). — Lidz & Gagliardi 2015, *Annual Review of Linguistics* 1:333–353, doi:10.1146/annurev-linguist-030514-125236 (abstract-level)
- [V] Opposing position: Ambridge, Pine & Lieven 2014 argue (title and abstract) that UG does not help explain child language acquisition, across five domains they review. — *Language* 90(3):e53–e90, doi:10.1353/lan.2014.0051 (abstract-level)
- [~] ★ One balanced statement the lesson can use: nobody serious now claims either "all innate" or "all input". Everyone needs *some* built-in learning machinery plus a lot of input; the live disagreement is whether the built-in biases are specific to language (UG) or are general-purpose learning and social-cognition abilities. The Saffran authors themselves wrote that the data point to "innately biased statistical learning mechanisms rather than innate knowledge" and left open whether SL is language-specific. [Synthesis of the sources above; not a quote from any one of them except the Saffran phrase.] — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 *Science* 274:1926–1928; Lidz & Gagliardi 2015; Ambridge et al. 2014
- [V] Sampling caveat for "what children do": child-language research has covered only about **103 languages (≈1.5% of the world's languages)** and is heavily skewed to English and other Indo-European languages — so universals are inferred from a tiny, WEIRD-adjacent sample (cf. lesson 14). — Kidd & Garcia 2022, *First Language* 42(6):703–735, doi:10.1177/01427237211066405 (abstract read)
- [V] Recent machine-learning existence proof (as of Oct 2026): a generic neural network trained on **61 hours** of head-mounted-camera video-plus-speech from **one child (age 6–25 months)** learned many of the word–referent mappings in that child's everyday experience and generalized zero-shot to new referents; the paper says this shows aspects of word meaning "are learnable" from joint representation and associative learning, not that the child learns it this way. — Vong, Wang, Orhan & Lake 2024, *Science* 383(6682):504–511, doi:10.1126/science.adi1374 (abstract read)
- GRADE C: STUDY "UG / innate language-specific syntax is required" (poverty-of-the-stimulus) — original: argument from the supposed absence of crucial evidence in the input (Chomsky 1980). Later evidence: Pullum & Scholz 2002 (the evidence claims don't hold in four exemplars), Perfors et al. 2011 (domain-general learner can find hierarchy), versus Lidz & Gagliardi 2015 (UG plus inference). Unresolved; teach as an open theoretical dispute, not a result.

### B · 统计学习 statistical learning and word segmentation ★ (the Saffran paradigm)
- [def] 转移概率 transitional probability (TP): the probability that syllable Y follows syllable X, TP(Y|X) = frequency(XY) / frequency(X). Inside a word the TP between syllables is high; across a word boundary it is lower, so a listener who tracks TPs can find likely word edges in speech with **no pauses**. 统计学习 statistical learning (SL) = picking up such regularities from exposure alone. — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996, *Science* 274(5294):1926–1928, doi:10.1126/science.274.5294.1926 (scanned paper read page by page)
- [V] ★ **Participants and design, Exp 1 (Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996)**: **24 eight-month-old infants** from American-English-speaking environments heard **2 min** of a continuous stream made of **four three-syllable nonsense words** in random order "with the stipulation that the same word never occurred twice in a row"; each word occurred **45 times** (180 word tokens = 540 syllables). Two counterbalanced languages: **Condition A: tupiro, golabu, bidaku, padoti**; **Condition B: dapiku, tilado, burobi, pagotu** (each infant heard only one). Sample stream shown in the paper: "bidakupadotigolabubidaku…". — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 (scan read)
- [V] ★ **Timing and acoustics**: speech from a **MacinTalk synthesizer, monotone female voice, 270 syllables per minute** (= 4.5 syllables/s ≈ 222 ms per syllable — my arithmetic from the stated rate; the paper states the rate per minute, not "270 ms per syllable"); no pauses, stress, or other prosodic word-boundary cues, so TPs were the only cue. 180 words × 3 syllables = 540 syllables ÷ 270 per min = exactly 2 min, which checks. [✗] "270 ms per syllable" is a mix-up of the unit. — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 (scan read)
- [V] ★ **The statistics**: TP = **1.0 between syllables inside a word** (e.g., bi→da in bidaku); **0.33 across a word boundary** (e.g., ku→pa), because after any word one of the other three words follows with equal probability. (In the adult study with six words the boundary TPs are 0.1–0.2 and within-word TPs 0.31–1.0; the "0.33/0.20" pair in some textbooks mixes the two studies.) — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996; Saffran, Newport & Aslin 1996 *J Mem Lang* 35:606–621, doi:10.1006/jmla.1996.0032
- [V] ★ **Test, Exp 1 (words vs nonwords)**: head-turn preference procedure (头转向偏好范式; infant on a parent's lap in a sound-attenuated booth, 30° head turn; a trial ends at a 2-s look-away or at 15 s of looking). Test strings were two words from the heard language and two "nonwords" (strings of its syllables that never occurred in that order), e.g. for Condition A: tupiro and golabu (words) vs dapiku and tilado (nonwords). Each of the 4 strings was presented on 3 trials (12 test trials: 6 words, 6 nonwords), repeated with 500-ms gaps. **Mean looking time 7.97 s (SE .41) for words vs 8.85 s (SE .45) for nonwords, t(23) = 2.3, P < .04** — the infants looked longer at the *novel* items (a novelty preference); no difference between conditions A and B, t(22) = 0.31. — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 (scan read)
- [V] ★ **Exp 2 (words vs part-words — the harder test)**: a new group of **24 infants**, same 2-min exposure to a new pair of languages (Condition A words **pabiku, tibudo, golatu, daropi**; Condition B **tudaro, pigola, bikuti, budopa**). Test items were two words vs two **part-words** (the last syllable of one word plus the first two of the next, e.g. **pigola** spans daropi|golatu; TPs of a part-word: 0.33 then 1.0; of a word: 1.0 and 1.0) — so the infants had heard the part-words, just not as units. **6.77 s (SE .44) for words vs 7.60 s (SE .42) for part-words, t(23) = 2.4, P < .03**; conditions A vs B, t(22) = 0.49. — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 (scan read)
- [~] Effect-size arithmetic (derived by me from t and N, paired design → d_z = t/√N, not reported in the paper): Exp 1 d_z ≈ 0.47; Exp 2 d_z ≈ 0.49; both with p between .03 and .04 and N = 24 — i.e., a modest, borderline-by-modern-standards result that has needed many conceptual replications. The looking-time difference is ≈ 0.8–0.9 s on a base of ≈ 7–9 s (≈ 10%).
- [V] **Frequency confound closed**: in Aslin, Saffran & Newport 1998 two words occurred 45 times and two occurred 90 times each, so test words and part-words were equally frequent and only their TPs differed (words 1.0/1.0; part-words **0.5**/1.0). **N = 30** 8-month-olds (2 groups of 15; 7 more excluded), 3-min corpora of 270 word tokens at 4.5 syllables/s (the same pabiku/tibudo/golatu/daropi and tudaro/pigola/bikuti/budopa languages). **6.78 s (SE .36) for words vs 7.36 s (SE .42) for part-words, t(29) = 2.10, p < .05** (d_z ≈ 0.38, my arithmetic); no corpus difference, t(28) = 0.63. — Aslin, Saffran & Newport 1998, *Psychological Science* 9(4):321–324 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Adults, same logic** (Saffran, Newport & Aslin 1996): **24 Rochester undergraduates** (12 per test; monolingual English) listened to a six-word language (babupu, bupada, dutaba, patubi, pidabu, tutibu; 12 CV syllables from p, t, b, d + a, i, u) in a monotone synthetic voice at **216 syllables/min** (≈ 278 ms per syllable), **4,536 syllables in total over 21 min** (3 blocks of 7 min with 5-min breaks), told only to work out "where the words began and ended". **36 two-alternative forced-choice trials** per test, 500 ms between strings. Words vs **nonword** foils: **27.2 of 36 (76%) correct**, chance 18, t(11) = 6.78, p < .01. Words vs **part-word** foils: **22.3 of 36**, t(11) = 3.38, p < .01; worse than the nonword test, t(22) = 2.66, p < .05. Higher-TP words were learned better: **79% vs 72%**, t(11) = 2.31, p < .05. — *J Mem Lang* 35(5):606–621, doi:10.1006/jmla.1996.0032 (full text read)
- [~] Two internal inconsistencies in that paper to avoid copying: (1) the text prints the part-word score as "22.3 of a possible 36 (65%)" but 22.3/36 = 62%; (2) it says "300 tokens of each of the six words" (= 5,400 syllables, 25 min at 216/min) but also "4,536 syllables" and "21 min" (= 252 tokens per word). Quote "21 min, 4,536 syllables, 216 syllables/min" and say "about 250–300 tokens per word". — Saffran, Newport & Aslin 1996 (full text read, arithmetic mine)
- [V] **Natural speech, not just toy streams**: **72 English-learning 8-month-olds** segmented words from fluent infant-directed **Italian** (a language they had never heard) using TPs, i.e., the effect survives natural prosody and coarticulation. — Pelucchi, Hay & Saffran 2009, *Child Development* 80(3):674–685, doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01290.x (full text read)
- [V] **Not language-specific and not human-specific (so far)**: infants and adults segmented sequences of **tones** just as well as syllables (Saffran, Johnson, Aslin & Newport 1999, *Cognition* 70:27–52, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(98)00075-4); cotton-top tamarins discriminated high- from low-TP sequences using the same speech streams (Hauser, Newport & Aslin 2001, *Cognition* 78:B53–B64, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(00)00132-3). The authors say future work must show where human and monkey abilities diverge. — abstracts read
- [V] ★ Rules, not only TPs — and a **2026 multi-lab failure**: Marcus et al. 1999 reported that **7-month-olds** looked longer to sentence structures that were new vs familiar in an artificial-grammar task designed so that counting or TP-sensitivity alone could not solve it, and inferred that infants "represent, extract, and generalize abstract algebraic rules" (*Science* 283(5398):77–80, doi:10.1126/science.283.5398.77, abstract read). "Dozens of studies have since replicated" it, yet the **ManyBabies 3 registered report** — **839 infants (650 after exclusions), 5–12 months, 30 laboratories (31 samples)**, conceptual replication — found **"no significant effect" of consistent vs inconsistent test trials, "strong evidence for a null effect" in a Bayesian analysis, no moderation by age, mono-/multilingualism or paradigm, and no evidence of rule learning in 30 of the 31 samples**. — Visser, Geambasu, Kachergis et al. 2026, *Developmental Science* e70259, doi:10.1111/desc.70259 (abstract read, as of Oct 2026) → this is the strongest recent warning about infant looking-time findings; it concerns *rule* learning, not the TP segmentation above, which has not (to my knowledge) had a multi-lab test.
- [V] Individual differences: in a battery of SL tasks given at two time points, SL as measured by *some* tasks is "a stable and reliable capacity of an individual", independent of IQ and working memory, but SL "is not a unified capacity" (sensitivity to conditional probabilities varies across modality and stimulus). — Siegelman & Frost 2015, *Journal of Memory and Language* 81:105–120, doi:10.1016/j.jml.2015.02.001 (abstract read)
- [V] Why infant looking-time effects need caution: **"Many studies using infant looking time as a measure have samples of 8 to 12 infants per cell, and studies with more than 24 infants per cell are uncommon"**; most published looking-time studies have low power, which "leads in the long run to an increase in both false positive and false negative results"; random subsamples of 8–24 infants drawn from three large datasets gave "highly variable" results — false positives and false negatives — although the full samples were clear. (The Saffran experiments each had N = 24.) — Oakes 2017, *Infancy* 22(4):436–469, doi:10.1111/infa.12186 (abstract read). A 2026 synthesis of the ManyBabies consortium ("thousands of babies across hundreds of labs") says "some, but not all findings in infant research replicate well" — McMillan et al. 2026, PsyArXiv doi:10.31234/osf.io/y59sn_v1 (preprint abstract read).
- [V] Reviews: statistical learning is "both a broad and flexible mechanism (supporting learning from different modalities across many different content areas) and input specific" — Saffran & Kirkham 2018, *Annual Review of Psychology* 69:181–203, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-122216-011805 (abstract read).
- [V] ★ **Replication / meta-analytic status (as of Oct 2026)**: (a) Isbilen & Christiansen 2022 — meta-analysis of 25 years of auditory-linguistic SL: "robust learning across stimuli" in infants and across stimuli and structures in adults, bigger effects when several cues are present, **but significant publication bias**, a narrow range of simplified stimuli, and Bayes-factor analyses showing the data are insensitive to commonly assumed moderators (amount of exposure, TP strength). — *Cognitive Science* 46(9):e13198, doi:10.1111/cogs.13198 (abstract read; effect-size table not opened). (b) Black & Bergmann 2017 — conceptual replications of Saffran et al. 1996 show a small significant effect, but across *all* TP word-segmentation studies the pooled effect was not significant and depended on stimulus type (natural vs synthesized speech); numeric effect sizes not retrieved. — *Proc. 39th Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society*, 124–129 (abstract read). (c) Bergmann & Cristia 2016 — meta-analysis of native-speech word segmentation: **168 experiments, 3,774 infants**; "a persistent familiarity preference across all ages" (no switch to a novelty preference as infants grow), no significant effect of native language or experiment design, "no sign of selective data collection or reporting"; the authors conclude that frequently cited familiarity-to-novelty models "may not, in fact, apply in the case of segmenting words from native speech". — *Developmental Science* 19(6):901–917, doi:10.1111/desc.12341 (abstract read). (d) Adults: Chang 2024 (PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/js97h, bias-adjusted meta-analysis of 20 adult studies, effect between the two original adult effects) and Ricketts & Hartshorne 2022 (CogSci; large CIs, low power) — preprint/proceedings only. (e) FORRT's replication atlas lists a 2018 failed replication of the adult experiment (Murdock et al.; I could not open the report) → [~].
- [V] Newborns (as of 2025): an EEG study of newborns reports statistical learning operating over both phonetic and voice dimensions of syllable streams, supporting that SL is present at birth. — Fló et al. 2025, *eLife* 13:RP101802, doi:10.7554/elife.101802 (abstract read)
- [✗] "Eight-month-olds learn words in two minutes." They *segment* four meaningless nonsense words from a synthetic stream (a prerequisite of learning words), with a ≈ 0.9-s looking-time difference at N = 24 and a laboratory stream with only 1.0-vs-0.33 contrasts; no meanings were attached. [✗] "Infants hear a pause-free stream in real life" — real infant-directed speech has prosody, pauses and many other cues (Pelucchi 2009 uses natural Italian; Black & Bergmann 2017 find synthetic vs natural speech matters).
- GRADE B: STUDY infant statistical learning of word boundaries — original: Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996 (N = 24 + 24, d_z ≈ 0.5, p ≈ .03–.04). Later evidence: Aslin 1998 (N = 30), Pelucchi 2009 (N = 72, natural Italian), Isbilen & Christiansen 2022 (meta-analysis: robust, with publication bias and insensitive moderators), Black & Bergmann 2017 (small pooled effect), Bergmann & Cristia 2016 (168 experiments). Direction is reliable in labs; size is modest and its role in real-life word learning is inferred, not shown.
- GRADE D: STUDY 7-month-olds' algebraic rule learning in the ABA/ABB looking-time paradigm — original: Marcus et al. 1999 (3 experiments). Later evidence: ManyBabies 3 (2026; registered report, 650 analysed infants in 30 labs; no effect, strong Bayesian evidence for the null; null in 30 of 31 samples). Counter-weight: dozens of earlier positive studies in other labs, stimuli and species, and MB3 is a *conceptual* (not exact) replication — so the original *effect in this paradigm* is D, while "can infants learn abstract rules at all" is an open question (treat as C).

### C · 语音知觉窄化 perceptual narrowing & live-person learning
- [def] 知觉窄化 / 知觉调谐 perceptual narrowing (attunement): young infants discriminate many speech contrasts, including ones absent from their home language; over the first year discrimination of native contrasts is kept or improves while discrimination of non-native contrasts declines. — Maurer & Werker 2014, *Developmental Psychobiology* 56(2):154–178, doi:10.1002/dev.21177 (abstract read: the review asks whether the evidence fits "maintenance/loss" or "enhancement/attunement")
- [~] Werker & Tees 1984 is the classic demonstration: English-learning infants discriminate non-native contrasts (the Hindi dental–retroflex /d̪a/–/ɖa/ and a Salish contrast) around 6–8 months but this is no longer readily observed by the end of the first year, while native contrasts stay strong. I could not open the paper (Elsevier, no open copy); what I read is a modern full-text restatement (Panneton et al. 2025, which also confirms the English /ba/–/da/ and Hindi dental–retroflex contrasts "used in Werker & Tees, 1984"). The per-age proportions of infants passing (the numbers on textbook bar charts) are **not verified** → do not quote them. — Werker & Tees 1984, *Infant Behavior and Development* 7(1):49–63, doi:10.1016/S0163-6383(84)80022-3 (abstract not available); Panneton, Ostroff, Bhullar & Netto 2025, *Infancy* 30(1):e12620, doi:10.1111/infa.12620 (full text read)
- [V] Linguistic experience alters phonetic perception "in infants by 6 months of age": 6-month-olds in the US and Sweden differed in how they perceived vowel sounds of their own language vs the other country's language (specific numbers not in the abstract; paper not opened). — Kuhl, Williams, Lacerda, Stevens & Lindblom 1992, *Science* 255(5044):606–608, doi:10.1126/science.1736364 (abstract read)
- [V] Meta-analytic support, with a timing correction: across behavioral, electrophysiological and neuroimaging studies of infants 0–14 months, effect sizes for **native and non-native vowel discrimination changed with age in opposite directions**, "significant by about 6 months of age" — earlier than the 10–12-month timing usually quoted for consonants. — Tsuji & Cristia 2014, *Developmental Psychobiology* 56(2):179–191, doi:10.1002/dev.21179 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003, Exp 1 (live exposure)**: 32 full-term 9-month-old American infants were randomly assigned to Mandarin (16) or an English-speaking control group (16); **21 completed (10 Mandarin, 11 control)**. Exposure: **12 sessions of 25 min over 4 weeks** (10 min of book reading + 15 min of play) with **four native Mandarin speakers** using infant-directed speech and eye contact; infants heard **25,989–42,184 Mandarin syllables (mean 33,120)**. Test 2–12 days later (median 6), computer-synthesized **Mandarin affricate vs fricative** (alveolo-palatal, absent in English; 375 ms syllables), head-turn conditioning, 30 trials: **Mandarin group 65.7% correct (SE 2.40) vs English control 56.7% (SE 2.29), F(1,19) = 7.34, P < .05**; no group difference on the English /ra/–/la/ contrast; no effect of the 2–12-day delay. The American infants' performance was "statistically equivalent" to that of Taiwanese infants raised on Mandarin (t(57) = 0.67, P > .10). — *PNAS* 100(15):9096–9101, doi:10.1073/pnas.1532872100 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Exp 2 (screen exposure)**: 32 more infants were exposed to the same Mandarin content by **audiovisual DVD (16) or audio-only (16)**, 28 completed (15 and 13); they heard **47,634–51,354 syllables (mean 49,866)** — *more* than the live group. Result: the AV and audio-only groups **did not differ from each other or from the English control group** (P > .10) and were significantly worse than the live-Mandarin group (P < .01); one-way ANOVA across the four groups F(3,45) = 3.94, P < .05. — Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003 (full text read)
- [V] Follow-ups: English-learning infants given Spanish exposure from 9.5 to 10.5 months showed a discriminatory ERP response to the Spanish contrast at 11 months but not at 9 months, and an *earlier* ERP response to the English contrast (native processing improved too). — Conboy & Kuhl 2011, *Developmental Science* 14(2):242–248, doi:10.1111/j.1467-7687.2010.00973.x (abstract read). Toddlers aged 24–30 months (N = 36) learned novel verbs from live interaction and from socially contingent video chat but **not** from non-contingent (yoked) video. — Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014, *Child Development* 85(3):956–970, doi:10.1111/cdev.12166 (abstract read)
- [V] Flexibility, not a hard stop: 9-month-olds who failed to discriminate a non-native contrast succeeded after watching two distinct objects paired with the two sounds (Yeung & Werker 2009, *Cognition* 113(2):234–243, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2009.08.010, abstract read); in 2025, English-learning **11-month-olds** discriminated a Hindi contrast when it was shown by a dynamic female face-and-voice (infant-directed or adult-directed) but not with a static object or a male speaker (final N = 39 in Exp 1; 22 + 24 in Exp 2 — small samples, one lab). — Panneton et al. 2025, doi:10.1111/infa.12620 (full text read)
- [V] The "video deficit": across **122 effect sizes from 59 reports** (children 0–6), children learn about **half a standard deviation less** from video than from face-to-face presentation; the deficit shrinks with age; the authors warn of quality and publication-bias issues that may overstate it. — Strouse & Samson 2021, *Child Development* 92(1):e20–e38, doi:10.1111/cdev.13429 (abstract read)
- [✗] "Babies can't learn language from a screen at all." The evidence is a *deficit* (about half an SD, shrinking with age; contingent video chat works for toddlers), not zero; Kuhl 2003 is two small experiments (21 and 28 completers) tested on one phonetic contrast, and I found no direct replication of its exact design. [✗] "Infants lose non-native sounds forever at 12 months" — the effect is attenuated by context, and adults can learn new phonemes (but with less ease).
- GRADE B: STUDY perceptual narrowing in speech — original: Werker & Tees 1984 (English-learning infants vs Hindi/Salish contrasts), Kuhl et al. 1992 (6-month-old US vs Swedish). Later evidence: Tsuji & Cristia 2014 (vowel meta-analysis: opposite changes by ~6 months), Maurer & Werker 2014 (review: attunement, not simple loss), Yeung & Werker 2009 and Panneton 2025 (context can re-open discrimination). Direction reliable; timing varies by contrast; "closure" is not absolute.
- GRADE B: STUDY live social exposure beats screen-only exposure for infant phonetic learning — original: Kuhl, Tsao & Liu 2003 (N = 21 + 28 completers; 65.7% vs 56.7%). Later evidence: Conboy & Kuhl 2011 (live second-language exposure works), Roseberry 2014 (contingent video chat works for verbs at 24–30 months), Strouse & Samson 2021 (video deficit ≈ 0.5 SD, possible overestimate). No direct replication of the original design found; related designs agree, effects are small-N.

### D · 语言的关键期 critical (sensitive) period for language
- [def] 关键期/敏感期 critical/sensitive period: a developmental window in which experience has an outsized effect on a function, with reduced plasticity afterward; applied to language by Lenneberg (*Biological Foundations of Language*, 1967 — book not opened; Johnson & Newport's abstract states his hypothesis that language could be acquired only within a critical period "extending from early infancy until puberty"). Werker & Hensch's review says the question is no longer whether critical periods exist but what opens and closes them. — Werker & Hensch 2015, *Annual Review of Psychology* 66:173–196, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-010814-015104 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Johnson & Newport 1989** (the paper is Johnson & Newport; the task file's "Newport & Johnson" is a reversal): native Korean and Chinese speakers who arrived in the US at ages **3–39**, tested after **3–26 years** of residence, on grammaticality judgments of English; performance was **linearly related to age of arrival up to puberty**, and after that **lower and highly variable, unrelated to age of arrival**. The authors' summary (Newport 1990): effects over age of first exposure are "approximately linear through childhood, with a flattening of the function in adulthood". The paper is cited with N = 46 immigrant participants (Hartshorne et al. simulate "N = 69, the size of the classic Johnson and Newport study", which fits 46 immigrants + 23 native controls). The textbook correlations (about −.87 before puberty, about −.16 after; and the task file's "−.77 before 15") are **not verified** → do not quote. — The effect was not an artifact of amount of experience, motivation, self-consciousness or American identification, and appeared on every grammatical structure tested. — *Cognitive Psychology* 21(1):60–99, doi:10.1016/0010-0285(89)90003-0 (abstract read); Newport 1990, *Cognitive Science* 14(1):11–28, doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1401_2 (abstract read)
- [~] Replications with different outcomes: Birdsong & Molis 2001 (61 Spanish-speaking learners of English; age of arrival still correlated with attainment *after* the presumed critical period, and some late learners scored in the native range — secondary descriptions; paper not opened), *J Mem Lang* 44(2):235–249, doi:10.1006/jmla.2000.2750; Seol 2005 (34 Korean speakers, mixed results, via FORRT summary) → only a partial, mixed replication record.
- [V] ★ **Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker 2018** — dataset of **669,498** native and non-native English speakers who took an online English-grammar quiz (gameswithwords.org; advertised as guessing your native language/dialect; <10 min; 680,333 completed, 669,800 after demographic-consistency exclusions, 669,498 after excluding ages <7 and >89; analysis groups: **monolinguals N = 246,497, immersion learners N = 45,067, non-immersion learners N = 266,701**, 122,068 intermediate cases not analysed; 38 languages with ≥1,000 native speakers). A model separating current age, age of first exposure and years of experience fit best (**R² = .89**, cross-validated) when the *learning rate* starts to decline at **17.4 years**, vs R² = .66 with no change and R² = .70 with a step function. Their reading: grammar-learning ability is "preserved almost to the crux of adulthood (17.4 years old) and then declines steadily"; "a sharply-defined critical period … but the age of offset is much later than previously speculated". — *Cognition* 177:263–277, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2018.04.007 (author manuscript PMC6559801, full text read)
- [V] ★ Ultimate attainment in the same data (learners with ≥30 years of experience and aged ≤70: 11,371 immersion, 29,708 non-immersion): for immersion learners attainment declined only minimally until age of first exposure ≈ **12** (B = −0.009, 0.01 SD/year) and then more steeply (B = −0.06, 0.07 SD/year); non-immersion learners showed no decline from 4 to 9 years (B = +0.01) then a steep decline. The authors conclude learners must start by **about 10–12** to reach native-level proficiency (the offset in *ultimate attainment* is earlier than the 17.4 offset in *learning rate*, because learning takes time) and that earlier lab studies (nearly all <250 subjects) are "largely statistical noise" — a study would need "in excess of 10,000" subjects. — Hartshorne et al. 2018 (full text read)
- [✗] "Hartshorne et al. showed that the critical period ends at 17." 17.4 is where the model puts the start of the decline of the *learning rate*; native-level *ultimate attainment* needs a start by about 10–12; the dataset is a self-selected English-only internet quiz (gameswithwords.org, advertised as a language-guessing game), so representativeness and measurement validity (about 10 minutes of grammar items) are open — and the model itself is a reanalysis target (next bullet).
- [V] ★ Reanalysis of the very same data: van der Slik et al. conclude that Hartshorne et al.'s "one sharply defined critical age at 17.4 for all language learners is based on artificial results"; a *continuous* exponential-learning-with-sigmoidal-decay model fit monolinguals, bilinguals and early-immersion learners better than the discontinuous one; a discontinuous model fit better only for non-immersion and later-immersion learners (critical ages **18.6 and 19.0**), which "can be interpreted as schooling effects" — i.e., societal and personal factors rather than cognition-driven critical periods. Title: "Critical Period Claim Revisited: Reanalysis of Hartshorne, Tenenbaum, and Pinker (2018) Suggests Steady Decline and Learner-Type Differences". — van der Slik, Schepens, Bongaerts & van Hout 2021, *Language Learning* 72(1):87–112, doi:10.1111/lang.12470 (abstract read). Earlier statistical critique: Vanhove 2013 argues that most critical-period studies use statistics irrelevant to the hypothesis's predictions, reanalyses two datasets from a 2010 paper by piecewise regression, finds that the specific age patterns predicted by the hypothesis "are not cross-linguistically robust" and concludes that age patterns in second-language acquisition "are not governed by a critical period". — *PLoS ONE* 8(7):e69172, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0069172 (abstract read; erratum PLoS ONE 2014;9(7):e102922).
- [V] First-language evidence: Mayberry & Lock 2003 report that early language experience, spoken **or signed**, supports near-native second-language grammar in adulthood, whereas deaf adults with little accessible early language performed poorly — i.e., what matters is early exposure to *some* language, not to speech. — *Brain and Language* 87(3):369–384, doi:10.1016/S0093-934X(03)00137-8 (abstract read); Mayberry, Lock & Kazmi 2002, *Nature* 417:38 (citation only).
- [V] Language creation by children: Nicaraguan Sign Language arose among deaf Nicaraguans over the 25 years before 2004; in its initial creation children analyzed complex events into basic elements and sequenced them into hierarchically structured expressions "according to principles not observed in gestures accompanying speech in the surrounding language", and successive cohorts of learners extended the procedure, turning a gestural system into a linguistic one. — Senghas, Kita & Özyürek 2004, *Science* 305(5691):1779–1782, doi:10.1126/science.1100199 (abstract read)
- [~] Genie (found 1970 at about age 13 after extreme isolation and abuse; language developed but with persistent syntactic limits) is the textbook single case; the abuse, malnutrition and possible pre-existing impairment confound any conclusion about *timing alone*. — Curtiss et al. 1974, *Language* 50(3):528–554, doi:10.2307/412222 (citation checked; paper not opened; details here are the standard summary, [~])
- [V] Reinterpretation, not refutation: Hernandez, Bodet, Gehm & Shen argue that Hartshorne et al.'s result — "a critical period ending around 17 years of age for the most effective acquisition of a second language" — "can be conceptualized by emergentist models of language acquisition in which both behavior and brain interactively reorganize across development", i.e., a gradual, experience-dependent decline rather than a switch. — *Cognition* 206:104478 (2021), doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2020.104478 (abstract read; a theoretical reflection, no new data)
- GRADE B: STUDY sensitive period for language/grammar — original: Lenneberg 1967 (hypothesis), Johnson & Newport 1989 (46 immigrants + controls; linear decline to puberty). Later evidence: Hartshorne et al. 2018 (N = 669,498; decline of learning rate from 17.4; attainment ceiling from ~10–12), van der Slik et al. 2021 (continuous model fits better; breaks look like schooling), Vanhove 2013, Mayberry & Lock 2003, Nicaraguan Sign Language (Senghas 2004). Direction reliable (earlier start → better ultimate attainment); the *sharp end age* and whether it is a "period" or gradual decline are disputed.
- GRADE C: STUDY "exact end of the critical period" (puberty / 12 / 17.4 / 18.6) — different datasets give different ages by 5+ years; teach as a gradual decline with debated shape.

### E · 词语学习与词汇量 word learning and vocabulary size
- [V] Infants know some word meanings far earlier than "first words": 6- to 9-month-olds shown pairs of pictures looked at the one their parent named (common nouns, not lab-trained), "from the age of 6 mo onward"; abstract words ('all gone', 'eat') were understood only from about **10 months** (n = 98, 6–16 months), with robust gains around 14 months — mothers used abstract words less often when their referent was present. — Bergelson & Swingley 2012, *PNAS* 109(9):3253–3258, doi:10.1073/pnas.1113380109; Bergelson & Swingley 2013, *Cognition* 127(3):391–397, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2013.02.011 (abstracts read)
- [V] **Parent-report norms (Wordbank; MacArthur–Bates CDI 麦克阿瑟–贝茨沟通发展量表)**: in *every* language in the database the **median child produces 50 words between 16 and 20 months**, "though this analysis masks tremendous between-child variability"; most parents report that their child understands most CDI words by 18 months; variability does not shrink with age once ceiling effects are removed (for American English production, the median absolute deviation about equals the median from age 1 to almost 2). — Frank, Braginsky, Yurovsky & Marchman 2021, *Variability and Consistency in Early Language Learning: The Wordbank Project* (MIT Press), ch. 5, https://langcog.github.io/wordbank-book/vocabulary.html (full chapter read); Frank et al. 2017, *J Child Lang* 44(3):677–694, doi:10.1017/S0305000916000209 (abstract read)
- [V] **How wide "normal" is** (Norwegian CDI data, Wordbank Table 5.1; the age by which the 25th and 75th percentile of children reach the milestone): produces 10 words **13–16 months**; produces 50 words **17–20**; produces 100 words **18–23**; produces 200 words **20–26**; understands 50 words **10–15**; walking (national Norwegian survey, 47,515 parent respondents) **12–14**. The spread of language milestones is 3–6 months (17–42% of age), vs 2 months (15%) for walking. — Frank et al. 2021, ch. 5, Table 5.1 (full chapter read; walking data from Størvold, Aarethun & Bratberg 2013 as cited there)
- [~] Parent-report caveat: the same chapter notes that median early-comprehension reports differ wildly across datasets (from 8 months, medians of 4.5 words in Swedish vs 123.5 in Mandarin-Taiwanese), "high numbers feel unlikely", and the authors are "largely agnostic" — CDI numbers are parent reports, with cohort, form-length and administration differences between languages. — Frank et al. 2021, ch. 5
- [V] **Fast mapping then forgetting**: in four experiments, **24-month-olds** were "excellent at referent selection, but very poor at retention" after a 5-min delay (retention of the first 4 novel names appeared only when selection was augmented with ostensive naming) — so a single exposure creates a transient link, not a learned word. — Horst & Samuelson 2008, *Infancy* 13(2):128–157, doi:10.1080/15250000701795598 (abstract read). The term 快速映射 fast mapping and the original demonstration are from Carey & Bartlett 1978 (*Papers and Reports on Child Language Development* 15:17–29; citation only, not opened). The 形状偏好 shape bias (young children generalize a new object name to objects of the same *shape*) is from Landau, Smith & Jones 1988, *Cognitive Development* 3(3):299–321, doi:10.1016/0885-2014(88)90014-7 — abstract not available to me; numbers not verified, description = standard summary [~].
- [✗] "There is a vocabulary explosion at 18 months (a special switch)." The *acceleration* is real, but a spurt as a distinct change is not well supported: among 38 children only **5** fit a spurt (logistic) curve better than a smoothly accelerating one (Ganger & Brent 2004, *Developmental Psychology* 40(4):621–632, doi:10.1037/0012-1649.40.4.621); McMurray 2007 shows by simulation and mathematics that acceleration is a necessary by-product if many words are learned in parallel and words vary in difficulty, so "specialized cognitive changes are unnecessary" (*Science* 317(5838):631, doi:10.1126/science.1144073). (abstracts read) → GRADE C for any claim of a dedicated "naming insight".
- [V] ★ **Adult vocabulary size** (the number to use): an average **20-year-old native speaker of American English knows about 42,000 lemmas** (词元; headwords counted with their inflections merged) **and 4,200 non-transparent multiword expressions, derived from 11,100 word families**; range 27,000 (lowest 5%) to 52,000 (highest 5%); the median 60-year-old knows 48,200 lemmas (+6,200 in 40 years, "about one new lemma every 2 days"), 13,400 word families. Method: **221,268 people (265,346 sessions)** judged 67 words + 33 non-words each from a list of 61,800 lemmas; "knowing" can be as shallow as knowing a word exists. — Brysbaert, Stevens, Mandera & Keuleers 2016, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1116, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116 (full text read)
- [V] The paper's own rate arithmetic: 11,100 word families = **about 1.7 new words per day if learning starts at age 2** (Nation's estimate, quoted there). My arithmetic: 42,000 lemmas ÷ (18 years × 365.25 days) ≈ **6.4 lemmas/day** averaged over childhood and adolescence, many of them inflected/derived relatives of words already known. — Brysbaert et al. 2016 (full text read); the second figure is my arithmetic
- [~] "Children learn about 10 words a day" (often attributed to Bloom 2000 or Pinker 1994): I could not trace it to a primary source I could open (Bloom 2000, *How Children Learn the Meanings of Words*, MIT Press — book not opened); the Brysbaert-based arithmetic above gives about 1.7 families or about 6 lemmas per day on average. Treat "10 a day" as a rough upper-end folk figure → CANNOT VERIFY, do not use as fact.
- [def] 词族 word family / 词元 lemma / 词形 type — Brysbaert et al.'s three counting units; the choice changes a vocabulary estimate by a factor of about 4 to 6 (11,100 families vs 42,000 lemmas vs ~71,400 word types with inflections = 1.7 × 42,000, the paper's rough multiplier), so any "how many words" claim needs its unit. — Brysbaert et al. 2016 (full text read)

### F · "词汇鸿沟" the word gap ★ (Hart & Risley "30 million words")
- [V] ★ **What was measured (Hart & Risley 1995, as described by the authors in 2003)**: **42 families** observed for **one hour a month for about 2½ years** (**1,318** observations) while children learned to talk (observation began when the children were **7–9 months old** and continued until they turned **3**). By occupation, **13 upper-SES (professional), 10 middle, 13 lower, 6 on welfare**; analyses group them as **13 professional / 23 working-class / 6 welfare**; **17 of the 42 children were African American** (1 upper-SES, 3 middle, 7 lower, all 6 welfare families), 23 were girls. — Hart & Risley 2003, "The Early Catastrophe: The 30 Million Word Gap by Age 3", *American Educator*, Spring 2003, https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2003/hart_risley (full text read; the original is the 1995 book *Meaningful Differences in the Everyday Experience of Young American Children*, Brookes, not opened)
- [V] ★ **The numbers in the article**: words *heard by the child per hour* — **2,153 (professional), 1,251 (working-class), 616 (welfare)**; affirmatives : prohibitions per hour — **32 : 5 (6 : 1), 12 : 7 (2 : 1), 5 : 11 (1 : 2)**. — Hart & Risley 2003 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **How "30 million" is produced — an extrapolation, not a measurement**: the authors "linearly extrapolate" the hourly averages to a **100-hour week** (14-hour waking day) → 215,000 / 125,000 / 62,000 words per week; then to a **5,200-hour year** → **11.2 / 6.5 / 3.2 million words**; then to **four years** → **almost 45 / 26 / 13 million**; "By age 4, the average child in a welfare family might have 13 million fewer words of cumulative experience than the average child in a working-class family." The text itself never states "30 million"; the figure is the professional–welfare difference (≈ 45M − 13M ≈ 32M) and appears only in the section heading, which says "by Age 3" although the extrapolation is to age 4. They also warn "all researchers caution against extrapolating" and "we depend on future studies to refine this estimate". — Hart & Risley 2003 (full text read)
- [V] Why it mattered to the authors: for the 29 children followed up, **rate of vocabulary growth at age 3 correlated r = .58 with PPVT-R (receptive vocabulary) and r = .74 with TOLD-2 language scores at age 9–10**; vocabulary use at age 3 correlated r = .57 (PPVT-R), .72 (TOLD), and .56 with reading comprehension (CTBS/U). Small N, correlational, within a sample that confounds SES, race and neighbourhood. — Hart & Risley 2003 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Direct re-test (Sperry, Sperry & Miller 2019)**: five studies in five American communities across the socioeconomic spectrum, ethnography plus longitudinal home observations of **42 children (18–48 months)**: "Results do not support Hart and Risley's claim", show "substantial variation in vocabulary environments within each socioeconomic stratum", and the authors argue that definitions of verbal environment that **exclude multiple caregivers and bystander talk** underestimate what low-income children are exposed to. — *Child Development* 90(4):1303–1318, doi:10.1111/cdev.13072 (abstract read)
- [V] The reply: Golinkoff, Hoff, Rowe, Tamis-LeMonda & Hirsh-Pasek 2019 argue the gap should not be abandoned and that what matters is **quality speech directed to children**, not overheard speech; there are "serious risks" in claiming low-income children on average hear sufficient high-quality language. — *Child Development* 90(3):985–992, doi:10.1111/cdev.13128 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Meta-analysis (as of Oct 2026 the best summary)**: **19 studies**; a significant effect of SES on input quantity, **moderated by what is counted**: studies counting *only child-directed speech* find a **large** SES difference; studies counting *all speech in the environment* find **no** SES effect; overall "young children from low-SES homes heard less child-directed speech than children from mid- to high-SES homes, though this difference was much smaller than Hart & Risley's '30 Million Word Gap'". — Dailey & Bergelson 2022, *Developmental Science* 25(3):e13192, doi:10.1111/desc.13192 (abstract read)
- [V] **Sampling method matters**: comparing hour-long video with daylong audio in the same **44 infants** (6–7 months), the 1-hour videos gave denser noun input — "more akin to 'peak' audio hours" — and awake time captured was about 10× greater in audio but noun input only 2–4× greater; the authors say short recordings are "a dense and somewhat different sample … rather than a typical one" and warn against extrapolating. (This is exactly the Hart & Risley design: one observed hour per month, extrapolated.) — Bergelson, Amatuni, Dailey, Koorathota & Tor 2019, *Developmental Science* 22(1):e12715, doi:10.1111/desc.12715 (abstract read; the application to Hart & Risley is my inference)
- [V] **It's culturally specific as well** (link to lesson 14): among Tsimane forager-horticulturalists in Bolivia, **less than 1 minute per daylight hour** is spent talking to children under 4, about **4 times less** than the amount of talk directed to others present at the same time and place — yet those children learn the language; the authors conclude there is "large cross-cultural variation" in input and that generalizable acquisition theories need more diverse populations. (Ns between 9 and 24 per age band; observational time-allocation study.) — Cristia, Dupoux, Gurven & Stieglitz 2019, *Child Development* 90(3):759–773, doi:10.1111/cdev.12974 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **The largest cross-cultural datasets (as of Oct 2026)**: (a) >2,500 day-long child-centered audio recordings of **1,001 children aged 2–48 months from 12 countries on six continents** (urban, farmer-forager and subsistence-farming settings): age and clinical risk predicted how much speech children produced, and so did adult talk in their environment ("children who heard more talk from adults produced more speech"), but **maternal education (SES) was not significantly associated with children's speech over the first 4 years, and neither were gender or multilingualism** — Bergelson et al. 2023, *PNAS* 120(52):e2300671120, doi:10.1073/pnas.2300671120 (abstract read). (b) A systematic review of **29 reports on 1,314 infants** (≤ 24 months; observations ≥ 30 min): outside North America infant-directed vocalization was far more frequent for **urban (median 12.6% of observations) than rural (3.6%)** infants; in the US the SES gap was tiny — **16.4% (high SES) vs 15.1% (low SES)** — i.e., much smaller than the urban–rural gap elsewhere; the authors caution the literature is a biased sample of populations. — Cristia 2023, *Developmental Science* 26(1):e13265, doi:10.1111/desc.13265 (abstract read)
- [V] **What survives: interaction quality**. (a) In **60 low-income families**, the quality of parent–child communication at 24 months (symbol-infused joint engagement, routines and rituals, fluent connected communication) accounted for **27% of the variance in expressive language one year later** and was a "considerably more potent" predictor than the quantity of mothers' words or sensitive parenting — Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015, *Psychological Science* 26(7):1071–1083, doi:10.1177/0956797615581493. (b) In **36 SES-diverse children aged 4–6**, more **conversational turns** with adults (independent of SES, IQ and adult–child utterances alone) went with greater left inferior frontal (Broca's area) activation during story listening, which statistically explained the link between conversational exposure and verbal skill — Romeo et al. 2018, *Psychological Science* 29(5):700–710, doi:10.1177/0956797617742725. (c) In **3,213 12-hour recordings** (children 2–48 months), child vocalizations and conversational turns predicted **7%–16% of variance** in language-assessment scores; lower-SES children produced fewer vocalizations and turns, with large within-group variation — Gilkerson et al. 2017, *Am J Speech-Lang Pathol* 26(2):248–265, doi:10.1044/2016_ajslp-15-0169. (all abstracts read; all correlational)
- [V] **Why might a gap exist? A causal hint (2022)**: in two pre-registered studies, higher-SES caregivers prompted to reflect on financial scarcity spoke less to their 3-year-olds in a later play session than a control group (Study 1), and mid- to higher-SES caregivers had fewer back-and-forth exchanges with their children at the end of the month, when hardship is more likely, than at other times (Study 2); the authors call it "preliminary evidence" that "structural constraints may affect how much parents speak to their children". Sample sizes not in the abstract. — Ellwood-Lowe, Foushee & Srinivasan 2022, *Developmental Science* 25(1):e13151, doi:10.1111/desc.13151 (abstract read)
- GRADE D: STUDY "children in poverty hear 30 million fewer words" (literal number) — original: Hart & Risley 1995 (42 families, hourly samples extrapolated linearly to age 4). Later evidence: Sperry et al. 2019 (five communities, no support), Dailey & Bergelson 2022 (19 studies: gap much smaller and depends on counting child-directed speech only), Bergelson et al. 2019 (short recordings unrepresentative), Cristia et al. 2019 (huge cultural variation). Teach as a cautionary tale about extrapolation from a small, local, hour-sampled study.
- GRADE B: STUDY quality and contingency of caregiver–child interaction (conversational turns, joint engagement) predict language outcomes — original: Hirsh-Pasek 2015 (60 families, 27% of variance), Romeo 2018 (N = 36), Gilkerson 2017 (3,213 recordings, 7–16%). Direction consistent across three designs, but all correlational, small or modest effects, and mostly US samples.
- GRADE B: SES differences in children's *vocabulary* (as opposed to input quantity) exist and are replicated widely, but I did not pull the meta-analytic numbers in this session → no effect size here (see COULD NOT VERIFY).

### G · 语言与思维 language and thought (Whorf, 萨丕尔–沃尔夫假说) ★
- [def] ★ 萨丕尔–沃尔夫假说 Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, two versions. **Strong = 语言决定论 linguistic determinism**: language determines (limits) what can be thought or perceived. **Weak = 语言相对论 linguistic relativity**: the categories and habits of one's language bias or influence perception, memory and habitual thought without making other thoughts impossible. A one-line formulation from a review that argues for a weak version: "The Whorf hypothesis holds that we view the world filtered through the semantic categories of our native language"; the same authors note "consensus has oscillated between embrace and dismissal". — Regier & Kay 2009, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 13(10):439–446, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.07.001 (abstract read; Whorf's own texts, e.g. *Language, Thought and Reality* 1956, not opened)
- [V] ★ **Boroditsky 2001 (time)**: three experiments. English talks about time mostly horizontally, Mandarin also vertically; Mandarin speakers were faster to confirm "March comes earlier than April" after seeing a *vertical* array of objects than a horizontal one, English speakers the reverse; the vertical bias in Mandarin–English bilinguals was related to the age they began English; English speakers *taught* vertical time terms later showed the Mandarin-like bias. Her own conclusion is limited: language "plays an important role in shaping habitual thought … but does not entirely determine one's thinking in the strong Whorfian sense". — *Cognitive Psychology* 43(1):1–22, doi:10.1006/cogp.2001.0748 (full abstract read; sample sizes not in the abstract)
- [V] ★ **Replication record for Boroditsky 2001** — contested: January & Kako 2007 report **six unsuccessful attempts** and conclude the original "provides no support for the Whorfian hypothesis" (doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2006.07.008); Chen 2007 reports that Chinese speakers actually use **horizontal** spatial metaphors for time *more often* than vertical ones and **four failed replications** (doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2006.09.012); Boroditsky, Fuhrman & McCormick 2011 review "all of the available evidence both for and against" and report new data they say show Mandarin speakers do think about time vertically more than English speakers (doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2010.09.010). I found no pre-registered multi-lab replication. — *Cognition* 104(2):417–426, 104(2):427–436, 118(1):123–129 (abstracts read) → GRADE C
- [V] ★ **Russian blues (Winawer et al. 2007)**: Russian has obligatory words for lighter blue (голубой goluboy) and darker blue (синий siniy). Russian speakers were **faster to discriminate two blues that straddled the siniy/goluboy boundary** than two from the same category, the advantage was **eliminated by a verbal dual task but not a spatial one**, was stronger for perceptually close colors, and English speakers tested on the same stimuli showed **no category advantage**. — *PNAS* 104(19):7780–7785, doi:10.1073/pnas.0701644104 (abstract read)
- [V] Brain evidence: Greek has two basic blue terms (ghalazio, ble); the visual mismatch negativity (an automatic, preattentive change-detection EEG signal) was **larger for blue than green deviants in Greek speakers but similar in English speakers** — an implicit effect of terminology. — Thierry, Athanasopoulos, Wiggett, Dering & Kuipers 2009, *PNAS* 106(11):4567–4570, doi:10.1073/pnas.0811155106 (abstract read)
- [V] **Where it fails or weakens**: (a) the "Whorf is right only in the right visual field" claim (faster discrimination across color-name borders in the right but not left visual field; disrupted by verbal but not spatial load — Gilbert, Regier, Kay & Ivry 2006, *PNAS* 103(2):489–494, doi:10.1073/pnas.0509868103) was **not reproduced** in ten versions of the two original experiments with **230 naive observers**: category effects appeared "to the same extent in both visual fields" (Witzel & Gegenfurtner 2011, *Journal of Vision* 11(12):16, doi:10.1167/11.12.16); (b) a 2024 preprint finds **no category effect for Italian blues** (Italian is assumed to have two basic blue terms) vs English (Stratmann & Lohmann, PsyArXiv doi:10.31234/osf.io/p54wm; not peer-reviewed; abstract read). → the *lateralization* claim is GRADE D; the *basic* category advantage is GRADE B (small, language- and stimulus-dependent).
- [V] Review (as of 2020): across behavioral and EEG evidence "a consistent finding is that language causes us to perceive in a more categorical way" and such effects "naturally arise from the interactive and predictive nature of perception" (the authors include proponents of linguistic relativity, Lupyan and Boroditsky). — Lupyan, Abdel Rahman, Boroditsky & Clark 2020, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 24(11):930–944, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2020.08.005 (abstract read). Methods review: **101 dual-task studies** show covert language helps memory and categorization when items have ready labels, helps inhibitory control, task-set reminders and mental arithmetic, and has less support for cross-modal integration, reasoning relying on visual detail, low-nameability items and theory of mind. — Nedergaard, Wallentin & Lupyan 2023, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 30(2):464–488, doi:10.3758/s13423-022-02144-7 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Universals vs relativity in color words**: Berlin & Kay 1969 (*Basic Color Terms*, University of California Press — book, [def]) proposed ~11 universal basic color categories in a fixed order of emergence. Kay & Regier 2003 test this against naming data from industrialized and non-industrialized societies and conclude **"strong universal tendencies in color naming exist across both sorts of language"** (doi:10.1073/pnas.1532837100, *PNAS* 100(15):9085–9089, abstract read). The counter-evidence: Berinmo speakers of Papua New Guinea (5 basic color terms) showed **no recognition advantage for focal colors** once response bias was controlled and categorical-perception effects "only at the boundaries of existing linguistic categories" (Roberson, Davies & Davidoff 2000, *J Exp Psychol: General* 129(3):369–398, doi:10.1037/0096-3445.129.3.369); **Himba** semi-nomadic adults in Southern Africa (the abstract says "semi-nomadic tribesmen") organized color differently from English and from another five-term language, and categorical-perception effects differed even among languages with broadly similar categories (Roberson, Davidoff, Davies & Shapiro 2005, *Cognitive Psychology* 50(4):378–411, doi:10.1016/j.cogpsych.2004.10.001). Regier & Kay's integrative reading: "Whorf was half right" — language matters mainly in one half of the visual field (see the lateralization failure above) *and* naming is shaped by both universal and language-specific forces. (abstracts read)
- [V] ★ **Pirahã number** (an Amazonian people): Gordon 2004 reports a "one-two-many" system, and that performance with quantities greater than three was "remarkably poor" but showed a constant coefficient of variation (analog estimation) — *Science* 306(5695):496–499, doi:10.1126/science.1094492. Frank, Everett, Fedorenko & Gibson 2008 re-examined it and found **no linguistic method whatsoever for expressing exact quantity, not even "one"**, yet speakers matched large sets perfectly in the no-delay task and failed only when memory/delay was involved — concluding that number words are "a cognitive technology" for tracking cardinality across time, space and modality rather than something that changes the underlying number representation — *Cognition* 108(3):819–824, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2008.04.007. **Mundurukú** (Amazonia; number words only up to 5): approximate comparison and addition of large numbers is fine but exact arithmetic fails beyond 4–5 — Pica, Lemer, Izard & Dehaene 2004, *Science* 306(5695):499–503, doi:10.1126/science.1102085. (abstracts read) → counting is a cultural technology (lesson 14 cross-reference); a "strong" reading is false, a "weak" reading (words help exact tracking) is supported. GRADE B.
- [V] **Pirahã grammar** — contested: Everett 2005 claimed Pirahã culture constrains communication to "immediate experience" and so explains the absence of numbers, color terms, embedding, relative tenses, fiction, etc. (*Current Anthropology* 46(4):621–646, doi:10.1086/431525, abstract read); Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009 reject each claim: no evidence that Pirahã lacks embedded clauses or quantifiers, and the "gaps" are neither gaps nor exceptional (*Language* 85(2):355–404, doi:10.1353/lan.0.0107, abstract read); Futrell et al. 2016 searched a corpus and found **no unambiguous evidence for recursive embedding** but a corpus "plausibly consistent with … a regular language", "although this is not the only plausible analysis" (*PLoS ONE* 11(3):e0145289, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0145289, abstract read). → whether culture constrains Pirahã grammar is GRADE C (no consensus); do not present "Pirahã has no recursion" as established.
- [V] **Spatial frames of reference (cross-link to lesson 14, section F)**: the third classic domain for linguistic relativity. Of 20 languages surveyed, only English, Dutch and Japanese had an egocentric frame as their single preferred system (as reported by Henrich et al. 2010 §3.4 citing Majid et al. 2004); Dutch vs Tzeltal speakers solve the 180°-rotation arrow task differently, and Hai//om and apes show allocentric preferences (Haun et al. 2006, *PNAS* 103(46):17568, doi:10.1073/pnas.0607999103). The causal role of language is disputed: Li & Gleitman 2002 flipped the strategy in English speakers by manipulating landmark cues (*Cognition* 83(3):265, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(02)00009-4). Abstracts read; details in the lesson-14 section.
- [✗] "Eskimo languages have 50 (or 100, or 400) words for snow." The claim "has been greatly exaggerated through popularization"; it is the textbook example of a Whorfian myth, traced by Martin 1986 (*American Anthropologist* 88(2):418–423, doi:10.1525/aa.1986.88.2.02a00080) and Pullum 1989 ("The great Eskimo vocabulary hoax", *Natural Language & Linguistic Theory* 7(2):275–281, doi:10.1007/BF00138079) — I could not open either, so I give no count for the original claim. The defensible kernel (and the better version for a lesson): languages that use the same form for snow and ice tend to be spoken in warmer climates, mediated by lower communicative need to talk about snow and ice. — Regier, Carstensen & Kemp 2016, *PLoS ONE* 11(4):e0151138, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0151138 (abstract read)
- [✗] "Language determines what you can think" (strong determinism) — false in the domains tested above: Pirahã speakers match large sets exactly without number words; Mundurukú add large approximate sets; Berinmo and Himba speakers discriminate colors their language doesn't name; English speakers can learn vertical time talk in a short session (Boroditsky 2001). What the evidence supports is a weaker, *domain-specific*, often small effect on speed and categorization, especially when language is used online (verbal interference removes it).
- GRADE D: STUDY strong linguistic determinism — no sourced evidence; the proponents of the weak form themselves reject it (Boroditsky 2001; Regier & Kay 2009).
- GRADE B: STUDY color-category advantage in speeded discrimination — original: Winawer et al. 2007 (Russian blues; erased by verbal interference). Later evidence: Thierry et al. 2009 (Greek EEG), Roberson et al. 2005 (Himba/Berinmo), Lupyan et al. 2020 (review), Nedergaard et al. 2023 (101 dual-task studies), but: Witzel & Gegenfurtner 2011 (no lateralization, 230 observers), Stratmann & Lohmann 2024 preprint (no Italian effect). Small effects, direction reliable in several languages, boundaries unsettled.
- GRADE C: STUDY Mandarin–English vertical time thinking — original: Boroditsky 2001; mixed: 10 failed attempts (January & Kako 2007, Chen 2007) vs the originators' 2011 review. No independent large replication found; no consensus.

### H · 双语与认知 bilingualism and executive function (keep brief)
- [def] 双语优势 bilingual advantage hypothesis: managing two languages trains domain-general executive control (inhibition, shifting, monitoring), so bilinguals outperform monolinguals on non-linguistic control tasks and may show later dementia onset. Bilingualism's *communicative* and social benefits are not in dispute; the question is a general cognitive advantage.
- [V] ★ Paap & Greenberg 2013: **three studies, 15 indicators** of executive processing; no main effect of group on any; the Group × Condition interaction (the marker of an advantage) was significant for only **one** indicator, and in the direction of a bilingual *disadvantage*; both groups performed identically on Raven's matrices; matching on parents' education or restricting to highly fluent bilinguals changed nothing; indicators that should measure the same process (e.g., inhibition in flanker vs Simon tasks) did not correlate across tasks. — *Cognitive Psychology* 66(2):232–258, doi:10.1016/j.cogpsych.2012.12.002 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Publication bias**: of conference abstracts (1999–2012) on bilingualism and executive control, studies fully supporting the advantage were most likely to be published, mixed-results studies next, studies challenging it least — "not due to differences in sample size, tests used, or statistical power"; a funnel-plot asymmetry test gave further evidence. — de Bruin, Treccani & Della Sala 2015, *Psychological Science* 26(1):99–107, doi:10.1177/0956797614557866 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Meta-analyses**: adults — **891 effect sizes from 152 studies**: a very small advantage for inhibition, shifting and working memory *before* bias correction, "no evidence for a bilingual advantage remained after correcting for bias", a small *disadvantage* in verbal fluency; conclusion "no systematic support" — Lehtonen et al. 2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144(4):394–425, doi:10.1037/bul0000142. Children — **143 group comparisons, 583 effect sizes**: overall EF advantage **g = 0.06** ("significant, albeit marginal"), indications of publication bias, advantages concentrated in middle-class samples and one lab; switching survived bias correction; large unexplained heterogeneity — Gunnerud et al. 2020, *Psychological Bulletin* 146(12):1059–1083, doi:10.1037/bul0000301. (abstracts read)
- [V] **Large samples**: in the US ABCD study (**n = 4,524** 9–10-year-olds, demographically representative) "little evidence for a bilingual advantage" for inhibitory control, attention, task switching or cognitive flexibility; bilinguals scored lower in English vocabulary, but that difference was "substantially mitigated" by socioeconomic status and intelligence — Dick et al. 2019, *Nature Human Behaviour* 3(7):692–701, doi:10.1038/s41562-019-0609-3 (abstract read; two errata exist: 3(9):999 and 3(10):1124). **11,041** participants on 12 executive tasks: bilinguals better on 1 test, monolinguals on 4, all differences gone after matching; the unmatched positive effect "so small that it would likely have a negligible impact" on any individual — Nichols, Wild, Stojanoski, Battista & Owen 2020, *Psychological Science* 31(5):548–567, doi:10.1177/0956797620903113 (abstract read). A 2024 analysis of 39 studies argues advantages and disadvantages across cognitive domains are about equally frequent ("a zero-sum game", Bayesian) — Dentella, Masullo & Leivada 2024, *Scientific Reports* 14:2107, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-52417-5 (abstract read; it is a re-framing, not a replication).
- [V] **Dementia — the one place the claim persists, and is contested**: Bialystok, Craik & Freedman 2007 (184 dementia patients diagnosed at a memory clinic, **51% bilingual**): bilinguals showed symptoms **4 years later**, same rate of decline thereafter — *Neuropsychologia* 45(2):459–464, doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2006.10.009. Systematic review (13 articles included) with a meta-analysis of the **prospective** studies (n = 5,527 bilingual and monolingual participants): OR of dementia in bilinguals **0.96 (95% CI 0.74–1.23)** — no protection; retrospective studies "more prone to confounding by education, or cultural differences in presentation to dementia services" — Mukadam, Sommerlad & Livingston 2017, *J Alzheimer's Disease* 58(1):45–54, doi:10.3233/JAD-170131. A later meta-analysis separating age of onset from incidence: **d = 0.32** for age of onset, **d = 0.10** for incidence, "cannot be explained by SES, education, or publication bias" — Anderson, Hawrylewicz & Grundy 2020, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 27(5):952–965, doi:10.3758/s13423-020-01736-5. Newer community studies split: Bengaluru, India (N = 1,234 aged ≥ 60) dementia prevalence 4.9% monolinguals vs 0.4% bilinguals (Venugopal et al. 2024, *Alzheimer's & Dementia* 20(4):2620–2631, doi:10.1002/alz.13702) vs Mexico (urban N = 1,063; rural N = 814) no advantage, and rural Spanish–Indigenous bilinguals *lower* (Kamalyan et al. 2026, *Alzheimer's & Dementia: DADM* 18(1):e70287, doi:10.1002/dad2.70287). (abstracts read; all observational)
- [✗] "Bilingual children are better at executive functioning / bilingualism makes you smarter." The best evidence (Lehtonen 2018 after bias correction; Gunnerud g = 0.06; ABCD n = 4,524; Nichols n = 11,041) says any general executive-function advantage is zero to negligible. Learning a second language has *other* demonstrable value (communication, access), which is not what this claim is about.
- GRADE D: STUDY bilingual executive-function advantage — original: Bialystok and colleagues' 1990s–2000s task studies (not individually opened). Later evidence: Paap & Greenberg 2013 (15 indicators, none), de Bruin 2015 (publication bias), Lehtonen 2018 (0 after bias correction, 152 studies), Gunnerud 2020 (g = 0.06), Dick 2019 (n = 4,524), Nichols 2020 (n = 11,041). Large or bias-corrected tests fail to find a reliable general advantage.
- GRADE C: STUDY bilingualism delays dementia onset — original: Bialystok et al. 2007 (N = 184, 4 years). Later evidence: Anderson 2020 (d = 0.32 onset, 0.10 incidence) vs Mukadam 2017 (prospective OR 0.96) and 2026 Mexico data; retrospective designs confounded by education/culture; no randomized evidence. Mixed.

### I · 读写能力改变大脑 literacy and the brain
- [V] ★ **Dehaene et al. 2010**: fMRI in adults of different literacy — **10 illiterate, 22 who became literate as adults ("ex-illiterates"), 31 literate in childhood** — measured responses to speech, written words, faces, houses, tools and checkers. Literacy enhanced the left fusiform response to writing, induced "a small competition with faces at this location", broadly enhanced visual responses in fusiform and occipital cortex "extending to area V1", enhanced phonological activation to speech in the planum temporale, and afforded top-down activation of orthography from spoken input; "most changes occurred even when literacy was acquired in adulthood". — *Science* 330(6009):1359–1364, doi:10.1126/science.1194140 (abstract read)
- [V] Review: literacy "has significant cognitive consequences that go beyond the processing of written words and sentences" (cultural inventions such as reading shape general cognitive processing); the authors note more than one-fifth of humanity is unable to read and write. — Huettig & Mishra 2014, *Language and Linguistics Compass* 8(10):401–427, doi:10.1111/lnc3.12092 (abstract read)
- GRADE B: STUDY reading rewires visual and language cortex — original: Dehaene et al. 2010 (N = 63 in three groups; cross-sectional, with a clever ex-illiterate group). Later evidence: Huettig & Mishra 2014 (review). Direction plausible and consistent, but groups were not randomized (literacy correlates with schooling, culture and visual experience), small N per group; no independent replication of the specific fMRI contrasts found by me.

### J · 靠语言传授知识 teaching by telling: natural pedagogy
- [def] ★ 自然教学 natural pedagogy (Csibra & Gergely 2009): human communication is "specifically adapted to allow the transmission of generic knowledge between individuals", enabling fast and efficient social learning of "cognitively opaque cultural knowledge that would be hard to acquire relying on purely observational learning mechanisms alone"; infants are prepared to receive it by (i) sensitivity to **ostensive signals** (e.g., eye contact, infant-directed speech, contingent responding) that indicate they are being addressed, (ii) developing **referential expectations** in ostensive contexts, and (iii) a bias to interpret ostensive-referential communication as conveying **kind-relevant, generalizable** information. — *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 13(4):148–153, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.01.005 (abstract read; theory paper, not a single result)
- [V] Calibration for infant speech-perception effects: the **ManyBabies 1** project tested the preference for infant-directed over adult-directed speech across **67 labs** and three methods and found overall **d = 0.35 (95% CI 0.29–0.42)** — "reliably above zero but smaller than the meta-analytic mean computed from previous literature (0.67)"; stronger in older infants, for matching native language/dialect, and in labs using the head-turn preference procedure. This is the best single estimate of how much a famous infant-language effect shrinks under multi-lab replication (≈ half). — ManyBabies Consortium 2020, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 3(1):24–52, doi:10.1177/2515245919900809 (abstract read)
- GRADE C: STUDY natural pedagogy as a specific mechanism — original: Csibra & Gergely 2009 (framework). Later evidence: supporting lab findings on ostensive cues exist, but I found no independent replication or meta-analysis testing the theory as a whole in this session; ManyBabies 1 confirms the IDS component at a smaller size. Cumulative culture's dependence on teaching is revisited in lesson 14.

### Widget constants (13) — the Saffran paradigm (exact published values)
- [V] ★ **Infant stream (Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996, Exp 1)**: 4 trisyllabic nonsense words per language; **Language A: tupiro, golabu, bidaku, padoti; Language B: dapiku, tilado, burobi, pagotu**; 12 distinct syllables; random order, same word never twice in a row (so the next word is one of the other 3 → boundary TP = 1/3); **45 tokens of each word = 180 words = 540 syllables**; MacinTalk monotone synthetic female voice, **270 syllables/min (4.5 per s, ≈ 222 ms each)** → **2 min**; no pauses or prosodic cues; sample: "bidakupadotigolabubidaku". **TP inside words = 1.0; across word boundaries = 0.33.** — doi:10.1126/science.274.5294.1926
- [V] **Infant test, Exp 1 (words vs nonwords)**: HPP; 4 test strings (2 words, 2 nonwords) × 3 trials = 12 trials, 500-ms gaps; trial ends at 2-s look-away or 15 s; **words 7.97 s (SE .41) vs nonwords 8.85 s (SE .45), t(23) = 2.3, P < .04**; N = 24. **Exp 2 (words vs part-words)**: Language A pabiku, tibudo, golatu, daropi; Language B tudaro, pigola, bikuti, budopa; part-word = last syllable of one word + first two of the next (e.g., pigola from daropi|golatu); part-word TPs 0.33 then 1.0; **words 6.77 s (SE .44) vs part-words 7.60 s (SE .42), t(23) = 2.4, P < .03**; N = 24. — Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996, *Science* 274(5294):1926–1928, doi:10.1126/science.274.5294.1926 (scanned paper read)
- [V] **Frequency-controlled version (Aslin et al. 1998)**: 2 words × 45 + 2 words × 90 tokens (270 tokens, 3 min at 4.5 syllables/s); part-word TP 0.5 vs 1.0 for words; **6.78 s (SE .36) vs 7.36 s (SE .42), t(29) = 2.10, p < .05**; N = 30.
- [V] **Adult version (Saffran, Newport & Aslin 1996)**: six words babupu, bupada, dutaba, patubi, pidabu, tutibu; within-word TPs 0.31–1.0, across boundaries 0.1–0.2; 216 syllables/min; **21 min** (3 × 7 min, 5-min breaks), 4,536 syllables; 2AFC, 36 trials per test; **76% (27.2/36) vs nonwords; 22.3/36 (≈ 62%; printed as 65%) vs part-words**; chance 50% (18/36). — Saffran, Newport & Aslin 1996, *J Mem Lang* 35:606–621, doi:10.1006/jmla.1996.0032 (full text read; note the printed 65% vs 62% inconsistency)
- [~] Widget logic that is safe to implement: build a stream by sampling words at random with no immediate repeat; show the TP table (syllable → next syllable probabilities) computed from the stream; mark the word boundaries as the local dips in TP. Display looking-time results as *published means ± SE*, not simulated individual infants.

## 14 · 文化 (culture)
Story hook: words move models from one brain to another; the moved models become local priors — and almost all the evidence on human psychology comes from one unusual slice of humanity. Neutrality rules used below: every "group A differs from group B" statement is given with sample, year and size; a difference between averages of two samples is not a trait of every member of either population; "WEIRD" (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) describes a *sampling* problem, not a ranking of people. Items marked ADDED were not in the task file.

### A · 谁被研究了：WEIRD 样本 who is studied ★
- [def] ★ WEIRD = Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic. Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan's claim: broad claims about human psychology rest on WEIRD samples, and these subjects "are particularly unusual compared with the rest of the species — frequent outliers"; domains reviewed: visual perception, fairness, cooperation, spatial reasoning, categorization and inferential induction, moral reasoning, reasoning styles, self-concepts and motivations, heritability of IQ; "there are no obvious a priori grounds for claiming that a particular behavioral phenomenon is universal based on sampling from a single subpopulation". — Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 33(2–3):61–83, doi:10.1017/S0140525X0999152X (abstract + author-hosted full text read)
- [V] ★ **Who the subjects were (Arnett 2008, as summarized in the BBS paper)**: in the top journals of six subdisciplines of psychology, 2003–2007, **68% of subjects were from the US and 96% from Western industrialized countries** (North America, Europe, Australia, Israel) — countries with **12% of the world's population**; **73% of first authors were at American universities and 99% at universities in Western countries**; in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* **67% of American samples (80% of samples from other countries) consisted solely of undergraduates in psychology courses**; so "a randomly selected American undergraduate is more than 4,000 times more likely to be a research participant than is a randomly selected person from outside of the West". — Henrich et al. 2010 §2.1 (full text); Arnett 2008, *American Psychologist* 63(7):602–614, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.63.7.602 (abstract read: American contributors "comprise less than 5% of the world's population")
- [V] ★ **Has it changed? (as of the 2014–2018 data)**: the same six journals now have authors and samples "a little over 60% American based" (Arnett: over 70%); the change is "mainly due to an increase in authorship and samples from other English-speaking and Western European countries"; authors summarize that about **11% of the world's population is represented, 89% neglected**; **majority-world authors and samples are still only 4–5%**. — Thalmayer, Toscanelli & Arnett 2021, *American Psychologist* 76(1):116–129, doi:10.1037/amp0000622 (abstract read)
- [V] *Psychological Science* in particular (Rad, Martingano & Ginges 2018): almost all research published by that journal "relies on Western samples and uses these data in an unreflective way to make inferences about humans in general" — *PNAS* 115(45):11401–11405, doi:10.1073/pnas.1721165115 (abstract read). Developmental psychology: high-impact developmental journals "are heavily skewed toward publishing articles with data from WEIRD populations", with "a habitual dependence on convenience sampling and little evidence that the discipline is making any meaningful movement toward drawing from diverse samples" — Nielsen, Haun, Kärtner & Legare 2017, *J Exp Child Psychol* 162:31–38, doi:10.1016/j.jecp.2017.04.017 (abstract read). Child language: only ~103 languages (~1.5% of the world's) studied — Kidd & Garcia 2022 (lesson 13).
- [V] **Latest on sampling (2025–26)**: a survey of the Methods sections of five British Psychological Society journals (social, health, clinical, developmental, general; Jan 2021–Dec 2023; **661 articles, 1,293 samples**) found "an over-reliance on Western perspectives" — participants from **Latin America, Eastern Europe, the Middle East and Africa together made up 8.7% of samples** — and inconsistent reporting of gender, race, SES indicators and education — Petrutiu, Birney, Cooke & Stewart 2026, *International Journal of Psychology* 61(2):e70168, doi:10.1002/ijop.70168 (abstract read). A counter-argument: Sherman 2025 holds that sample-diversity recommendations are "sometimes applied to research that does not aim for generalizability across peoples", and stresses "the distinction between WEIRD samples and convenience samples and the importance of convenience samples for globalizing psychological science" — *American Psychologist* 80(7):1073–1080, doi:10.1037/amp0001443 (abstract read; an argument, not data).
- [V] ADDED: a quantitative alternative to "WEIRD vs non-WEIRD": Muthukrishna et al. apply the fixation index (F_ST) to World Values Survey responses to give a **cultural/psychological distance from the US (or China) for any country**, show "the relative homogeneity of the United States" in regional analyses, and report that cultural distance predicts various psychological outcomes. — Muthukrishna, Bell, Henrich, Curtin, Gedranovich, McInerney & Thue 2020, *Psychological Science* 31(6):678–701, doi:10.1177/0956797620916782 (abstract read)
- [V] **Counter-balance — the BBS paper's own list of similarities** (do not present WEIRD as "different in everything"): (i) some illusions, e.g. the Perspective Drawing Illusion, vary little and WEIRD people are not outliers; (ii) color perception emerges in small-scale societies even though the number of basic color terms varies; (iii) recognition of basic facial expressions is largely universal, with a main effect of emotion recognition across populations of **58% of variance** against **9% for cultural specificity** (quoted from Elfenbein & Ambady 2002); (iv) false-belief understanding emerges in every population studied (China, US, Canada, Peru, India, Samoa, Thailand), at ages ranging from **4 to at least 9**, with industrialized populations at the *early* extreme; (v) analog number sense in two Amazonian groups with very limited counting systems; (vi) psychological essentialism in many societies. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.6 (full text read)
- [~] ★ Two ways to misuse the WEIRD paper: "WEIRD people are the odd ones in every domain" (the paper's own §3.6 lists universals) and "non-WEIRD people are all alike" (the paper shows large variation among small-scale societies as well, e.g., Müller-Lyer PSEs from 1 to 15, ultimatum offers from 26% to 58%). Caveat added 2026: the paper's lead example, the Müller-Lyer data, has since been challenged on data-quality and mechanism grounds (section B-update), so use it as an *illustration of the argument*, not as settled evidence.

### B · 米勒–莱尔错觉 Müller-Lyer illusion across societies ★ (Segall, Campbell & Herskovits 1966, as presented by Henrich et al. 2010, Fig. 2) — CONTESTED since 2025: read B-update before using any number below
- [V] ★ **Design**: Segall, Campbell & Herskovits (1966; book *The Influence of Culture on Visual Perception*, Bobbs-Merrill) gathered data on susceptibility to **five "standard illusions"** from **children and adults in many societies**; Henrich et al. present the Müller-Lyer results for **16 societies, 14 of them small-scale** (children aged **5–11**). The measure is the **PSE (point of subjective equality)**: "the percentage that segment a must be longer than b before subjects perceived the segments as equal in length"; larger PSE = stronger illusion. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.1, Fig. 2 (full text read; the 1966 book not opened)
- [V] ★ **Result**: "American undergraduates anchoring the extreme end of the distribution, followed by the South African-European sample from Johannesburg": on average **line a had to be "about a fifth longer" than line b**; at the other end the **San foragers of the Kalahari** were unaffected — "the San's PSE value cannot be distinguished from zero" — and the effect "cannot be detected in two populations" (San and the South African miners); the American undergraduates' PSE "is significantly different from all the other societies studied". Many other populations "cannot be distinguished from one another". — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.1 (full text read)
- [~] ★ **Chart values (adult PSE / child PSE, % — read by me from the printed bar chart at high resolution, ±1; the exact table is in Segall et al. 1966, not opened)**: San foragers 1 / –; South African miners 1 / –; Bete 4 / 3; Ijaw 4 / 10; Songe 5 / 7; Fang 5 / 9; Suku 6 / –; Toro 6 / 15; Yuendumu (Australian Aboriginal) 6 / –; Zulu 5 / 11; Hanunoo 8 / 7; Ankole 8 / 10; Bassari 9 / 11; Senegalese 11 / 14; South African European (Johannesburg) 13 / –; **Evanston, Illinois (US undergraduates / children) 19 / 21**. — Henrich et al. 2010, Fig. 2 (read from the figure; use as "about", mark [~] in any lesson table)
- [V] **Interpretation then**: Segall et al. proposed that visual exposure during development to "carpentered corners" of modern environments favors optical habits that create the illusion; Henrich et al. call it "a kind of culturally evolved by-product". The same project found population-level variation for the Sander-Parallelogram and both Horizontal-Vertical illusions. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.1
- [V] Later evidence that visual context effects vary by culture/environment: **Ebbinghaus illusion** (N = 336; UK, remote Namibian Himba, urban Namibians): remote traditional **Himba children showed no illusion until 9–10 years** whereas UK children showed a robust illusion from 7–8; the UK–Himba difference persisted to adulthood and **urban Namibians** fell in between — Bremner et al. 2016, *Child Development* 87(3):962–981, doi:10.1111/cdev.12511 (abstract read). Himba adults showed markedly **reduced flanker interference** vs Westerners — de Fockert et al. 2011, *PLoS ONE* 6(10):e26337, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0026337 (abstract read).
- [V] Method caveat: a 2024 systematic review of 70 articles on children's illusion susceptibility finds opposing developmental trends (Müller-Lyer, Poggendorff and Vertical-Horizontal magnitudes tend to **decrease** with age; Ebbinghaus and Ponzo tend to **increase**), "substantial variability in methods and stimuli", and that the **method of adjustment tends to yield decreasing, and two-alternative forced choice increasing, magnitude with age** — so cross-society PSE numbers depend on paradigm. — Wincza, Hartley, Fenton-Romdhani, Linkenauger & Crawford 2024, *Cognitive Development* 69:101410, doi:10.1016/j.cogdev.2023.101410 (abstract read)

### B-update · 2025–26: the "cultural byproduct" story is contested (ADDED; the biggest correction in this lesson)
- [V] ★ **Amir & Firestone 2025** argue that the **cultural byproduct hypothesis** — experience with carpentered environments *creates* the Müller-Lyer illusion, so most people in most of history would not have seen it — "is almost certainly false", and that "the evidence for cultural variation and its correlation with key cultural variables is in fact highly inconsistent, beset by questionable research practices, and misreported by later discussions". They do **not** claim that culture never shapes perception: they name myopia, attention styles, local/global biases and reading-dependent phenomena as live candidates. — *Psychological Review* 133(5):1082–1108 (Crossref print date Oct 2026; online 2025), doi:10.1037/rev0000549 (abstract and the author-posted in-press manuscript, PsyArXiv doi:10.31234/osf.io/y7mtf, read for the introduction and sections 3–7; the printed wording may differ)
- [V] ★ **What they found wrong in the 1960s data (their §5)**: Segall et al. tested "nearly 2,000 participants from 16 diverse cultures" with **12 drawings** of varying length discrepancy, forced choice ("which is longer"), the individual score being the number of illusion-supporting answers. Respondents with side biases, alternating answers, or who always chose the outward-fins line were **excluded — "in some cases more than half"** of a sample. For the Zulu, Segall et al. themselves note that with all data included "the score places the Zulus closer to the Western samples in Müller-Lyer susceptibility". Instructions went through interpreters (colleagues "expressed some concern" about what was communicated) and one administrator reported developing "very strong expectations" of the answers and an impulse to correct respondents. The carpentry variable was one subjective questionnaire per site ("crude estimates", including a rating of "degree of acculturation to EuroAmerican ways"). Segall et al. had a section "Inconsistencies in our Own Data" and wrote that "the fit is not so exact or so uniform as to rule out the possibility of other factors" (p. 191). — Amir & Firestone 2025 §5.2 (quotations from Segall et al. 1966 as given there; the monograph itself not opened)
- [V] **Carpentry did not line up with susceptibility**: of the three least susceptible samples (San, Bété, South African mine labourers) only the San fit the carpentry story; Segall et al. found the miners' "very low degree of susceptibility is surprising". The site surveys describe rectangular dwellings almost everywhere — Segall et al. state that only "two samples (Zulu and Bushmen) live in circular houses" ("All other non-Western samples live predominantly in rectangular houses"), and the Zulu survey itself notes "as many as 25 percent of the houses in the region are roughly rectangular" (A&F Table 1). — Amir & Firestone 2025 §5.3, Table 1
- [~] **Sample sizes (A&F Table 1, from Segall et al. 1966; not stated whether before or after the exclusions)**: Ankole 344, Toro 109, Suku 76, Songe 98, Fang 110, Bété 95, Ijaw 162, Zulu 100, San ("Bushmen") 46, Senegalese 262, Dahomean children 65, Hanunóo 57, South African Europeans 44, South African mine labourers 72, Evanstonians 208, Northwestern students 30 — together **1,878**. The table does not list the Yuendumu or Bassari samples that appear in Henrich et al.'s Fig. 2, and lists "Dahomean children" and Northwestern students that Fig. 2 does not show separately, so the two sources are not the same 16 samples; per-society N for Yuendumu and Bassari I do not have.
- [V] **Contrary and older data (their §5.1)**: Rivers (1905, Todas): Toda weaker than English, but the jungle-dwelling Urali and Sholaga showed the **strongest effect of all, above the English**; A&F's one-way ANOVA on Rivers's summary table: F(5, 212) = 7.15, p < .001, with only **5 of 15** pairwise comparisons significant (mostly between two versions of the protocol). Smith (1970, PhD thesis, Rhodes University): rural Xhosa **more** susceptible than urban Xhosa and not different from white South Africans — "contrary to" the carpentered-world expectation. A&F also list mixed or contradictory results for Aboriginal Australians, Lobi and Dagomba (Ghana), Singaporeans, urban/rural Canadians and Bashi/Mbuti (originals not opened). — Amir & Firestone 2025 §5.1
- [V] ★ **Five independent lines against "created by carpentry" (their §4; the cited abstracts I read)**: (1) *Animals*: guppies chose the line with inward arrowheads as longer, with controls excluding physical-difference cues (Santacà & Agrillo 2020, *Current Zoology* 66(2):205–213, doi:10.1093/cz/zoz041); A&F add other fish, parakeets, pigeons, horses, capuchins, bearded dragons, ants and flies, with the effect in every parakeet, pigeon and capuchin and all but one lizard and guppy; **dogs did not show it** once controls showed they used global size (Keep, Zulch & Wilkinson 2018, *Learning & Behavior* 46:501–512, doi:10.3758/s13420-018-0344-z). (2) *Natural scenes*: sampling a range-image database of natural scenes, the Müller-Lyer effects "are correctly predicted by the probability distributions of the possible physical sources" of the retinal images (Howe & Purves 2005, *PNAS* 102(4):1234–1239, doi:10.1073/pnas.0409314102); A&F add that images with human artifacts were no more biased than natural ones. (3) *Variants without lines or corners* (semicircles, dots, faces) still bias judgments (A&F Fig. 5; not independently checked). (4) *Touch*: a haptic Müller-Lyer illusion in blindfolded-sighted, late-blind, low-vision and **congenitally blind** people, with "the effect of visual status … nonsignificant" (Heller et al. 2002, *Perception* 31(10):1263–1274, doi:10.1068/p3340); Suzuki & Arashida 1992 (doi:10.3758/bf03209149) found haptic effects equivalent to visual ones for Müller-Lyer, Ponzo and vertical-horizontal figures, none for Poggendorff and an opposite one for Zöllner — so a haptic illusion does not appear for every figure. (5) *Sight restoration*: children aged 8–16 who gained sight after extended early-onset blindness showed the Müller-Lyer and Ponzo illusions "immediately after the onset of sight" (Gandhi et al. 2015, *Current Biology* 25(9):R358–R359, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2015.03.005; nine children per A&F's description; Henrich et al. 2022 call it "tiny", with "a crude measure" — as quoted by A&F, who answer that nine of nine showing the effect gives p < .002).
- [V] **Age pattern points the other way too**: Müller-Lyer magnitudes tend to *decrease* from childhood to adulthood (Wincza et al. 2024, above), whereas an illusion acquired by exposure should grow with experience; in Segall et al.'s own chart the child PSEs are often as large as or larger than the adult ones (read from the figure above). — Amir & Firestone 2025 note 13
- [~] ★ **The counter-move (preprint, not peer reviewed in the version I read; no Müller-Lyer data)**: Kroupin, Davis, Lopes, Konkle & Muthukrishna 2025 tested four *other* stimuli on online UK/US participants, residents of the semi-urban Namibian town of Opuwo and rural Himba villagers. **Coffer illusion** (rectangles vs circles): UK/US (N = 263) 81% only rectangles, 17% rectangles then circles, 3% circles then rectangles, 0% only circles; Himba villages (N = 113) **48% only circles**, 48% circles then rectangles, under 3% rectangles then circles, under 2% only rectangles; Opuwo (N = 113) 67% circles then rectangles, 19% only circles; replication N = 281 / 126 / 90; vocabulary control (90 more villagers): 43 of 45 circle-only viewers knew a Herero word for rectangles. **Curvature-blindness illusion** seen by 85% (UK/US, N = 231), 36% (Opuwo, N = 97), 19% (villages, N = 90). **Gestalt shapes**: shape named spontaneously vs not detected even when asked: 93% vs 1% (UK/US, N = 304), 10% vs 78% (villages, N = 93). **Café wall**: lines judged angled by 90% (UK/US, N = 304), 81% (Opuwo, N = 133), 40% (villages, N = 96). A brightness-contrast illusion was reported by 81%, 96% and 100% (ceiling in villages). The authors call these "categorical differences" in visual phenomenology and say the design avoids the translation and sample-size problems A&F flagged; caveats: preprint, UK/US participants did it on a screen and Namibian participants on a laminated sheet (about 20 cm per side), the authors themselves suggest the Coffer effect "is connected with attentional priors", and whether such differences are in perception or in attention is open. — PsyArXiv doi:10.31234/osf.io/gxzcp (v3 text read; funding: John Templeton Foundation / Templeton World Charity Foundation)
- [~] A mechanism hypothesis in response (2026): von der Heydt proposes that people in a "carpentered world" develop mechanisms that exploit linear perspective for space perception, comparing this speculatively to the word-form area that reading creates; it takes the Kroupin et al. reports as its starting point. — *Perception* 2026, doi:10.1177/03010066261469794 (abstract read; theory, not data)
- [V] Older Himba–urban results on *other* illusions (they show strength moving with environment, not an illusion created): traditional Himba had a stronger local bias (weaker Ebbinghaus illusion) than Japanese and British participants, reduced after even a brief visit to a town and more reduced in permanently urbanized Himba — Caparos et al. 2012, *Cognition* 122(1):80–85, doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2011.08.013; Himba showed enhanced simultaneous lightness contrast but reduced White's illusion vs urban Himba and Westerners — Linnell et al. 2018, *JEP: HPP* 44(1):2–6, doi:10.1037/xhp0000498 (abstracts read). A&F read these as the environment "pushing around" an illusion that remains present, and note the town-visit effect was correlational.
- GRADE D: STUDY the Müller-Lyer illusion is created by carpentered environments / absent where there are none — original: Segall, Campbell & Herskovits 1963/1966 (16 samples, 1,878 participants in A&F's table; San PSE ≈ 1%, US ≈ 19–21%). Later evidence: Amir & Firestone 2025 (exclusions up to over half a sample, interpreters, experimenter expectations, contrary datasets, no systematic measure of carpentry), sight-restored children (Gandhi 2015), congenitally blind haptic data (Heller 2002), natural-scene statistics (Howe & Purves 2005), many animal species. As of Oct 2026 no modern, preregistered cross-society Müller-Lyer replication exists that I could find; the replies so far use other illusions.
- GRADE C: STUDY the *strength* of some geometric illusions varies with population and urban experience (Ebbinghaus, lightness, café wall, Coffer) — original: Caparos et al. 2012; Bremner et al. 2016 (N = 336); Linnell et al. 2018. Later evidence: Kroupin et al. 2025 preprint (large online/in-person samples, large percentages, no Müller-Lyer). The direction is repeatedly found in one research network (Himba/Namibia); effect sizes, mechanism (attention vs perception) and independent replication are the open parts.

### C · 公平博弈 fairness games across small-scale societies ★ (ultimatum, dictator games)
- [def] 最后通牒博弈 Ultimatum Game: a proposer offers a share of a real sum to an anonymous responder, who accepts (both get paid) or rejects (both get nothing); a purely self-interested responder accepts any positive offer, so a self-interested proposer offers the minimum. 独裁者博弈 Dictator Game: same, but the responder cannot reject, so offers measure fairness without the threat of punishment. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.2 (full text read)
- [V] Industrialized samples (mostly undergraduates in the US, Europe, Asia): proposers typically offer **40–50% with a modal offer of 50%**; offers below about **30% are often rejected** (Camerer 2003, as cited); in Henrich et al. 2001's summary, mean offers in industrial societies are close to **44%**, and offers below 20% were rejected with probability **0.40–0.60**. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.2; Henrich et al. 2001, *American Economic Review* 91(2):73–78, doi:10.1257/aer.91.2.73 (Table 1 and text read)
- [V] ★ **Mean ultimatum-game offers (fraction of the pie) in the 15 small-scale societies of Henrich et al. 2001, Table 1**: Machiguenga (Peru) **0.26** (modes 0.15 and 0.25, together 72% of offers; only 1 of 21 offers rejected, 0.048); Hadza big camp 0.40 / small camp 0.27; Tsimane 0.37; Quichua 0.27; Torguud 0.35; Khazax 0.36; Mapuche 0.34; Au 0.43; Gnau 0.38; Sangu farmers 0.41 / herders 0.42; unresettled villagers 0.41 / resettled villagers 0.45; Achuar 0.42; Orma 0.44; Aché **0.51**; Lamalera **0.58** (includes experimenter-generated low offers). Text: mean offers ranged from 26% to 58% against ~44% in industrial samples, and **sample modes ranged from 15% to 50%** (50% in industrialized societies). — Henrich et al. 2001 (Table 1, text read)
- [V] **Rejections do not follow a single rule**: no rejections after 16, 51 and 70 offers among the Achuar, Aché and Tsimane; the Machiguenga rejected only one offer though over 75% of their offers were below 30%; the Hadza rejected 24% of all offers and **43% of offers ≤ 20%**; the Au and Gnau rejected both **unfair and hyper-fair** offers. — Henrich et al. 2001 (text read)
- [V] ★ **Henrich et al. 2005 (ultimatum, public-goods and dictator games in 15 small-scale societies)**: (1) the canonical self-interest model "fails in all of the societies studied"; (2) "substantially more behavioral variability across social groups" than in previous research; (3) group-level differences in economic organization and the structure of social interaction explain a substantial portion — "the higher the degree of market integration and the higher the payoffs to cooperation in everyday life, the greater the level of prosociality expressed in experimental games"; (4) available individual-level economic and demographic variables do **not** consistently explain game behavior within or across groups; (5) in many cases experimental play appears to reflect common interactional patterns of everyday life. — *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 28(6):795–815, doi:10.1017/S0140525X05000142 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Phase 2 (15 societies, thousands of subjects across the two phases from 23 societies)**: for Dictator Game offers the **US adult non-student sample had the highest mean offer**, followed by the Sanquianga of Colombia; US offers were "nearly double" those of the **Hadza and Tsimane**; for Ultimatum Game offers the US had the **second highest** mean offer (behind the Sursurunga of Papua New Guinea); the US income-maximizing offer was tied with the Sursurunga's, "five times higher than 70% of the other societies"; the authors say none of this shows industrialized people are "entirely unique" but that they "consistently occupy the extreme end of the human distribution". — Henrich et al. 2010 BBS §3.2, Fig. 3 (full text read)
- [V] **What predicted the variation**: across **15 diverse populations**, **market integration** (percentage of purchased calories) positively covaried with fairness, **community size** positively covaried with punishment, and **participation in a world religion** was associated with fairness "although not across all measures" — read by the authors as showing that "modern prosociality is not solely the product of an innate psychology, but also reflects norms and institutions that have emerged over the course of human history". — Henrich, Ensminger, McElreath et al. 2010, *Science* 327(5972):1480–1484, doi:10.1126/science.1182238 (abstract read; erratum *Science* 332(6036):1381, 2011)
- [~] Cautions for the lesson: results are for *real money, one-shot, anonymous* games, with stakes and framing set locally; "unfair" offers are not necessarily rejected because rejection is costly or locally meaningless; and the explanation (market integration/world religion) is correlational at the level of 15 populations. Do not turn "mean offer 26% vs 44%" into a statement about individuals — the sample modes (15% vs 50%) and the Machiguenga's 1-in-21 rejection rate are population-level patterns in particular samples.
- GRADE B: STUDY ultimatum-game fairness norms vary with society — original: Henrich et al. 2001/2005 (15 small-scale societies; mean offers 26%–58% vs 44% in industrial samples). Later evidence: Phase 2 (Henrich et al. 2010 *Science*, 15 populations: markets, religion, community size). Direction (large between-society variation, WEIRD at the high-fairness end) reliable and consistently reported by the same research network; independent replication by non-affiliated teams of the *explanations* I did not find.

### D · 跨文化再现 Many Labs 2: what happened to famous cultural contrasts when tested in 36 countries ★
- [V] ★ **Design and overall**: Klein et al. 2018 replicated **28 findings in 125 samples from 36 countries and territories (N = 15,305)**; **15 (54%)** replicated at p < .05 (14, or 50%, at p < .0001); the median effect size was **d = 0.60 originally vs 0.15 in replication**; **21 (75%)** replications were smaller than the original and **9 (32%)** went in the opposite direction. — Klein et al. 2018, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science* 1(4):443–490, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Cultural moderation was small**: the intraclass correlation of samples across effects was **.782** and of effects across samples **.004**: "to predict effect sizes across the 28 findings and dozens of samples … it is very useful to know the effect in question and barely useful to know the sample"; heterogeneity (Q) was significant for 11 effects (39%) but only **1 effect had tau > .20**; for **13 of the 14 reliable same-direction effects** the magnitude was similar in WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples; the exception was **Huang et al. 2014** (cardinal direction and perceived socioeconomic status: effect in WEIRD, none in less-WEIRD samples). The authors report only "occasional evidence for cultural variation" and that a priori predictions of moderators were "minimally successful". — Klein et al. 2018 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **East Asian–Western contrasts in the replication project that did NOT replicate as originally reported**: (a) **Norenzayan et al. 2002 formal vs intuitive reasoning**: original d = 0.00 for European Americans and d = 0.67 for East Asians; replication aggregate N = 7,396, pooled d = 0.86 [0.81, 0.91], **WEIRD d = 0.95 vs less-WEIRD d = 0.56** — the "belonging-to → more rule use" pattern replicated everywhere, the cultural pattern did not appear as reported; (b) **Savani et al. 2010 (construing actions as choices)**: original d = −0.65 for Indians vs +0.04 for Americans; replication whole-sample d = −0.24; in **Indian university labs (N = 122) d = −0.03** — the cultural asymmetry was not reproduced; (c) correspondence bias (Miyamoto & Kitayama 2002) — see lesson 12 (A2). — Klein et al. 2018 (full text read)
- GRADE B: STUDY WEIRD-vs-less-WEIRD moderation of well-known effects — original: dozens of "culture × effect" claims. Later evidence: Many Labs 2 (36 countries, N = 15,305): most reliable effects similar in size across WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples (13 of 14); effects that were culture-specific in the original often did not reproduce the original pattern (Norenzayan 2002, Savani 2010). A substantial, informative negative result for "culture changes everything" and for "nothing varies".

### F · 空间参照系 spatial frames of reference (ADDED; Henrich et al. 2010 §3.4, cross-link to lesson 13 Whorf)
- [def] 自我中心 (egocentric / relative) frame: location relative to the viewer ("the man is on the right side of the flagpole"); 地理 / 绝对 (geocentric / absolute) frame: cardinal or landscape directions ("the man is west of the house"); 物体中心 (intrinsic) frame: anchored to the object ("the man is behind the house"). The last two are the paper's "two flavors" of allocentric frame. — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.4 (full text read)
- [V] ★ **Language survey**: in data from **20 languages** from foragers, horticulturalists, agriculturalists and industrialized populations, "only three languages relied on egocentric frames as their single preferred system of reference. All three were from industrialized populations: Japanese, English, and Dutch" (the paper's citation: Majid et al. 2004). — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.4 (full text read; I read only the abstract of Majid, Bowerman, Kita, Haun & Levinson 2004, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 8(3):108–114, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2004.01.003, which says frames of reference "have long been thought of as innate concepts" yet "the use of such frames in language, cognition and gesture varies cross-culturally, and … children can acquire different systems with comparable ease")
- [V] **Rotation task (the standard test)**: Dutch and Tzeltal speakers sat at a table and saw an arrow pointing right (north) or left (south); rotated 180° to a second table with two arrows, they chose which "is like the one they saw before"; "Dutch speakers chose the relative solution, whereas the Tzeltal speakers chose the absolute solution". The paper adds that "lively debates about interpretation persist". — Henrich et al. 2010 §3.4 (full text read; per-group N not given there)
- [V] ★ **Across ages and species (Haun et al. 2006)**: Dutch-speaking adults and 8-year-olds showed the egocentric bias, Hai//om-speaking (Namibian foragers, allocentric language) adults and 8-year-olds the allocentric bias; 4-year-old German-speaking children and gorillas, orangutans, chimpanzees and bonobos, on a simplified version, "all showed a marked preference for allocentric reasoning". The PNAS abstract: spatial cognition "systematically varies with language and culture but … there is a clear inherited bias for one spatial strategy in the great apes", so "language and culture mask the native tendencies in our species". — Haun, Rapold, Call, Janzen & Levinson 2006, *PNAS* 103(46):17568–17573, doi:10.1073/pnas.0607999103 (abstract read; the allocentric/ape details from the BBS paper's summary; sample sizes not obtained)
- [~] A body-movement version: 8-year-old Hai//om children kept a learned dance in absolute (north–south–east–west) coordinates after a 180° rotation, a matched German sample in egocentric left/right coordinates (as summarized in the commentary by Majid & Levinson 2010, *BBS* 33(2–3):103, doi:10.1017/S0140525X1000018X (author-hosted full text read; page from Crossref); original: Haun & Rapold 2009, *Current Biology* 19(23):R1068–R1069, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2009.10.041 — record confirmed, no abstract available, original not opened)
- [V] **The dissent**: Li & Gleitman 2002 "reproduce[d] these different problem-solving strategies in speakers of a single language (English) by manipulating landmark cues, suggesting that language itself may not be the key causal factor in choice of spatial perspective" — *Cognition* 83(3):265–294, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(02)00009-4 (abstract read). So the *cultural variation* is well documented, while *language as its cause* is contested (see lesson 13 Whorf section).
- [~] WEIRD lesson from the paper: with only US and European subjects "one might conclude that children start off with an allocentric bias but naturally shift to an egocentric bias with maturation" — a conclusion that "would not apply to many human populations". Treat as an example of a developmental claim that depends on whom you sample.
- [V] Two later studies: (a) 4-year-old English-speaking children, in a word-learning paradigm, "interpreted ambiguous spatial terms as having environment-based meanings akin to north and south" and learned north–south words readily, but struggled with left/right on a doll — "the first direct evidence that children invoke geocentric representations in spatial language acquisition" (Shusterman & Li 2016, *Cognitive Psychology* 88:115–161, doi:10.1016/j.cogpsych.2016.06.001; abstract read; N not in the abstract); (b) in an online virtual-navigation task with **1,823 participants**, cultural background "significantly influenced reference frame selection": "the majority of North-Americans preferred an allocentric strategy, while Latin-Americans preferred an egocentric navigation strategy", with European and Asian groups in between, and neither age nor gender mattered (Goeke et al. 2015, *Scientific Reports* 5:11426, doi:10.1038/srep11426; abstract read). My observation: (b) is a navigation preference, not a language measure, and its direction for North Americans is not what the "English = egocentric" story alone would predict, so navigation strategies and linguistic frames should not be treated as one variable.
- GRADE C: STUDY language/culture-linked spatial frames of reference (allocentric vs egocentric preferences) — original: Levinson and colleagues' rotation tasks (Tzeltal, Guugu Yimithirr, Dutch), Haun et al. 2006 (humans + apes). Later evidence: the cross-group pattern replicates across several tasks, but Li & Gleitman 2002 show the strategy shifts with landmark cues within English speakers; causal role of language is the debated part; per-group sample sizes not obtained here and I did not find a large preregistered replication.
### F · 认知风格：整体 vs 分析 holistic vs analytic cognition ★ (East Asian–Western contrasts, with effect-size caution)
- [def] 整体性 vs 分析性认知 holistic vs analytic cognition (Nisbett & Miyamoto 2005): "Westerners tend to engage in context-independent and analytic perceptual processes by focusing on a salient object independently of its context, whereas Asians tend to engage in context-dependent and holistic perceptual processes by attending to the relationship between the object and the context"; the authors say perception "can no longer be regarded as consisting of processes that are universal across all people at all times". (A claim about *group tendencies*, not about every individual.) — *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 9(10):467–473, doi:10.1016/j.tics.2005.08.004 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Masuda & Nisbett 2001 (fish scenes)**: Japanese and Americans watched animated underwater vignettes and reported the contents, then did a recognition test of old and new objects in original or novel settings; Study 2 replicated recognition with wildlife photographs. The Japanese (a) made more statements about **contextual information and relationships** than Americans and (b) recognized previously seen objects **more accurately in their original settings** than in novel ones, a manipulation that had "relatively little effect on Americans". The widely repeated "60% more background statements" figure is **not in the abstract and I could not open the paper** (no open copy) — do not quote a percentage. — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 81(5):922–934, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.81.5.922 (abstract read)
- [V] **Eye movements**: American and Chinese participants viewed photographs with a focal object on a complex background: Americans **fixated focal objects more and looked at them sooner**; the Chinese made **more saccades to the background**. — Chua, Boland & Nisbett 2005, *PNAS* 102(35):12629–12633, doi:10.1073/pnas.0506162102 (abstract read; N not in abstract)
- [V] **Framed-line test**: draw a line identical to the first in absolute length or in proportion to its surrounding frame — Japanese were more accurate on the *relative* task, Americans on the *absolute* task, and "when engaging in another culture, individuals tended to show the cognitive characteristic common in the host culture". — Kitayama, Duffy, Kawamura & Larsen 2003, *Psychological Science* 14(3):201–206, doi:10.1111/1467-9280.02432 (abstract read; two studies, N not in abstract)
- [V] ★ **Infancy (as of 2025)**: eye-tracking of **15-month-olds in Japan (n = 45) and the US (n = 52)**: Japanese infants looked at the background about **twice as long** as US infants in cartoon videos, and Japanese parents referred to the background more in book reading (uncorrelated with infant behavior) — Heise, Meristo, Ueno, Itakura & Carlson 2025, "Cultural Differences in Visual Attention Emerge in Infancy", *Infancy* 30(1):e12651, doi:10.1111/infa.12651 (abstract read). A **pre-registered** study of **9-month-olds in Uppsala (Sweden) and Harare (Zimbabwe), n = 338**, found attention allocation differs between the two cultural contexts, but "each culture has a unique pervasive, and dynamic attentional style that includes a mix of holistic and analytic components" — Tu, van den Berg, Astor, Mhizha & Gredebäck 2025, *Infancy* e70063, doi:10.1111/infa.70063 (abstract read).
- [V] ★ **The critical result (Na et al. 2010)**: in a large-scale study with **10 measures of independent vs interdependent social orientation and 10 measures of analytic vs holistic cognitive style**, the measures distinguished the groups (cultures and social classes) at the group level but "the correlations among the measures were negligible", with similar results for the cognitive measures: "no coherent individual differences despite the validity of the construct at the group level". Title: "Cultural differences are not always reducible to individual differences". **Correction to the task file**: the paper does *not* say "cultural differences are small"; it says group-level differences do not necessarily have counterparts as individual-difference dimensions. — Na, Grossmann, Varnum, Kitayama, Gonzalez & Nisbett 2010, *PNAS* 107(14):6192–6197, doi:10.1073/pnas.1001911107 (abstract read)
- [V] Measurement problem in a second dataset: a pre-registered validation of **six** instruments for analytic/holistic style (**N = 392** Czech participants, two waves) found rod-and-frame methods "may be unreliable", recommended embedded- and hierarchical-figure tasks, said the self-report questionnaire had an "unsatisfactory factor structure", and found the results "did not correspond with the original two-dimensional theory". — Lacko, Prošek, Čeněk et al. 2023, *PLoS ONE* 18(6):e0287057, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0287057 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Pre-registered 11-country test (2025)**: **993 university students in 11 countries** (Armenia, Australia, Brazil, Bulgaria, Czechia, Germany, Ghana, Philippines, Slovakia, Taiwan, Türkiye) did three perceptual tasks (Navon hierarchical figures, Gottschaldt embedded figures) analysed with reaction-time models. Result: "notable cross-cultural variations in cognitive styles, though these differences did not align with predictions from analytic/holistic cognitive style theory"; "countries traditionally characterized as more holistic or analytic did not consistently show the expected cognitive style patterns"; among country-level predictors (Hofstede, Schwartz) individualism and long-term orientation were associated with both analytic and holistic thinking while many predictors had no significant effect; the authors say the findings "challenge the straightforward application of analytic/holistic theory across diverse cultural contexts". Note that the list contains no US, Japanese, Chinese or Korean sample, so it is not a direct re-test of the classic US–East Asia contrast. — Lacko, Čeněk, Arıkan, Dresler, Galang, Stachoň, Šašinková, Tsai, Prošek, Ugwitz & Šašinka 2025, *J Exp Psychol: General* 154(2):325–346, doi:10.1037/xge0001685 (abstract read)
- [V] Within-China variation: rice-growing southern China is "more interdependent and holistic-thinking than the wheat-growing north" in **1,162 Han Chinese participants at six sites**, with differences "just as large" between neighboring counties along the rice–wheat border; modernization and pathogen-prevalence accounts "do not fit the data" — Talhelm et al. 2014, *Science* 344(6184):603–608, doi:10.1126/science.1246850 (abstract read; a Frontiers commentary by Roberts, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00950, and critical papers exist: Ruan, Xie & Zhang 2015, *Food Policy* 56:51–58, doi:10.1016/j.foodpol.2015.07.010 and a 2018 *Acta Psychologica Sinica* 50:572 "Questioning the Rice Theory", doi:10.3724/SP.J.1041.2018.00572 — I could not read their abstracts, so I do not report their findings). Field follow-up: **8,964 people in cafés in six cities** (northerners more often sat alone) and a chair-blocking experiment (**n = 678**; northerners more often moved the chair, southerners squeezed through) — Talhelm, Zhang & Oishi 2018, *Science Advances* 4(11):eaap8469, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aap8469 (abstract read).
- [V] **Rice farming and norm tightness**: historically rice-farming areas of China had tighter social norms than wheat areas "even beyond differences in development and urbanization"; rice–wheat differences were "just as large" among people (n = 3,835) in **10 neighboring provinces** along the rice–wheat border; outside China, rice farming predicted norm tightness in **32 countries**; people in rice areas scored lower on innovative thinking. Observational regional and national correlations, from the group that proposed the rice theory. — Talhelm & English 2020, *PNAS* 117(33):19816–19824, doi:10.1073/pnas.1909909117 (abstract read)
- GRADE B: STUDY East Asian–Western group differences in attention to context — original: Masuda & Nisbett 2001; Kitayama et al. 2003; Chua et al. 2005 (Japan/China vs US; recognition, line-drawing, eye movements). Later evidence: 2025 infant eye-tracking in Japan–US (n = 45 + 52), Sweden–Zimbabwe (n = 338, pre-registered); but the 2025 pre-registered 11-country study (N = 993) found cultural variation that did not match the theory's predictions. Direction repeatedly found across tasks and ages in East Asia–US comparisons, but sample sizes per study are modest, effect sizes are rarely reported in abstracts, the broader theory's country-level predictions did not hold in Lacko et al. 2025, and the reasoning-style cousin (Norenzayan 2002) did not replicate as reported in Many Labs 2.
- GRADE C: STUDY "holistic vs analytic" as a *dimension on which individuals can be placed*, and as a country-level ranking — Na et al. 2010 (negligible correlations among 10 + 10 measures), the 2023 validation (N = 392), and the 2025 pre-registered 11-country study (N = 993), where country patterns did not match the theory. Some group-level differences in attention have support (Chua 2005; infant studies); the individual-level scale and the clean country-level ordering do not.

### G · 个人主义–集体主义与自我建构 individualism–collectivism and self-construal ★
- [def] 独立型 vs 互依型自我建构 independent vs interdependent self-construal: "People in different cultures have strikingly different construals of the self, of others, and of the interdependence of the 2"; Asian conceptions "insist on the fundamental relatedness of individuals to each other", American culture "neither assumes nor values such an overt connectedness"; the paper proposes these construals shape cognition, emotion and motivation. — Markus & Kitayama 1991, *Psychological Review* 98(2):224–253, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.98.2.224 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Oyserman, Coon & Kemmelmeier 2002 meta-analysis** (title: "Rethinking individualism and collectivism"): "European Americans were found to be both more individualistic … and less collectivistic … than others. However, European Americans were **not more individualistic than African Americans, or Latinos, and not less collectivistic than Japanese or Koreans**. Among Asians, only **Chinese** showed large effects, being both less individualistic and more collectivistic." Moderate IND-COL effects on self-concept and relationality, and "large effects … on attribution and cognitive style". — *Psychological Bulletin* 128(1):3–72, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.1.3 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **The reference-group effect (Heine et al. 2002)**: "Whereas cultural experts agreed that East Asians are more collectivistic than North Americans, cross-cultural comparisons of trait and attitude measures failed to reveal such a pattern"; manipulating reference groups enhanced the expected differences; and "people from different cultural backgrounds within the same country exhibited larger differences than did people from different countries" (Study 3). Conclusion: cross-cultural comparisons using subjective Likert scales "are compromised because of different reference groups" (people rate themselves against local standards). — *J Pers Soc Psychol* 82(6):903–918, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.82.6.903 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Japan vs US**: a review of **35 empirical studies** comparing Japanese and Americans on individualism/collectivism (15 + 20 later ones; 7 behavioral and 13 questionnaire studies in the second batch) found **19 with no clear difference, 11 in which the Japanese were *more* individualistic**, and only **5 supporting** the common view "Japanese are collectivists, Americans individualists" (even counting 3 questionable ones); the authors conclude the common view "is not valid". — Takano & Osaka 2018, *Asian Journal of Social Psychology* 21(4):301–316, doi:10.1111/ajsp.12322 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Beyond "East vs West"**: across **2 multinational surveys** — Study 1 N = 2,924 students in 16 nations; Study 2 **N = 7,279 adults from 55 cultural groups in 33 nations** — Vignoles et al. developed and validated a **7-dimensional model** of ways of being independent or interdependent; they note that commonly used self-report measures "frequently fail to show predicted cultural differences", that "patterns of global variation support some of Markus and Kitayama's predictions, but a simple contrast between independence and interdependence does not adequately capture the diverse models of selfhood", and that groups differ depending on individualism-collectivism, national socioeconomic development and religious heritage. — Vignoles et al. 2016, *J Exp Psychol: General* 145(8):966–1000, doi:10.1037/xge0000175 (abstract read)
- [V] Priming a "cultural frame": six pre-registered experiments in Hong Kong (N = 369, 434, 330, 565, 451, 522; **2,671 in all**) plus mini meta-analyses "consistently failed to replicate the cultural priming effect" of Hong et al. 1997 (bicultural participants switching between Chinese and Western frames) — *Japanese Psychological Research* 2025, doi:10.1111/jpr.12599 (abstract read). A pre-registered replication of self-construal priming (Gardner, Gabriel & Lee 1999; story and pronoun-circling primes) in **Chinese participants** found it "did not change the relative independence or interdependence of one's self-construal" (PsyArXiv 2021, doi:10.31234/osf.io/u5dxn; preprint; I did not check later publication).
- [V] **Hofstede and its critics**: Hofstede's national-culture dimensions (1980 book *Culture's Consequences*, not opened) derive from survey data "collected in the late 1960s and early 1970s"; McSweeney's critique challenges "crucial assumptions which underlie his claim to have uncovered the secrets of entire national cultures" and questions "the plausibility of systematically causal national cultures" (*Human Relations* 55(1):89–118, doi:10.1177/0018726702551004, abstract read); Minkov & Hofstede 2011 reply by tracing the evolution of the doctrine (*Cross Cultural Management* 18(1):10–20, doi:10.1108/13527601111104269, abstract read). A World Values Survey cohort replication finds contemporary societies score **higher on Individualism and Indulgence and lower on Power Distance** than past societies, but "cultural change is absolute rather than relative" — rank order and cultural distances between country pairs are "generally stable" (Beugelsdijk, Maseland & van Hoorn 2015, *Global Strategy Journal* 5(3):223–240, doi:10.1002/gsj.1098, abstract read).
- [V] **Tight vs loose cultures**: with data from **33 nations**, cultures that are "tight (have many strong norms and a low tolerance of deviant behavior)" vs "loose (have weak social norms and a high tolerance of deviant behavior)" are linked to ecological and historical threats (population density, resource scarcity, territorial conflict, disease) and to socialization in institutions (e.g., autocracy, media regulation); the authors call it "part of a complex, loosely integrated multilevel system". — Gelfand et al. 2011, *Science* 332(6033):1100–1104, doi:10.1126/science.1197754 (abstract read; erratum *Science* 333:937). Ecological follow-up: as of 16 October 2020, nations with high looseness are estimated to have had **4.99× the COVID-19 cases and 8.71× the deaths** (per million) of tight nations after controls — a cross-national *correlation* from OLS regressions, not a causal test — Gelfand et al. 2021, *Lancet Planetary Health* 5(3):e135–e144, doi:10.1016/S2542-5196(20)30301-6 (abstract read).
- [V] **Ideal affect**: controlling for actual affect, European Americans and Asian Americans valued **high-arousal positive affect (excitement)** more than Hong Kong Chinese, while Chinese and Asian Americans valued **low-arousal positive affect (calm)** more than European Americans; for all groups the gap between ideal and actual affect correlated with depression ("culture influences ideal affect more than actual affect"). — Tsai, Knutson & Fung 2006, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 90(2):288–307, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.90.2.288; synthesis in Tsai 2007, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2(3):242–259, doi:10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00043.x (abstracts read; sample sizes not in abstracts)
- GRADE D: STUDY "East Asians (especially Japanese) are collectivists, Americans individualists" as measured by self-report scales — original: Hofstede 1980 / Markus & Kitayama 1991. Later evidence: Takano & Osaka 2018 (only 5 of 35 studies support it), Oyserman 2002 (Americans not more individualistic than African Americans/Latinos nor less collectivistic than Japanese/Koreans), Heine 2002 (reference-group effect), Vignoles 2016 (7 dimensions, not 2). Group *differences* exist in some behaviors, but this national-stereotype formulation does not hold.
- GRADE B: STUDY tight vs loose cultures (norm strength varies systematically with ecological threat) — original: Gelfand et al. 2011 (33 nations). Later evidence: consistent follow-ups from the same network (Gelfand 2021); I did not retrieve an independent replication. Mostly cross-sectional national-level correlations.
- GRADE B: STUDY cultural variation in ideal affect — original: Tsai, Knutson & Fung 2006. Later evidence: Tsai 2007 review; a 2026 *Emotion* paper reports that popular song lyrics emphasize low-arousal positive affect in Japan and high-arousal positive affect in the United States (Morling, Masui & Miyamoto, *Emotion* 26(7):1836–1840, doi:10.1037/emo0001688; I have the title only, not the abstract). Pattern consistent but concentrated in one research network and in self-reported ideals.
- GRADE C: STUDY self-construal as a causal mechanism, malleable by priming — original: Markus & Kitayama 1991 (theory), Hong et al. 1997, Gardner et al. 1999. Later evidence: 2,671 participants across six pre-registered Hong Kong experiments and a pre-registered Chinese replication failed to reproduce priming; Vignoles 2016 finds the 2-factor structure too simple; the stable-difference part of the theory is partly supported (Oyserman: Chinese show large effects), the priming/mechanism part is not.

### H · 文化演化与累积文化 cultural evolution and the "collective brain" ★
- [def] ★ 累积文化 cumulative culture / 文化演化 cultural evolution: knowledge, skills and norms are learned from others, accumulate across generations, and are selected and transmitted with errors — so the product can exceed what any one individual could invent. Standard texts (books, not opened): Boyd & Richerson 1985 *Culture and the Evolutionary Process* (U Chicago Press); Mesoudi 2011 *Cultural Evolution* (U Chicago Press, doi:10.7208/chicago/9780226520452.001.0001); Henrich 2015 *The Secret of Our Success* (Princeton UP); Henrich 2020 *The WEIRDest People in the World* (Farrar, Straus & Giroux). — [def] only
- [V] **The "collective brain"**: innovations are "an emergent property of our species' cultural learning abilities, applied within our societies and social networks"; three sources of innovation (serendipity, recombination, incremental improvement); rates depend on (i) sociality, (ii) transmission fidelity, (iii) cultural variance; the authors also report "preliminary evidence" that languages with more speakers are more efficient. — Muthukrishna & Henrich 2016, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 371(1690):20150192, doi:10.1098/rstb.2015.0192 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **A culturally evolved adaptation with numbers (Fiji)**: food taboos for pregnant and lactating women "selectively target the most toxic marine species, effectively reducing a woman's chances of fish poisoning by **30 per cent during pregnancy and 60 per cent during breastfeeding**"; transmission fits models combining familial transmission with selective learning from locally prestigious individuals; some non-adaptive taboos persist as by-products of cognition. — Henrich & Henrich 2010, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 277(1701):3715–3724, doi:10.1098/rspb.2010.1191 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **The Tasmanian thesis**: archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence "indicates that, over an approximately 8,000-year period" from the beginning of the Holocene to European contact, the societies of Tasmania "lost a series of valuable skills and technologies" — "these likely included" bone tools, cold-weather clothing, hafted tools, nets, fishing spears, barbed spears, spear-throwers and boomerangs; a formal model suggests the drop in effective population size after rising seas isolated Tasmania could keep simple skills stable while complex ones deteriorated. — Henrich 2004, *American Antiquity* 69(2):197–214, doi:10.2307/4128416 (abstract read)
- [V] ★ **Population size → cultural complexity: mixed evidence**. Supportive: **Kline & Boyd 2010** — in Oceania, "islands with small populations had less complicated marine foraging technology" (they note two earlier systematic tests on continental data, without contact rates, found no support) — *Proc R Soc B* 277(1693):2559–2564, doi:10.1098/rspb.2010.0452; **Collard et al. 2013** — in **40** nonindustrial farming and pastoralist groups both richness and complexity of toolkits rose with population size, independent of risk of resource failure — *PLoS ONE* 8(9):e72628, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0072628; **Derex et al. 2013** — in a dual-task computer game "players in larger groups maintained higher cultural complexity" (less deterioration, more improvements, more diversity) — *Nature* 503(7476):389–391, doi:10.1038/nature12774; Powell, Shennan & Thomas 2009 — demography can explain geographic variation in when modern behavior first appears "without invoking increased cognitive capacity" — *Science* 324(5932):1298–1301, doi:10.1126/science.1170165. Critical: **Vaesen et al. 2016** — Henrich's (2004) and Powell's (2009) models "only support a relationship between demography and culture in implausible conditions" and "their predictions conflict with the available archaeological and ethnographic evidence" — *PNAS* 113(16):E2241–E2247, doi:10.1073/pnas.1520288113 (abstracts read).
- [V] **Recent evidence on when and how cumulative culture arose (2024)**: comparing the complexity of stone-tool manufacturing sequences over **3.3 million years** with the complexity achievable without cumulative culture (estimated from non-human primate technologies and knapping experiments), archaeological technologies "become significantly more complex than expected in the absence of cumulative culture only after ~600 kya" — Paige & Perreault 2024, *PNAS* 121(26):e2319175121, doi:10.1073/pnas.2319175121 (abstract read). For chimpanzees, "limited levels of group connectivity favored the emergence of a few instances of cumulative culture", with complexification "likely" happening in steps — Gunasekaram et al. 2024, *Science* 386(6724):920–925, doi:10.1126/science.adk3381 (abstract read). Both are model- and comparison-based inferences, not direct observations of transmission.
- GRADE C: STUDY "population size and connectedness drive technological complexity (Tasmanian loss, Oceania)" — original: Henrich 2004 (model), Powell et al. 2009 (model), Kline & Boyd 2010 (Oceania). Later evidence: Collard 2013 (positive, n = 40 groups), Derex 2013 (lab game, positive) vs Vaesen 2016 (models implausible, predictions conflict with record) and Kline & Boyd's own note of earlier null tests. Active dispute; teach as a hypothesis with a lab demonstration, not a settled cause of any particular society's technology.
- [~] Do not teach technology loss, or low technology, as evidence of lower intelligence: the demographic argument is explicitly about population size and connectedness of "hominin populations with similar cognitive abilities" (Kline & Boyd 2010) and works "without invoking increased cognitive capacity" (Powell et al. 2009).
- [V] **Henrich's historical argument for WEIRD psychology**: the Western Church's transformation of European kinship — "small, nuclear households, weak family ties, and residential mobility" — "fostered greater individualism, less conformity, and more impersonal prosociality"; tested with **24 psychological outcomes**, historical measures of Church exposure and kinship, across countries, European regions and individuals from different cultural backgrounds. — Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp & Henrich 2019, *Science* 366(6466):eaau5141, doi:10.1126/science.aau5141 (abstract read)
- [~] **Critique and extensions of the Church–kinship thesis (all non-peer-reviewed or secondary here)**: Passmore & Watts 2021 (PsyArXiv, doi:10.31234/osf.io/p5wt4; abstract read) review what is known about pre-Church kinship systems in Europe and re-analyse cross-national associations between the Church's Marriage and Family Program and kinship structure with phylogenetic comparative methods, "raise alternative hypotheses about the relationships between the Western Christian Church and kinship structures" and say further research is needed to arbitrate them (their results are not in the abstract; I did not open the full text). Extensions by others: le Bris & Gay 2023 — distance to innovation centers is "strongly associated with kinship intensity", and associated with psychological traits, "especially outside Western Europe in which exposure to the Church seems to play an additional role" (*PLoS ONE* 18(1):e0279864, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0279864, abstract read); Chen, Atari, Slingerland, Hong, Fu, Wang, Schulz, Bol & Henrich 2025 — text analysis of two classical Chinese corpora (770 BCE–1911 CE, 270 prefectures) finds regional variation and non-linear change in psychological measures and "support" for kinship intensity co-evolving with socio-cooperative psychology (PsyArXiv preprint, doi:10.31234/osf.io/27u94_v1, abstract read; same research group as the original).
- GRADE C: STUDY the Church–kinship account of WEIRD psychology — original: Schulz et al. 2019 (24 outcomes; observational, historical exposure measures; correlational by design). Later evidence: a 2021 preprint critique that raises alternative hypotheses about the Church–kinship direction (Passmore & Watts; abstract only), extensions that support a kinship–psychology link in other regions (le Bris & Gay 2023; Chen et al. 2025 preprint, from the original group). I did not find a peer-reviewed independent replication. Interesting and consistent with Henrich et al. 2010 on markets/religion, but causal claims about history are not established by correlations.
- [~] ★ Not verified (book claims, do not use as fact): the *Burke and Wills* expedition (1860–61) as an illustration of how outsiders fail to use local knowledge, and the *manioc/cassava detoxification* story as cultural-evolutionary "wisdom" are narrated in Henrich 2015 (*The Secret of Our Success*), which I did not open; see COULD NOT VERIFY.

### I · 情绪与文化 emotion and culture (cross-reference lessons 9–10 and T3a)
- [V] ★ **Meta-analysis (Elfenbein & Ambady 2002)**: "Emotions were universally recognized at better-than-chance levels"; accuracy was higher when emotions were expressed *and* recognized by members of the same national, ethnic or regional group (an **in-group advantage**), smaller for groups with more exposure to one another; majority-group members judged minority-group members worse than the reverse. — *Psychological Bulletin* 128(2):203–235, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.2.203 (abstract read). [~] Henrich et al. 2010 §3.6 quote the same meta-analysis as a main effect of emotion recognition across populations of 58% of variance against 9% for cultural specificity — second-hand; I did not see the table.
- [V] **Recognition depends on method**: Himba participants (Namibia) and US participants sorted posed facial expressions by emotion; "without cues to emotion concepts, Himba participants did not show the presumed 'universal' pattern, whereas U.S. participants produced a pattern with presumed universal features"; with cues, both groups moved toward the presumed pattern "although substantial cultural variation persisted". — Gendron, Roberson, van der Vyver & Barrett 2014, *Emotion* 14(2):251–262, doi:10.1037/a0036052 (abstract read; N not in abstract)
- [V] **Production/mental representations**: reconstructed mental representations of the six "basic" facial expressions from **30 individuals** (Western and East Asian) show that "Westerners represent each of the six basic emotions with a distinct set of facial movements common to the group, Easterners do not", and that East Asian representations convey intensity through dynamic eye activity — Jack, Garrod, Yu, Caldara & Schyns 2012, *PNAS* 109(19):7241–7244, doi:10.1073/pnas.1200155109 (abstract read). In the other direction, machine-learning analysis of **6 million videos from 144 countries** found that **16 facial expressions** each had distinct associations with a set of social contexts that were "**70% preserved** across 12 world regions", with regions differing in how often expressions were produced — Cowen et al. 2020/2021, *Nature* 589(7841):251–257, doi:10.1038/s41586-020-3037-7 (abstract read).
- [V] **Consensus statement from a systematic review (Barrett et al. 2019)**: people "do sometimes smile when happy, frown when sad, scowl when angry, and so on … more than what would be expected by chance", yet "how people communicate anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise varies substantially across cultures, situations, and even across people within a single situation". — Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 20(1):1–68, doi:10.1177/1529100619832930 (abstract read; erratum 20(3):165–166)
- [V] **Display and regulation rules**: Ekman & Friesen 1969 is the standard citation for the concept of "display rules" (norms about managing expressions; *Semiotica* 1(1):49–98, doi:10.1515/semi.1969.1.1.49 — no abstract available, [def] only; the classic Japan–US stress-film study attributed to Ekman/Friesen 1972 was not retrievable → CANNOT VERIFY). In **23 countries**, cultures emphasizing the maintenance of social order (long-term-oriented, valuing embeddedness and hierarchy) had **higher suppression** scores and a positive reappraisal–suppression correlation, whereas cultures valuing affective autonomy and egalitarianism had lower suppression and a negative correlation; country-level emotion regulation correlated with country-level adjustment indices. — Matsumoto, Yoo & Nakagawa 2008, *J Pers Soc Psychol* 94(6):925–937, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.94.6.925 (abstract read)
- GRADE B: STUDY basic facial expressions are recognized above chance across cultures, with an in-group advantage — original: Ekman & Friesen 1969 and Ekman's later studies (not opened). Later evidence: Elfenbein & Ambady 2002 (meta-analysis), Cowen et al. 2020 (6 million videos, 144 countries), Barrett et al. 2019 (above-chance but highly variable).
- GRADE C: STUDY "six basic emotions have universal, fixed facial signals and are universally recognized as discrete categories" — Gendron 2014 (Himba sorting), Jack 2012 (30 individuals), Barrett 2019 (review) vs the cross-cultural above-chance recognition. The *fine-grained universality* claim is contested; sample sizes of the dissenting experiments are small.

### J · 跨文化可重复性的快照 replicability across cultures: one or two numbers per classic (cross-reference lesson 12)
- [V] **Asch conformity**: 133 line-judgment studies from 17 countries — US conformity **declined since the 1950s**, and collectivism scores were significantly related to conformity across countries (Bond & Smith 1996, *Psychological Bulletin* 119(1):111–137, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.119.1.111; see lesson 12 B1).
- [V] **Milgram obedience**: full-obedience rates in Milgram-type studies ranged **from 28% to 91%** across studies and countries, with **no trend over time** (Spearman rs = .002; Blass 1999, doi:10.1111/j.1559-1816.1999.tb00134.x; see lesson 12 B2).
- [V] **Fundamental attribution error**: Miller 1984 (Hindu Indians vs Americans, ages 8–15 and adults): at older ages Americans cited dispositions more and context less than Hindus; Many Labs 2 did not reproduce the US–Japan gap in the essay-attitude paradigm (see lesson 12 A2). — Miller 1984, *JPSP* 46(5):961–978, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.961 (abstract read); Klein et al. 2018 (full text read)
- [V] **Moral Machine**: 40 million decisions from 233 countries and territories, with substantial between-country variation and three country clusters; respondents were a self-selected internet sample (Awad et al. 2018, doi:10.1038/s41586-018-0637-6; see lesson 12 C6).
- [V] **Fairness games**: mean ultimatum offers 26%–58% across 15 small-scale societies (Henrich et al. 2001); **endowment effect** absent among isolated Hadza (Apicella et al. 2014); **Müller-Lyer** PSE ≈ 1% (San) to ≈ 19–21% (US students/children) in the 1960s data — **contested since 2025** (Amir & Firestone: exclusions, interpreters, contrary datasets; the illusion also appears in newly sighted children, congenitally blind touch observers and many animals; sections B and B-update above).
- [V] **Many Labs 2 (36 countries)**: 13 of 14 reliable effects had similar sizes in WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples; the culture-specific patterns of Norenzayan 2002 and Savani 2010 did not reproduce as originally reported (section D above). — Klein et al. 2018, *AMPPS* 1(4):443–490, doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 (full text read)
- [V] **Child-directed language across societies** (cross-reference lesson 13): day-long recordings of **1,001 children from 12 countries** found maternal education unrelated to children's speech production over ages 2–48 months, and adult talk in the environment predicting it (Bergelson et al. 2023, *PNAS*, doi:10.1073/pnas.2300671120); a review of **29 reports (1,314 infants)** found infant-directed vocalization in **12.6% of observations in urban vs 3.6% in rural** settings outside North America, but **16.4% vs 15.1% in high- vs low-SES US families** (Cristia 2023, *Dev Sci*, doi:10.1111/desc.13265) — the review benchmarks the urban–rural gap against US SES variation, which it calls "much smaller", and notes that "published research represents a biased sample of the world's populations". (abstracts read)

### L · 怎样读一条跨文化结论 how to read a cross-cultural claim — checklist (each item has a source in this file)
- [V] ★ 1. **Is the measure equivalent across groups?** Common self-report scales "frequently fail to show predicted cultural differences" (Vignoles et al. 2016, N = 7,279 in 55 cultural groups); in a validation of six analytic/holistic instruments (N = 392) the rod-and-frame tasks "may be unreliable" and the questionnaire's factor structure was unsatisfactory (Lacko et al. 2023). Ask: was the instrument validated in *each* group?
- [V] ★ 2. **Reference-group effect**: people rate themselves against local standards, so Likert-scale means are not comparable across cultures — "cultural experts agreed that East Asians are more collectivistic than North Americans" yet trait and attitude scales "failed to reveal such a pattern"; behavioral or manipulated-reference designs are safer. — Heine et al. 2002
- [V] ★ 3. **Who exactly was sampled?** Convenience samples (students in one city) stand for a "nation" only by assumption: 67% of American samples in JPSP were psychology undergraduates (Arnett 2008); the WEIRD gap persists (≈ 60% American authors/samples in 2014–2018; majority-world samples 4–5%, Thalmayer et al. 2021). Ask for N, age, education, urbanization.
- [V] 4. **Nation ≠ culture**: within-country differences can exceed between-country differences ("people from different cultural backgrounds within the same country exhibited larger differences than did people from different countries", Heine et al. 2002 Study 3); rice-vs-wheat differences *within China* are claimed to be as large as national contrasts (Talhelm et al. 2014); a "national culture" score is an average with a wide spread (McSweeney 2002 questions "systematically causal national cultures").
- [V] 5. **Group-level ≠ individual-level**: a valid difference between group means need not give a usable individual-difference dimension (Na et al. 2010: negligible correlations among 10 + 10 measures); a mean difference says little about any particular person.
- [V] ★ 6. **Report effect size, N and year — and check the replication**: in Many Labs 2 (36 countries, N = 15,305) the median effect shrank from d = 0.60 to 0.15 and culture-specific patterns such as Norenzayan 2002 (original East Asian d = 0.67 vs European American 0.00) did not reproduce as reported; cultural priming failed in six pre-registered Hong Kong experiments (N = 2,671); the Müller-Lyer numbers come from 1960s fieldwork whose exclusions (more than half of some samples), interpreters and experimenter expectations were re-examined in 2025 (Amir & Firestone, *Psychological Review*), and the method itself changes magnitudes (Wincza et al. 2024).
- [V] 7. **Mechanism vs correlation**: market integration, religion, rice farming, Church kinship and population size are *correlates* in a handful (15–55) of populations or ecological regressions — plausible causes, not demonstrated ones; cultural-evolution models (Tasmania) are contested (Vaesen et al. 2016).

### [✗] 文化刻板印象与常见误传 myths and over-readings (lesson 14)
- [✗] "Collectivist cultures don't have individuals / East Asians can't see the focal object (and Americans can't see context)." What was found: *differences in tendencies and speed* — Americans fixated focal objects more and sooner; Chinese made more saccades to the background (Chua et al. 2005); Japanese recognized objects better in their original setting while the manipulation had "relatively little effect" on Americans (Masuda & Nisbett 2001); the individual-level scales do not hold together (Na et al. 2010); even in the Müller-Lyer data every society's PSE is a mean with overlapping distributions.
- [✗] "Japanese are collectivists and Americans individualists" (as measured by self-report). Only 5 of 35 comparisons support it; 11 found the Japanese *more* individualistic (Takano & Osaka 2018); Americans were not less collectivistic than Japanese or Koreans in the 2002 meta-analysis (Oyserman et al.).
- [✗] Both textbook versions of the Müller-Lyer story are overstated. (a) "Some peoples (the San) do not see it at all because they lack carpentered environments" (the classic cultural-byproduct claim): the San PSE "cannot be distinguished from zero" in Segall et al.'s data as shown by Henrich et al. 2010, but that dataset excluded more than half of the respondents in some samples, relied on interpreters, carries reported experimenter-expectation problems and conflicts with contemporaneous results (Amir & Firestone 2025), and newly sighted children, congenitally blind touch observers and many animal species show the effect (section B-update, lesson 14). (b) "It is a universal, hard-wired illusion and culture never affects perception": culture-linked differences in the strength of *other* illusions (Ebbinghaus, lightness contrast) are reported (Caparos 2012; Linnell 2018; Bremner 2016) and a 2025 preprint reports large rural–urban differences on four other stimuli (Kroupin et al.), though none for Müller-Lyer; magnitudes also depend on method and age (Wincza et al. 2024). Teach: "the cross-cultural Müller-Lyer data are weak; the question of how much experience tunes perception is open".
- [✗] "Small-scale societies are more selfish/less fair than the West" or "the West is the fairest": mean ultimatum offers among 15 small-scale societies ran **from 26% to 58%** around ~44% in industrial samples; two societies (Aché 51%, Lamalera 58%) offered *more* than industrial samples; at the same time the US sample had the highest *Dictator Game* offers in Phase 2. The pattern tracks market integration, religion and community size, not "human nature differences" (Henrich et al. 2001, 2010).
- [✗] "Tasmanian Aboriginal people lost technology because they were less intelligent." The model attributes loss to the *demography* of an isolated population (Henrich 2004), and even that account is disputed (Vaesen et al. 2016); the broader argument explicitly concerns populations "with similar cognitive abilities" (Kline & Boyd 2010) and works "without invoking increased cognitive capacity" (Powell et al. 2009).
- [✗] "Hofstede's national scores are fixed traits." Scores come from late-1960s/early-1970s survey data; countries change absolutely over cohorts (more individualism, less power distance) while relative positions are stable (Beugelsdijk et al. 2015); McSweeney 2002 challenges the idea of systematically causal national cultures.
- [✗] "Facial expressions of six basic emotions are produced and read the same way everywhere." Recognition is above chance across cultures with an in-group advantage (Elfenbein & Ambady 2002; Cowen et al. 2020's 70% preserved context associations), but communication of the six emotions "varies substantially across cultures, situations, and even across people within a single situation" (Barrett et al. 2019).
- [✗] Language to avoid in lessons: "primitive", "backward", "savage" for small-scale societies; "the Chinese/Japanese/Americans think …" without "on average", N and year; "WEIRD" as an insult. Use "small-scale societies", "industrialized samples", named groups.
