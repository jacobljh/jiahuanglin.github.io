# FACTS_LEAD — grade keys assigned by the lead from facts that already sit in the other FACTS files

Purpose: build_grades.py harvests every `STUDY … GRADE X` block under facts/. Where a research agent reported a fact but did not
write an explicit STUDY/GRADE line (or the item sits across two files), the lead writes the block here, citing the FACTS section it
rests on. Nothing here introduces a new fact: every number is copied from the FACTS file named at the end of the block.
Grade rules: BIBLE §4 (A 稳健 · B 成立但有限定 · C 有争议 · D 站不住; current evidence, not fame).

- STUDY Dress survey (Lafer-Sousa 2015) — original: Lafer-Sousa, Hermann & Conway 2015, N = 1,401 (mostly online: 1,190 US, 161 India, 50 other countries; NOT representative): 57.0 % blue/black, 30.3 % white/gold, 10.6 % blue/brown, 2.1 % other; some people flip on retest; with the dress embedded in warm/cool-light scenes 64 % of white/gold perceivers switched under the warm scene and 80 % of blue/black perceivers switched under the cool scene. Later evidence: Wallisch 2017 (~13,000 online observers): assumptions about illumination predict the percept much more strongly than age or gender; other labs in the same journal volume support the illuminant-prior account.
  GRADE B: the split and the context effect are robust; the exact percentages describe one sample, and why people hold different illuminant priors (circadian type?) is correlational and unsettled. — FACTS_T1 §00 "The Dress"

- STUDY retina counts — original: Curcio, Sloan, Kalina & Hendrickson 1990 (8 whole-mount retinas from 7 donors aged 27–44): 4.6 million cones (4.08–5.29 M) and 92 million rods (77.9–107.3 M); roughly 1 million optic-nerve axons leave each eye (studies list 0.56–1.14 M, 0.8–1.0 M, 1.2 M). Later evidence: the older textbook counts (Osterberg 1935: ≈ 6.4 M cones, 110–125 M rods) differ in detail but agree on the order of magnitude, so photoreceptors outnumber optic-nerve fibres by at least ~100 : 1.
  GRADE A for the order of magnitude (a few million cones, ~100 million rods, ~1 million fibres per eye); exact counts vary by study and person. — FACTS_T1 §00 "The retina in numbers"

- STUDY size constancy — original: Holway & Boring 1941 (five observers, including the authors): deviation from perfect size constancy ≈ 7% with both eyes open, ≈ 19% with one eye, ≈ 92% in darkness behind reduction screens; Emmert's law (1881): perceived size ∝ retinal size × perceived distance. Later evidence: Millard, Sperandio & Chouinard 2025 (N = 20; real objects vs afterimages): with both eyes open both obeyed Emmert's law with ≈ 17% deviation from perfect scaling; monocular and dark conditions deviated more, in the dark ≈ 80% (afterimages looked about 18–20 cm across at any distance).
  GRADE B: the direction (size constancy runs on distance cues and degrades as they are removed) agrees across 80 years; both data sets are small and the percentages depend on the method. — FACTS_T2 §03 "Constancies, the Adelson checker-shadow, and the cost of removing cues"

- STUDY Flicker paradigm detection times (Rensink et al. 1997; Ouattara et al. 2026) — original: Rensink, O'Regan & Clark 1997 (N = 10; image 240 ms, blank 80 ms): mean detection time 10.9 s for marginal-interest changes and 4.7 s for central-interest changes; no-blank control 0.9 s. Later evidence: Ouattara et al. 2026 online replication (N = 69; flicker 11.22 s marginal / 5.79 s central; no-blank 4.33 s overall; overall difference 4.15 s, 95% CI [2.67, 5.62], d = 1.36); the authors attribute the gap between their no-blank time and the original 0.9 s to uncontrolled online viewing conditions.
  GRADE B: the effect (large changes are slow to find under flicker, fast without the blank) reproduced; absolute times depend on the images and viewing conditions. — FACTS_T2 §04 "Change blindness: flicker, mudsplashes, the real-world swap, and what it does and does not show"

- STUDY Failing to report a change is not the same as holding no trace (Simons, Chabris, Schnur & Levin 2002) — original: three real-world change-detection experiments (Consciousness and Cognition 11(1):78–97): people who fail to report a change often do hold a representation of some aspects of the pre-change scene and can "discover" it and report details of the changed object when probed. Later evidence: the strong reading in Rensink, O'Regan & Clark 1997 ("never form a complete, detailed representation") has since been refined by the original authors (Simons & Rensink 2005; Rensink 2018, as reported by Ouattara et al. 2026); related results for inattentional blindness (Nartker et al. 2025, non-noticers above chance) point the same way but are themselves contested.
  GRADE B: the direction (a failure to report is not proof of no trace) is accepted and supported by several lines; I found no independent quantitative replication of the 2002 probing results in the sources read, and what exactly is retained is unsettled. — FACTS_T2 §04 "Change blindness: flicker, mudsplashes, the real-world swap, and what it does and does not show"

- STUDY Lay beliefs about memory vs expert consensus (Simons & Chabris 2011 survey) — original: Simons & Chabris 2011, a representative US telephone survey (1,838 respondents, weighted to a nominal 1,500; 16 memory professors with 10+ years of research and 73 Psychonomic Society members for comparison): 63.0 % agreed that memory works like a video camera, 82.7 % that amnesia means forgetting one's own identity, 77.5 % that unexpected objects generally grab attention, 55.4 % that hypnosis can enhance memory, 47.6 % that memory is permanent, 37.1 % that a single confident eyewitness should be enough to convict; across the six items 60.4 % agreed on average with statements the expert sample almost uniformly rejected, and 1.5 % matched the experts on all six; more education went with fewer wrong answers. Later evidence: none retrieved (single survey).
  GRADE B: a large representative survey, so the picture of lay belief is reliable for that sample and year; the exact percentages depend on item wording and have not been re-measured in the sources read. — FACTS_T2 §05 "Everyday beliefs about memory vs expert consensus (hook material)"

- STUDY Facial movements carry above-chance information about emotion or social intent (accepted by both camps) — original: Ekman & Friesen 1971 (Fore adults chose the intended face 64–100 % of the time, chance ≈ 33 %, with the fear/surprise exception); Elfenbein & Ambady 2002 meta-analysis (emotions recognised across cultures at better-than-chance levels, with an in-group advantage); Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak 2019 (the constructionist-leaning review): "people do sometimes smile when happy, frown when sad, scowl when angry … more than what would be expected by chance", yet expressions vary substantially across cultures, situations and people. Later evidence: Crivelli et al. 2016 (smile → happiness on only 58 % / 56 % of trials in two small-scale societies, other emotions 7–53 %: above chance for some, far from universal for others); Durán & Fernández-Dols 2021 (spontaneous co-occurrence only .13–.23, r = .30 with intensity).
  GRADE A for the weak claim only ("some information, above chance, shaped by culture and context"); the strong claim (a fixed fingerprint per emotion) is graded separately and is C. — FACTS_T3a §07 C3 "What both camps accept, and the grades" (AGREED point 1) and C1/C2

- STUDY Hangry (everyday hunger goes with more irritable, negative self-reported mood) — original: Bushman, DeWall, Pond & Hanus 2014 (107 married couples, 21 days; lower blood glucose ↔ more pins in a voodoo doll and louder noise blasts for the spouse; correlational); Swami et al. 2022 (64 participants, 21-day experience sampling, 9,142 responses: more hunger ↔ more anger and irritability and less pleasure, robust to sex, age, BMI, diet and trait anger; no link to arousal); Ackermans et al. 2022 (women randomised to a 14 h fast, n = 53, or breakfast, n = 55: the fasting women reported more tension, anger, fatigue and confusion and less vigour). Later evidence: three independent designs agree on direction; glucose as the mechanism is untested/contested.
  GRADE B: hunger goes with more negative/irritable self-reported mood (3 independent designs, small samples, mostly self-report; mechanism open). — FACTS_T3a §07 C4 "Hangry"

- STUDY Emotion granularity (negative-emotion differentiation) goes with better regulation outcomes — original: Barrett, Gross, Christensen & Benvenuto 2001 introduced the idea; later tests: Kalokerinos, Erbas, Ceulemans & Kuppens 2019 (two experience-sampling studies, N = 200 and 101; 34,660 and 6,282 measurements: low differentiation was not linked to choosing worse strategies but to using strategies less effectively); Seah & Coifman 2022 (meta-analysis of 17 studies: negative-emotion differentiation went with fewer maladaptive behaviours, r = −.15, and r = −.09 after controlling negative affect; clinical n = 7, −.15; non-clinical n = 10, −.16).
  GRADE B (granularity ↔ better regulation): replicated associations, small, mostly correlational. — FACTS_T3a §07 C4 "Concept learning, granularity, body state and judgment"

- STUDY Critical period for monocular deprivation in kittens (Hubel & Wiesel 1970) — original: Hubel & Wiesel 1970 (kittens, visual-cortex recordings): susceptibility to lid suture begins suddenly near the start of the fourth week, stays high until some time between the sixth and eighth weeks, and disappears around the end of the third month; monocular closure for over a year in an adult cat produced no detectable effects; after 3 months of monocular deprivation from birth the proportion of cells the eye influenced fell from 85 to about 7 %; reopening the eye for up to 5 y gave only very limited recovery. Later evidence: Maya Vetencourt et al. 2008 (adult rats): chronic fluoxetine reinstated ocular-dominance plasticity and promoted recovery in adult amblyopic rats (animal evidence only; no human translation verified).
  GRADE B: the kitten result is a landmark (Nobel 1981) and the window is not absolutely closed in animals; the sources read here are the original and one rat study, and neither fixes any human timing. — FACTS_T3a §08 C5 "Critical periods"

- STUDY We use only 10% of our brains — original: folk claim (origin story, Beyerstein 1999, not opened). Later evidence: the real limit is energetic, not a silent 90 % (Lennie 2003: possibly fewer than 1 % of neurons can be substantially active at once); belief survey Macdonald et al. 2017: the item was answered incorrectly by 36 % of the US general public (N = 3,045), 33 % of educators (N = 598) and 14 % of the high-neuroscience-exposure group (N = 234).
  GRADE D: no support; what is true, and different, is the energy limit on simultaneous activity. — FACTS_T3a §08 D3 "Myth list (08)"

- STUDY Left-brained vs right-brained people (hemispheric personality types) — original: popular claim (Sperry's 1981 Nobel was for the functional specialization of the cerebral hemispheres, not for personality types). Later evidence: Nielsen et al. 2013 (resting-state fMRI of 1,011 people aged 7–29): strongly lateralised connections cluster as 9 left- and 11 right-lateralised hubs, lateralisation "appears to be a local rather than global property", and the data are not consistent with a whole-brain left-brained or right-brained phenotype.
  GRADE D: for the personality-type claim; hemispheres do specialise (language, attention). — FACTS_T3a §08 D3 "Myth list (08)"

- STUDY The brain stops developing at 25 — original: folk claim (where "about 25" comes from was not traced). Later evidence: Bethlehem et al. 2022 (123,984 MRI scans of 101,457 people, 115 days post-conception to 100 y): grey-matter volume peaks at 5.9 y, subcortical grey matter at 14.4 y, white-matter volume at 28.7 y (declining faster after about 50); Mills et al. 2016 (391 participants, 852 scans, ages 8–30): cortical grey matter decreases through the second decade, white matter rises to mid-to-late adolescence.
  GRADE D: there is no single age at which development stops; different tissues peak at different ages. — FACTS_T3a §08 D3 "Myth list (08)"

- STUDY Most neurons die with age (thousands lost every day) — original: folk claim. Later evidence: Pakkenberg et al. 2003 (stereology): the difference in total neocortical neuron number was less than 10 % across ages 20–90; neocortical glia about 36 billion (mean age 89) vs 39 billion (mean age 26), not significantly different.
  GRADE D: for "most neurons die with age" (neocortex). — FACTS_T3a §08 D3 "Myth list (08)"

- STUDY Wundt's Leipzig laboratory as the first psychology lab (1879) — original: the Boring-tradition convention. Alan Kim's SEP entry on Wundt (rev. 21 Oct 2022): in 1879 the university formally recognised Wundt's little room of equipment as a laboratory, "the world's first devoted to psychology". Later evidence: the same entry's note 7 adds that William James had set up a psychological "laboratory" at Harvard in 1875; Boring himself published "On the subjectivity of important historical dates: Leipzig 1879" (J Hist Behav Sci 1(1):5–9, 1965; title only, paper not opened).
  GRADE B: "usually dated to 1879" holds as the date of formal recognition; "the first" is a matter of definition, not a clean fact. — FACTS_T1 §01 A "Wundt and 1879"

- STUDY Chomsky's 1959 review killed behaviourism — original: folk claim (Chomsky 1959, Language 35(1):26–58, a review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior; the review itself is a landmark critique). Later evidence: chronology — Miller's span paper (1956), Chomsky's own Syntactic Structures (1957, per SEP Attention) and Broadbent's Perception and Communication (1958) all precede it; Mandler 2002: the "revolution" was largely an evolving return to attitudes and trends that existed before behaviourism and survived outside the US, often slow and piecemeal (four memory conferences 1955–1966); SEP Behaviorism: Chomsky was "one of behaviorism's most successful and damaging critics"; SEP Introspection: strict behaviourism declined in the 1960s–70s.
  GRADE D for "one review killed it / started the revolution"; the supportable wording is "one influential blow in a gradual, multi-causal shift". — FACTS_T1 §01 B (✗ item)

- STUDY Donders's subtraction method and the pure-insertion assumption — original: Donders 1868 (subtracting the reaction time of a simpler task from that of a more complex one to time the stage in between; Posner 2005). Later evidence: Sternberg 1998 — the method assumes that adding one stage leaves the durations of the other operations unchanged, and later psychologists recognised that this needed justification; Sternberg 1969's additive-factors method [def] was the extension.
  GRADE C: subtraction gives a stage duration only if pure insertion holds, an assumption that needs justification. — FACTS_T1 §01 D "Donders 1868"

- STUDY Reverse inference from brain activation to cognitive process (Poldrack 2006) — original: Poldrack 2006, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 10(2):59–63: inferring that a cognitive process was engaged from the activation of a brain region is "not deductively valid" but can still carry some information. Later evidence: none beyond the abstract in the sources read; a Bayesian analysis of the BrainMap database shows its usefulness is limited by how selective the region's activation is, so researchers should be circumspect when selectivity is weak or unknown.
  GRADE C: weak, probabilistic evidence whose value depends on how selective the activation is. — FACTS_T1 §01 D "What RT patterns cannot show" (ii)

- STUDY Double empathy information-transfer test (Crompton et al. 2020) — original: Milton 2012 (the double empathy problem [def]: breakdowns between autistic and non-autistic people are a two-way mismatch, not one-sided "mindblindness"). Later evidence: Crompton et al. 2020, 8-person diffusion chains, n = 72: detail retention declined faster in mixed (autistic + non-autistic) chains; all-autistic chains did not differ from all-non-autistic chains; rapport lowest in mixed chains; no independent replication of the information-transfer result was found.
  GRADE C: small single-lab study; a hint for the two-way reading, not a settled result. — FACTS_T4a §12 A1

- STUDY Colour-category effect only in the right visual field (Gilbert et al. 2006) — original: Gilbert, Regier, Kay & Ivry 2006, PNAS 103(2):489–494: faster discrimination across colour-name borders in the right but not the left visual field; disrupted by verbal but not spatial load. Later evidence: Witzel & Gegenfurtner 2011, J Vision 11(12):16: ten versions of the two original experiments with 230 naive observers; category effects appeared "to the same extent in both visual fields".
  GRADE D: for the lateralization claim; the basic category advantage in speeded discrimination is a separate, small and stimulus-dependent effect that is graded on its own. — FACTS_T4a §13 G

- STUDY Number words as a cognitive technology (Pirahã, Mundurukú) — original: Gordon 2004 (Pirahã "one-two-many" system; performance with quantities above three "remarkably poor", with a constant coefficient of variation). Later evidence: Frank, Everett, Fedorenko & Gibson 2008 (no linguistic method for exact quantity, not even "one", yet large sets matched perfectly with no delay; failures only when memory or delay was involved); Pica, Lemer, Izard & Dehaene 2004 (Mundurukú, number words only up to 5: approximate comparison and addition of large numbers fine, exact arithmetic fails beyond 4–5).
  GRADE B: the strong reading (language limits numerical thought) is false; the weak reading (number words help track exact cardinality across time, space and modality) is supported. — FACTS_T4a §13 G

- STUDY Culture constrains Pirahã grammar (Everett 2005) — original: Everett 2005, Current Anthropology 46(4):621–646: an "immediate experience" constraint explains the absence of numbers, colour terms, embedding and more. Later evidence: Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009 reject each claim (no evidence that Pirahã lacks embedded clauses or quantifiers); Futrell et al. 2016 (corpus search): no unambiguous evidence for recursive embedding, a corpus "plausibly consistent with … a regular language", "although this is not the only plausible analysis".
  GRADE C: no consensus; "Pirahã has no recursion" must not be presented as established. — FACTS_T4a §13 G

- STUDY A dedicated vocabulary explosion (naming insight) at about 18 months — original: the textbook spurt. Later evidence: Ganger & Brent 2004 (38 children, only 5 fit a spurt (logistic) curve better than a smoothly accelerating one); McMurray 2007 (simulation and mathematics: acceleration is a necessary by-product of learning many words in parallel when words vary in difficulty, so "specialized cognitive changes are unnecessary").
  GRADE C: the acceleration is real; a distinct spurt as a special switch is not well supported. — FACTS_T4a §13 E

- STUDY Rice–wheat theory of Chinese regional psychology (Talhelm et al. 2014) — original: Talhelm et al. 2014, Science 344(6184):603–608: rice-growing southern China more interdependent and holistic-thinking than the wheat-growing north in 1,162 Han Chinese participants at six sites, with differences "just as large" between neighbouring counties along the rice–wheat border. Later evidence: follow-ups from the same group (Talhelm, Zhang & Oishi 2018, 8,964 people in cafés in six cities plus a chair-blocking experiment, n = 678; Talhelm & English 2020, 3,835 people in 10 neighbouring provinces and rice farming vs norm tightness in 32 countries); critical papers exist (Ruan, Xie & Zhang 2015; Acta Psychologica Sinica 2018) whose findings were not read; no independent pre-registered replication was found.
  GRADE C: regional observational correlations with a contested interpretation; no independent replication found. — FACTS_T4a §14 F

- STUDY Fijian food taboos cut fish-poisoning risk (Henrich & Henrich 2010) — original: Henrich & Henrich 2010, Proc R Soc B 277(1701):3715–3724: taboos for pregnant and lactating women selectively target the most toxic marine species, reducing a woman's chance of fish poisoning by about 30% during pregnancy and 60% during breastfeeding; transmission fits familial transmission plus selective learning from locally prestigious individuals; some non-adaptive taboos persist as cognitive by-products. Later evidence: no independent replication was found.
  GRADE C: a single ethnographic-plus-modelling study; no independent replication found. — FACTS_T4a §14 H

- STUDY Neuromyth endorsement rates 神经迷思的流行程度 — original: Macdonald et al. 2017, Front Psychol 8:1314 (open full text read, Table 5 — FACTS_T4b §16F/§16L): online convenience samples of the general public (N = 3,045), educators (N = 598) and a high-neuroscience-exposure group (N = 234) judged statements keyed as false; endorsement public / educators / high-exposure: "learners learn better when information matches their preferred learning style" 93% / 76% / 78%; "we only use 10% of our brain" 36% / 33% / 14%; "listening to classical music increases children's reasoning ability" 59% / 55% / 43%; mean over seven items 68% / 56% / 46%. Later evidence: no replication or national-sample survey was opened.
  GRADE C: one survey with non-representative online samples; the percentages describe these samples only, and "most people believe we use 10% of the brain" is NOT supported (about a third did). — FACTS_T4b §16F, §16L

- STUDY Sensitive periods differ by visual function (children deprived by dense cataracts) — original: Lewis & Maurer 2005, Dev Psychobiol 46(3):163–183 (review; abstract read — FACTS_T3b §11 E): "multiple sensitive periods" in children deprived of vision by dense cataracts: for acuity alone a period of normal visually driven development, a sensitive period for damage and a sensitive period for recovery; relative to normal development the period for damage is "surprisingly long" for acuity, peripheral vision and optokinetic nystagmus asymmetry but "surprisingly short" for global motion. Later evidence: Le Grand et al. 2003 (early right-hemisphere deprivation severely impaired expert face processing, left-hemisphere deprivation did not) and the graded amblyopia and cochlear-implant results in FACTS_T3b §11 E all point to function-specific, graded windows; no independent replication of the review's period lengths was opened.
  GRADE B: the qualitative claim that different functions have different, graded sensitive periods is consistent across the human natural experiments in FACTS_T3b §11 E; the lengths of individual periods are not settled (one review abstract read; grade assigned by the lead). — FACTS_T3b §11 E

## Lesson 17 descriptive epidemiology (lead-assigned grades, 2026-10-14; every number is in FACTS_T5 §17 as [V])

- STUDY Mental disorders are common in surveys — original: Kessler et al. 2005 (US NCS-R, N = 9,282, DSM-IV structured interview): lifetime any disorder 46.4%, 12-month 26.2% (of those cases 22.3% serious, 37.3% moderate, 40.4% mild). Later evidence: Steel et al. 2014 (174 surveys, 63 countries: lifetime 29.2%, 12-month 17.6%, substantial heterogeneity, English-speaking countries highest); McGrath et al. 2023 (WHO WMH, 29 countries, N = 156,331: lifetime so far 28.6% of males / 29.8% of females, projected cumulative risk by age 75 of 46.4% / 53.1%); Huang et al. 2019 (China Mental Health Survey, N = 32,552: lifetime 16.6% excluding dementia, 12-month 9.3%).
  GRADE B: the qualitative claim — roughly three in ten adults have met criteria by the time of interview and about half by old age on projection — is consistent across large structured-interview surveys; the exact figures depend on the instrument, which disorders are covered, the country and whether the number is lifetime-so-far or projected, and the country gaps are not explained in the abstracts (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 items 23–30

- STUDY Most depressive episodes end within a year — original: Spijker et al. 2002 (NEMESIS, general population, N = 7,076; people with newly started major depressive episodes followed two years): median episode 3.0 months; about 50% recovered within 3 months, 63% within 6, 76% within 12; nearly 20% not recovered at 24 months. Later evidence: none opened for the same design.
  GRADE B: one large general-population cohort with a clear result and no replication opened; the curve is a population average and says nothing about any one person's course (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 137

- STUDY Depression often recurs in clinical samples — original: Mueller et al. 1999 (NIMH Collaborative Program, 380 recovered patients): Kaplan-Meier recurrence 85% within up to 15 years, 58% among the 105 who had stayed well at least 5 years. Later evidence: Solomon et al. 2000 (318 patients followed 10 years): nearly two-thirds had at least one recurrence, each additional lifetime episode raised recurrence risk by 16%, a longer well period lowered it.
  GRADE B: two clinical cohorts agree on the direction; both are treatment-seeking samples, so the curve cannot be joined to the general-population NEMESIS curve (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 items 138–139

- STUDY Few people with major depression get adequate care — original: Thornicroft et al. 2017 (WHO WMH surveys, 21 countries; 4,331 respondents with 12-month major depressive disorder out of 51,547): 16.5% received minimally adequate treatment — 22.4% in high-income countries versus 3.7% in low and lower-middle-income countries. Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE B: one multi-country survey with self-reported treatment; the figures depend on the authors' definition of "minimally adequate"; a large gap, larger in poorer countries, is what this survey shows (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 39

- STUDY Major depression is moderately heritable — original: Sullivan, Neale & Kendler 2000 (meta-analysis of family, adoption and twin studies): relatives of probands OR 2.84; heritability of liability 37% (95% CI 31–42%); shared family environment about 0% (0–5%); individual-specific environment plus measurement error 63% (58–67%). Later evidence: Howard et al. 2019 (GWAS meta-analysis of 807,553 people): 102 independent variants, each of small effect, no single depression gene.
  GRADE B: moderate, not zero and not high, with little shared-family environment, is the consistent qualitative reading; the 37% is a population statistic that depends on the model and the population and cannot be read as a share of one person's depression; the molecular result is consistent with, not a replication of, the 37% (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 items 70, 72

- STUDY Life events raise the odds of a depressive episode — original: Kendler, Karkowski & Prescott 1999 (population-based female twins, 24,648 person-months, 316 onsets): odds of onset in the month of a stressful event 5.64 in all subjects, 4.52 within dizygotic pairs, 3.58 within monozygotic pairs; about one third of the association is non-causal because predisposed people place themselves in higher-risk environments. Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE B: a within-pair design that separates part of the confounding; one population of female twins and a model-based "one third" (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 71

- STUDY Psychotic experiences are common and mostly transient — original: McGrath et al. 2015 (WHO WMH surveys, 18 countries, N = 31,261): lifetime any psychotic experience 5.8%, hallucinatory 5.2%, delusional 1.3%; 32.2% of those reported a single occurrence. Later evidence: Linscott & van Os 2013 (meta-analysis, 61 cohorts): about 80% remit, about 20% persist, 7.4% of people with baseline psychotic experiences later developed a psychotic disorder; Beavan et al. 2011 (17 general-population surveys): reported voice-hearing 0.6%–84%, median 13.2%, differing with definition and method.
  GRADE B: a survey and a meta-analysis agree that mild psychotic experiences are common and mostly remit and that the link to disorder is only probabilistic; the prevalence depends heavily on the definition (5.8% interview-defined versus a median 13.2% in looser surveys) (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 items 103–106

- STUDY Recovery from psychosis depends on the definition — original: Jääskeläinen et al. 2013 (50 studies of schizophrenia): median 13.5% (IQR 8.1–20.0%) met recovery criteria requiring clinical and social improvement sustained for about two years; higher at sites in poorer countries; not increased over recent decades. Later evidence: Lally et al. 2017 (first-episode psychosis, 79 studies, 19,072 patients): pooled remission 58% (mean follow-up 5.5 years) and recovery 38% (mean 7.2 years), recovery stable after the first two years.
  GRADE B: two meta-analyses with different populations and definitions give different numbers by design; neither is a cure rate, and the claim that survives is that recovery proportions vary with definition and population (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 107

- STUDY Depression has over a thousand symptom profiles — original: Fried & Nesse 2015 (3,703 outpatients with major depressive disorder, STAR*D level 1): 1,030 unique symptom profiles, 501 (48.6%) endorsed by one patient only, the commonest profile covering 1.8% of patients; controlling for severity did not remove the heterogeneity. Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE B: a count from one large clinical sample; the number of unique profiles depends on the symptom list used (lead's reading); the qualitative point — one diagnostic label groups very different symptom patterns — holds (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 36

- STUDY Obsessions and compulsions are common in mild form — original: Ruscio, Stein, Chiu & Kessler (Mol Psychiatry 15(1):53–63, online 2008; NCS-R subsample, N = 2,073): lifetime OCD 2.3%, 12-month 1.2%, although more than a quarter reported obsessions or compulsions at some point. Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE B: one US survey; the "more than a quarter" refers to sub-threshold experiences and depends on the interview's wording (lead-assigned). — FACTS_T5 §17 item 88
