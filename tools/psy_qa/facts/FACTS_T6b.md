## 04 · sleep 睡眠 (behavioural only; no medication guidance)

### 04-A · Architecture, the clock, adenosine, caffeine, chronotype
- [def] 睡眠分期 sleep stages (AASM scoring): N1, N2, N3 (slow-wave sleep 慢波睡眠; merges the older Rechtschaffen–Kales stages 3+4) and R (REM 快速眼动睡眠). The current AASM Scoring Manual is versioned (Version 2.2 update, Berry et al. 2015 *J Clin Sleep Med* 11(11):1253–1254, PMC4623121); 2007/2012 editions are what Boulos 2019 used as inclusion criteria. — [V] bibliographic record + abstract read; the 3+4→N3 merge is textbook (not shown in the abstracts I opened) → [~] for that clause. History: REM was first reported by Aserinsky & Kleitman 1953 (*Science* 118(3062):273–274, doi:10.1126/science.118.3062.273) and the NREM–REM EEG cycle by Dement & Kleitman 1957 (*Electroencephalogr Clin Neurophysiol* 9(4):673–690, doi:10.1016/0013-4694(57)90088-3); both PubMed records carry no abstract, so only title and year are verified, and the "about 90-minute cycle" is textbook [~].
- [V] Normative sleep in healthy adults, modern scoring (Boulos et al. 2019, *Lancet Respir Med* 7(6):533–543, doi:10.1016/S2213-2600(19)30057-8; meta-analysis, 169 studies, 5,273 healthy adults, AASM 2007/2012 criteria): per decade of age total sleep time −10.1 min (95% CI 7.5–12.8), sleep efficiency −2.1% (1.5–2.6), wake after sleep onset +9.7 min (6.9–12.4), sleep-onset latency +1.1 min (0.3–1.9), arousal index +2.1/h; the percentages of N2 (0.0%), N3 (−0.1%, CI −0.1 to 0.0) and REM (0.0%) did NOT change significantly with age.
- [V] Contrast: Ohayon, Carskadon, Guilleminault & Vitiello 2004 (*Sleep* 27(7):1255–1273, doi:10.1093/sleep/27.7.1255; 65 studies, 3,577 people aged 5–102, 1960–2003): in adults total sleep time, sleep efficiency, slow-wave % and REM % decreased with age while sleep latency, N1 %, N2 % and WASO increased; the authors report that effect sizes were "greatly modified by the quality of subject screening", which could shrink or even mask the age associations. So "deep sleep fades with age" holds in older, R&K-era, less-screened samples and is much weaker in screened healthy adults scored by AASM rules.
- [V] Sleep Heart Health Study (Redline et al. 2004, *Arch Intern Med* 164(4):406–418, doi:10.1001/archinte.164.4.406; N = 2,685 aged 37–92): sleep architecture varies with sex, age, ethnicity and sleep-disordered breathing; women had on average 106% more slow-wave sleep than men; age was associated with poorer sleep in men, less consistently in women; breathing disorders mattered "generally smaller than" sex/ethnicity/age associations.
- [V] ★ Intrinsic circadian period is ≈ 24.2 h, not 25 h: Czeisler et al. 1999 (*Science* 284(5423):2177–2181, doi:10.1126/science.284.5423.2177): earlier estimates of 25 h (activity-rhythm periods 13–65 h, median 25.2 h; body-temperature rhythm ≈ 25 h) came from people exposed to light levels "sufficient to confound circadian period estimation"; in controlled dim light the intrinsic period of melatonin, core body temperature and cortisol averaged 24.18 h in both young and older groups, with a tight distribution. Larger sample: Duffy et al. 2011 (*PNAS* 108 Suppl 3:15602–15608, doi:10.1073/pnas.1010666108; N = 157, aged 18–74): mean 24.15 ± 0.2 h (24 h 9 min ± 12 min); women 24.09 h vs men 24.19 h (P < .01); 35% of women vs 14% of men had periods < 24.0 h.
- STUDY Human circadian period is ≈24.2 h, not 25 h — original: Czeisler 1999, forced-desynchrony/constant-routine protocols in dim light, mean 24.18 h (young and old). Later evidence: Duffy 2011 N = 157, 24.15 ± 0.2 h; Smith, Burgess, Fogg & Eastman 2009 (*PLoS One* 4(6):e6014; Rush University, a different lab and method — ultradian light/dark days) report tau averaging 24.2 h, "similar to tau measured using different experimental methods"; Phillips et al. 2011 (*J Biol Rhythms* 26(5):441–453) call 24.1–24.2 h the "now established" average.
  GRADE A: independent labs and protocols converge on ≈ 24.2 h with a tight spread; the older 25-hour figure is a light-exposure artefact. — Czeisler 1999; Duffy 2011; Smith 2009; Phillips 2011
- [✗] "The human body clock runs on a 25-hour day" — superseded; the 25 h figure came from bunker/isolation studies where light and self-selected schedules shifted the rhythm (Czeisler 1999 abstract; Phillips 2011 abstract contrasts observed periods of 25 hours with the "now established" 24.1–24.2 h average intrinsic period).
- [V] Two-process model 双过程模型 (Process S 稳态过程 + Process C 昼夜过程): Borbély 1982 (*Hum Neurobiol* 1(3):195–204, PMID 7185792; PubMed record has no abstract); Daan, Beersma & Borbély 1984 (*Am J Physiol* 246(2 Pt 2):R161–R183, doi:10.1152/ajpregu.1984.246.2.R161): a sleep-regulating variable S rises in wake and falls in sleep; sleep starts when S approaches an upper threshold H and ends when S reaches a lower threshold L; the thresholds follow a circadian rhythm; S's time constants were derived from EEG power-density changes in regular sleep and after deprivation, and the circadian waveform from spontaneous wake-up times after partial deprivation. Reappraisal: Borbély, Daan, Wirz-Justice & Deboer 2016 (*J Sleep Res* 25(2):131–143, doi:10.1111/jsr.12371) — "remains conceptually useful". Numbers for the widget: see "Widget constants (04)".
- [V] Dijk & Czeisler 1995 (*J Neurosci* 15(5):3526–3538, doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.15-05-03526.1995): 8 men lived 33–36 days without time cues on a 28-h rest–activity schedule (9.33-h sleep episodes at all circadian phases): REM sleep peaks shortly after the core-temperature minimum; slow-wave activity falls across every sleep episode with only a low-amplitude circadian modulation; sleep-spindle activity has a strong circadian rhythm; the usual phase relation between the sleep–wake cycle and the pacemaker "promotes the consolidation of sleep and wakefulness". Reading: sleep homeostasis and the clock are separable and interact (the two-process idea tested by forced desynchrony).
- [V] Adenosine as the "sleep-pressure" molecule is animal evidence: Porkka-Heiskanen et al. 1997 (*Science* 276(5316):1265–1268, doi:10.1126/science.276.5316.1265): in freely behaving CATS, extracellular adenosine in the basal-forebrain cholinergic region rose during spontaneous wake vs slow-wave sleep, rose progressively during prolonged wakefulness and declined slowly in recovery sleep; raising adenosine locally mimicked the post-deprivation sleep pattern. Say "in cats, adenosine in one brain region tracked time awake"; do not write "adenosine is why you feel tired" as a human finding.
- [V] Caffeine mechanism: Nehlig, Daval & Debry 1992 (*Brain Res Rev* 17(2):139–170, doi:10.1016/0165-0173(92)90012-b): at physiological concentrations the "only likely mechanism" is antagonism of adenosine receptors (calcium mobilisation and phosphodiesterase inhibition occur only at non-physiological concentrations); effects on sleep "vary according to individual sensitivity". (Fredholm et al. 1999, *Pharmacol Rev* 51(1):83–133, PMID 10049999 — PubMed record has no abstract; cited by the task but not opened.)
- [V] Caffeine pharmacokinetics: Blanchard & Sawers 1983 (*Eur J Clin Pharmacol* 24(1):93–98, doi:10.1007/BF00613933): 10 healthy men aged 18.8–30.0 y, 5 mg/kg oral vs IV in a randomised crossover; peak plasma concentration after 29.8 ± 8.1 min (mean ± SEM), Cmax 10.0 ± 1.0 µg/ml, oral bioavailability 108.3 ± 3.6%, plasma half-lives "varied from 2.7 to 9.9 h". Review value: Temple et al. 2017 (*Front Psychiatry* 8:80, PMC5445139): "a relatively long half-life of 3–7 h in adults"; half-life longer in pregnancy (on average +8.3 h, up to +16 h), roughly doubled by oral contraceptives, smoking about doubles clearance, heavy alcohol raises the half-life. So for the widget: mean ≈ 5 h with an individual range ≈ 3–10 h; the "1.5–9.5 h" range attributed to the Institute of Medicine could not be opened (NCBI Bookshelf is bot-blocked) → write-ban. Kinetics are nonlinear: in 12 volunteers clearance was lower and half-life longer at 500 mg than at 250 mg (Kaplan 1997, *J Clin Pharmacol* 37(8):693–703), so "a half-life of N hours" is a low-to-moderate-dose approximation; CYP1A2 drives clearance, smoking and drug interactions change it, sex and menstrual cycle matter little (Arnaud 2011, *Handb Exp Pharmacol* 200:33–91).
- [V] Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth 2013 (*J Clin Sleep Med* 9(11):1195–1200, doi:10.5664/jcsm.3170, PMC3805807): fixed 400 mg caffeine vs placebo given 0, 3 or 6 h before habitual bedtime, home sleep, self-report plus a validated portable monitor: each timing had significant effects on sleep disturbance vs placebo (p < 0.05); the authors conclude that "caffeine taken 6 hours before bedtime has important disruptive effects" and support "a minimum of 6 hours". THE ABSTRACT PRINTS NO MINUTES: the often-repeated "reduced sleep by about an hour at 6 h" is NOT verifiable from the abstract and the full text is behind a bot wall (Cloudflare/cookie check) → treat "≈ 1 h" as [~] and use the meta-analysis below for sizes. N and per-condition values: could not verify.
- [V] ★ Gardiner, Weakley, Burke, Roach, Sargent, Maniar, Townshend & Halson 2023 (*Sleep Med Rev* 69:101764, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764; systematic review/meta-analysis, 24 studies): caffeine reduced total sleep time by 45 min and sleep efficiency by 7%, increased sleep-onset latency by 9 min and wake after sleep onset by 12 min, N1 +6.1 min (+1.7%), N3/N4 −11.4 min (−1.4%); modelled cut-offs "to avoid reductions in total sleep time": coffee (107 mg per 250 mL) at least 8.8 h before bedtime, a standard pre-workout supplement (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 h before bedtime.
- STUDY Caffeine before bed shortens and lightens sleep — original: Drake 2013 (400 mg at 0/3/6 h before bed, home study; significant at all three timings, abstract gives no minutes). Later evidence: Gardiner 2023 meta-analysis of 24 studies: TST −45 min, SE −7%, SOL +9 min, WASO +12 min, N3 −11.4 min; modelled cut-off 8.8 h for a coffee-size dose.
  GRADE A for "caffeine reduces and lightens subsequent sleep" (many studies, a meta-analysis, consistent direction); the SIZE for a given dose/time is B (mostly small lab studies, wide individual half-life range 2.7–9.9 h); "a 6-hour curfew is enough for everyone" is not supported — the meta-analysis suggests longer for coffee-size doses. — Drake 2013; Gardiner 2023; Blanchard & Sawers 1983
- [V] Chronotype and "social jetlag" 社会性时差: Roenneberg, Wirz-Justice & Merrow 2003 (*J Biol Rhythms* 18(1):80–90, doi:10.1177/0748730402239679; pilot of 500 questionnaires): sleep times differ strongly between work and free days except in extreme early types; late chronotypes accumulate sleep debt on workdays and compensate "by lengthening their sleep by several hours" on free days; more reported outdoor daylight advances sleep timing. Roenneberg et al. 2007 (*Sleep Med Rev* 11(6):429–438, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2007.07.005): "more than 55,000 people" had completed the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ 慕尼黑时间型问卷), validated against the Horne–Östberg MEQ, sleep logs, actimetry and physiology; sleep and wake times are near-Gaussian across the population; chronotype is age- and sex-dependent. NOTE: the 55,000 is 2007; the 2003 paper is 500.
- [V] Wittmann, Dinich, Merrow & Roenneberg 2006 (*Chronobiol Int* 23(1–2):497–509, doi:10.1080/07420520500545979; N = 501 volunteers): "social jetlag" = the discrepancy between biological and social time (work vs free-day sleep timing); associations of chronotype/social jetlag with sleep quality, wellbeing and stimulant use are strongest in teenagers and young adults to age 25; strongest correlation: later chronotype with smoking. Roenneberg, Allebrandt, Merrow & Vetter 2012 (*Curr Biol* 22(10):939–943, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2012.03.038): in a large epidemiological sample, "beyond sleep duration, social jetlag is associated with increased BMI" (the abstract prints no numbers; cross-sectional).
- STUDY Social jetlag goes with higher BMI and worse mood — original: Wittmann 2006 (N = 501), Roenneberg 2012 (large MCTQ sample; BMI). Later evidence: none opened beyond these (cross-sectional questionnaires; no randomised test of "removing" social jetlag).
  GRADE C: consistent associations from one research group's questionnaire data, cross-sectional and confounded (age, work, smoking, shift work); the concept (work-day vs free-day sleep timing gap) is a useful measurement, not a proven cause. — Wittmann 2006; Roenneberg 2012
- [~] Chronotype shifts with age: lateness peaks around the end of the teens/early twenties and then moves earlier (Roenneberg et al. 2004, *Curr Biol* 14(24):R1038–R1039, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2004.11.039, "A marker for the end of adolescence" — PubMed record has NO abstract; the often-quoted peak ages "≈ 19.5 y (women), ≈ 20.9 y (men)" are not verified here). Roenneberg 2007 (above) confirms only that chronotype depends on age and sex. Safe wording: "chronotype is latest in the late teens/early twenties".
- [V] Natural-sleep context: Yetish et al. 2015 (*Curr Biol* 25(21):2862–2868, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2015.09.046): in three pre-industrial societies (Hadza, San, Tsimane) sleep periods averaged 6.9–8.5 h with sleep durations of 5.7–7.1 h — "near the low end of those industrial societies"; sleep started on average 3.3 h after sunset; napping on < 7% of winter and < 22% of summer days; nearly 1 h seasonal difference. Contested in four published comments (Nature News & Views 2015; *Curr Biol* 2015–16; *Sleep* 2016); N and days not in the abstract.
- [def] Sleep inertia 睡眠惯性: the grogginess and performance deficit on awakening; "exaggerated by prior sleep loss and the time of day", measured in lab and real-world settings (Hilditch & McHill 2019, *Nat Sci Sleep* 11:155–165, PMC6710480, doi:10.2147/NSS.S188911; abstract read).

### 04-B · Sleep loss and performance (睡眠剥夺 / 睡眠限制 与 表现)
- [V] ★ Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges 2003 (*Sleep* 26(2):117–126, doi:10.1093/sleep/26.2.117; erratum *Sleep* 2004;27(4):600): n = 48 healthy adults aged 21–38, standardised lab; randomised to 4, 6 or 8 h time in bed (TIB) per night for 14 consecutive days, or 3 nights of total sleep deprivation (0 h TIB), each with 3 baseline and 3 recovery days. Results: chronic 4 h or 6 h produced "significant cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in cognitive performance on all tasks"; subjective sleepiness showed an acute response but "only small further increases on subsequent days" and "did not significantly differentiate the 6 h and 4 h conditions"; sleep EEG measures (incl. delta power) responded acutely and then changed little over the 14 nights; total deprivation produced disproportionately large responses relative to sleep lost; across conditions lapses of behavioural alertness were near-linearly related to the cumulative duration of wakefulness in excess of 15.84 h (s.e. 0.73 h). Conclusion (abstract): restricting sleep to 6 h or less per night produced deficits "equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation", and "subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits".
- [~] The popular mapping "two weeks at 6 h = one night of total deprivation; 4 h = two nights" is NOT in the abstract (which says "6 h or less … up to 2 nights"); the figure-level numbers were not opened → use the abstract wording, never "6 h = exactly N nights".
- [V] Belenky et al. 2003 (*J Sleep Res* 12(1):1–12, doi:10.1046/j.1365-2869.2003.00337.x): n = 66 volunteers randomised to 3 (n = 18), 5 (n = 16), 7 (n = 16) or 9 h (n = 16) TIB for 7 days, then 3 recovery days at 8 h. PVT (psychomotor vigilance task 精神运动警觉任务) in the 3-h group: speed declined and lapses (RT > 500 ms) rose steadily; in the 7- and 5-h groups speed declined, then "appeared to stabilize at a reduced level"; lapses increased only in the 5-h group; 9-h group stayed at baseline. After 3 recovery nights the 7- and 5-h groups showed "no evidence of recovery"; the 3-h group recovered rapidly after night 1 but incompletely. Interpretation (authors): the brain adapts to chronic restriction, stabilising performance at a reduced level.
- NOTE do not splice: Van Dongen (14 days, deficits keep accumulating at 6 h) and Belenky (7 days, speed plateaus at 5–7 h at a reduced level) differ in duration, measures and analysis; both agree restricted sleep leaves performance impaired and that recovery is slow. "You adapt" is true only in the sense of a stabilised, lower level; the felt sleepiness plateau is not evidence of normal function.
- [V] Banks, Van Dongen, Maislin & Dinges 2010 (*Sleep* 33(8):1013–1026, doi:10.1093/sleep/33.8.1013): n = 159 adults aged 22–45 (median 29); 5 nights at 4 h TIB, then ONE recovery night randomised to 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 h TIB (142 restricted, 17 controls at 10 h all along); recovery of PVT lapses, sleepiness (KSS) and fatigue was exponential in recovery-sleep dose and still "incomplete" after the 10-h night (8.96 h of sleep) for vigilance lapses, sleepiness and fatigue. One long night does not clear 5 nights of debt.
- [V] Cohen et al. 2010 (*Sci Transl Med* 2(14):14ra3, doi:10.1126/scitranslmed.3000458; forced-desynchrony-type protocol, sleep 10 h : wake 32.85 h, i.e. a 1:3.3 sleep–wake ratio across all circadian phases): 10-h sleep opportunities restored vigilance performance for the first several hours awake, but chronic sleep loss "markedly increased the rate of deterioration" across wakefulness, particularly during the circadian night — the cost hides until you are awake late. The authors open by noting that many people believe they adapt to chronic sleep loss or that a single long sleep is enough to recover.
- [V] Banks & Dinges 2007 (review, *J Clin Sleep Med* 3(5):519–528, PMC1978335): deficits accumulate over days of partial loss to levels equivalent to 1 to 3 nights of total sleep loss; individual differences in the response to restriction look stable and trait-like ("differential vulnerability"); a causal role for reduced sleep duration in adverse health outcomes "remains unclear" although lab restriction shows endocrine/metabolic/inflammatory effects.
- [V] Lim & Dinges 2010 (*Psychol Bull* 136(3):375–389, doi:10.1037/a0018883, PMC3290659; meta-analysis of short-term (< 48 h) TOTAL sleep deprivation, 70 articles, 147 cognitive tests, six domains): effect sizes from small and non-significant (reasoning accuracy: g = −0.125, 95% CI [−0.27, 0.02]) to large (lapses in simple attention: g = −0.776, 95% CI [−0.96, −0.60]); no speed–accuracy difference within domains; only time awake moderated accuracy effects. So attention/vigilance lapses are the big casualty; complex reasoning accuracy is least affected.
- [V] Lowe, Safati & Hall 2017 (*Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 80:586–604, doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2017.07.010; meta-analysis of experimentally manipulated sleep RESTRICTION, 61 studies, 71 populations): overall g = −0.383 (p < .001); executive functioning g = −0.324; sustained attention g = −0.409; long-term memory g = −0.192 (p = .002); insufficient evidence for effects in several other domains; age group, time of day, cumulative days of restriction, sleep latency, subjective sleepiness and sex moderated the effect.
- [V] Pilcher & Huffcutt 1996 (*Sleep* 19(4):318–326, doi:10.1093/sleep/19.4.318): meta-analysis of 19 studies, 143 study coefficients, total N = 1,932: sleep deprivation "strongly impairs human functioning"; mood is more affected than cognitive or motor performance; PARTIAL sleep deprivation had a more profound effect than long- or short-term total deprivation (so narrative reviews may underestimate partial loss). The "average sleep-deprived person performs at the 9th percentile" line in the task list is not in the abstract and I could not open the full text → see COULD NOT VERIFY.
- STUDY Sleep loss impairs vigilance and cognition — original: Pilcher & Huffcutt 1996 (19 studies, N = 1,932); Van Dongen 2003 (n = 48, 14 days at 4/6/8 h). Later evidence: Lim & Dinges 2010 (70 articles; lapses g = −0.78); Lowe 2017 (61 studies; restriction g = −0.38; sustained attention −0.41); Belenky 2003 (n = 66, dose–response); Banks 2010 (n = 159, one recovery night insufficient).
  GRADE A: several independent labs and three meta-analyses agree on direction and rough size; size differs by domain (largest for vigilance lapses, smallest for reasoning accuracy) and between total deprivation and restriction. — Van Dongen 2003; Lim & Dinges 2010; Lowe 2017
- STUDY Sleep-restricted people do not notice impairment — original: Van Dongen 2003 (sleepiness ratings flat from the first restricted days; 6 h and 4 h not distinguished while lapses kept rising). Later evidence: Belenky 2003 (performance stabilises at a lower level); Cohen 2010 (deficits hidden until the circadian night); individual trait-like vulnerability (Banks & Dinges 2007). No independent preregistered replication of the "unaware" claim found.
  GRADE B: replicated in direction by other restriction studies and consistent with the 15.84-h wakefulness model, but the "unaware" claim rests on self-rated sleepiness vs PVT in a few lab samples; real-world people can partly calibrate to a stable reduced level. — Van Dongen 2003; Belenky 2003; Cohen 2010
- [V] Williamson & Feyer 2000 (*Occup Environ Med* 57(10):649–655, doi:10.1136/oem.57.10.649): 39 subjects (30 transport employees, 9 army) studied over 28 h of sleep deprivation and after alcohol doses up to ≈ 0.1% blood alcohol concentration (BAC): after 17–19 h without sleep (clock times 22:30 and 01:00) performance on some tests "was equivalent or worse than that at a BAC of 0.05%", response speeds were up to 50% slower for some tests; after longer periods performance reached the level of the maximum alcohol dose (BAC 0.1%). Dawson & Reid 1997 (*Nature* 388:235, doi:10.1038/40775) is a one-page letter — its PubMed record has no abstract and I did not open the text, so I do not quote its numbers.
- STUDY 17–19 h awake impairs like a BAC of about 0.05% — original: Williamson & Feyer 2000 (n = 39, 28 h deprivation, matched alcohol doses to 0.1%); Dawson & Reid 1997 letter (not opened). Later evidence: no newer replication opened.
  GRADE B: two groups, similar finding on selected tests, but small samples; equivalence is test-specific ("on some tests") and it describes laboratory performance, not accident risk. Use as an analogy, not a conversion. — Williamson & Feyer 2000
- [V] Real-world risk: Tefft 2018 (*Sleep* 41(10):zsy144, doi:10.1093/sleep/zsy144; 6,845 drivers in a representative sample of US DOT-investigated crashes 2005–2007; modified case-control comparing culpable vs non-culpable drivers; controlled for fatigue-, driver-, environment-related factors): compared with 7–9 h of sleep in the previous 24 h, drivers who had slept 6, 5, 4 and < 4 h had 1.3 (95% CI 1.04–1.7), 1.9 (1.1–3.2), 2.9 (1.4–6.2) and 15.1 (4.2–54.4) times the odds of being culpable; < 4 h: 3.4 (2.1–5.6) times higher odds of culpable involvement in single-vehicle than multiple-vehicle crashes. Self-reported sleep, observational; odds ratios approximate incidence-rate ratios only under the stated assumption about non-culpable drivers. (Higher figures quoted from an earlier AAA Foundation report are NOT these; I did not open that report.)
- STUDY Short sleep raises culpable-crash odds — original: Tefft 2018 (6,845 drivers; OR 1.3 at 6 h → 15.1 at < 4 h). Later evidence: none opened; Tefft 2012 (*Accid Anal Prev* 45:180) on drowsy-driver crash prevalence exists but was not read.
  GRADE B: large real-world sample, dose-ordered, consistent with the lab data above; observational, self-reported sleep, very wide CI at < 4 h. — Tefft 2018
- [V] Yoo, Hu, Gujar, Jolesz & Walker 2007 (*Nat Neurosci* 10(3):385–392, doi:10.1038/nn1851): after one night of total sleep deprivation, hippocampal activity during episodic memory encoding was significantly reduced and later retention was worse; the abstract gives no N or percentages (do not quote a "−19%" figure from this file).

### 04-C · Sleep and memory (consolidation 巩固), with the critiques
- [~] Jenkins & Dallenbach 1924 (*Am J Psychol* 35(4):605–612, "Obliviscence during sleep and waking"): the classic sleep-vs-waking retention study used two participants (the authors themselves) learning nonsense syllables and testing after 1, 2, 4 or 8 h; retention was better after sleep. The paper is not in PubMed and I could not open it → quote no percentages; teach it as a famous N = 2 demonstration that later large-sample work (below) had to quantify.
- [V] Plihal & Born 1997 (*J Cogn Neurosci* 9(4):534–547, doi:10.1162/jocn.1997.9.4.534): 20 healthy men; paired-associate recall (declarative) and mirror-tracing (procedural) after retention intervals over early vs late nocturnal sleep or matched waking; sleep generally enhanced recall vs waking; paired-associate recall improved more over EARLY sleep (SWS-rich: 5× more SWS), mirror-tracing more over LATE sleep (REM about 2× as long as early sleep); cortisol differences "cannot account for" the effect. Small single-lab study.
- [V] Diekelmann & Born 2010 (*Nat Rev Neurosci* 11(2):114–126, doi:10.1038/nrn2762; review): SWS (slow oscillations, spindles, ripples) supports system consolidation (re-activation and redistribution of hippocampus-dependent memories to neocortex); REM supports synaptic consolidation (local immediate-early-gene plasticity). Theory-level; see Cordi & Rasch 2021 for the robustness problem.
- [V] ★ Berres & Erdfelder 2021 (*Psychol Bull* 147(12):1309–1353, doi:10.1037/bul0000350; 823 effect sizes, 271 independent samples, 177 articles, 1967–2019): moderate overall sleep benefit in episodic memory g = 0.44; larger when stimuli were studied multiple times; for word materials largest in free recall, then cued recall, then recognition; stronger in pre–post difference measures than in delayed memory tests; larger for natural sleep and nighttime naps than for alternative designs (SWS-deprived sleep, daytime naps); selective-reporting adjustment reduces g to 0.28 (still significant). Later evidence: Cordi & Rasch 2021 ("less SWS-related"); Berres & Erdfelder 2021 (SWS-deprived designs and daytime naps show smaller benefits than natural sleep — i.e., not a clean SWS-only story).
  GRADE C: a neat, widely taught dichotomy that rests on small studies and has been qualified by later work; teach as a hypothesis about which stages do what, not as a fact. — Plihal & Born 1997; Cordi & Rasch 2021

- STUDY Targeted memory reactivation during NREM sleep — original: Rasch 2007 (odour cue during SWS improved declarative recall; fMRI hippocampal activation). Later evidence: Hu et al. 2020 meta-analysis (91 experiments, N = 2,004): g = 0.29 [0.21, 0.38], none in REM or waking; Cordi & Rasch 2021 caution about robustness of sleep–memory effects in general.
  GRADE B: a reliable but small effect across many labs and cue types (sounds, odours); durability beyond hours-to-days, relevance to real studying, and exact mechanism are open; it needs a sleep lab or cue-delivery gear (nothing to "do Monday" except know it exists). — Hu et al. 2020; Cordi & Rasch 2021

- [V] School performance (Dewald, Meijer, Oort, Kerkhof & Bögels 2010, *Sleep Med Rev* 14(3):179–189, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2009.10.004): three meta-analyses of children/adolescents — sleep quality (k = 16 studies, N = 13,631) r = 0.096; sleep duration (k = 17, N = 15,199) r = 0.069; sleepiness (k = 17, N = 19,530) r = −0.133 with school performance: "significantly but modestly related"; larger effects in younger participants; correlational — the authors call for longitudinal and experimental work. Later evidence: none opened; all correlational.

### 04-D · Naps (小睡) and sleep inertia
- [V] Tietzel & Lack 2001 (*Sleep* 24(3):293–300, doi:10.1093/sleep/24.3.293): 12 young healthy sleepers, repeated measures: no nap vs 10-min vs 30-min afternoon nap after a night of 4.7 h total sleep; after the 10-min nap alertness and cognitive performance improved immediately and stayed improved through the hour of post-nap testing; after the 30-min nap most measures declined immediately and partly recovered by the end (probably sleep inertia; test window only 1 h). (The task list says N = 13; the abstract says 12.)
- [V] Tietzel & Lack 2002 (*J Sleep Res* 11(3):213–218, doi:10.1046/j.1365-2869.2002.00299.x): 16 young healthy sleepers, nocturnal sleep limited to 24:00–05:00: 30-s, 90-s and 10-min naps vs no nap: only the 10-min nap improved alertness and cognitive performance; 30-s and 90-s naps did not.
- [V] Brooks & Lack 2006 (*Sleep* 29(6):831–840, doi:10.1093/sleep/29.6.831): 24 healthy young good sleepers, not regular nappers; nocturnal sleep restricted to ~5 h at home, then naps of exactly 5, 10, 20 or 30 min of sleep (or none) at 15:00 with 3 h of post-nap testing: 5-min nap "few benefits"; 10-min nap immediate improvements in sleep latency, sleepiness, fatigue, vigour and cognitive performance, some maintained "for as long as 155 minutes"; 20-min nap improvements emerging at 35 min and lasting to 125 min; 30-min nap produced impaired alertness/performance right after waking (sleep inertia) then improvements up to 155 min. Authors: 10 min most effective overall of the lengths tested.
- [V] Hilditch, Centofanti, Dorrian & Banks 2016 (*Sleep* 39(3):675–685, doi:10.5665/sleep.5550; a different lab from Tietzel/Brooks): 31 healthy adults aged 21–35 (18 females), simulated night shift, randomised to total sleep deprivation, a 10-min nap or a 30-min nap ending at 04:00; the 30-min nap had 14.7 ± 5.7 min of SWS vs 0.8 ± 1.5 min; PVT performance: no change over time after the 10-min nap; the 30-min nap performance deteriorated right after waking and was still worse at 47 min (4.0 vs 4.3 1/s pre-nap); "Self-rated performance did not reflect objective performance following a nap" (all conditions, even no nap, rated themselves better).
- [V] Mednick, Nakayama & Stickgold 2003 (*Nat Neurosci* 6(7):697–698, doi:10.1038/nn1078): sleep-dependent learning of a texture-discrimination task occurred after brief (60–90 min) naps containing both SWS and REM; magnitude, stage-dependency and retinotopic specificity resembled an 8-h night; the authors conclude that for behavioural improvement a nap is "as good as a night of sleep" — for this perceptual task only (the abstract gives no N).
- [V] Milner & Cote 2009 (*J Sleep Res* 18(2):272–281, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2869.2008.00718.x) is a REVIEW (the task list calls it a meta-analysis — it is not); Lovato & Lack 2010 (*Prog Brain Res* 185:155–166, doi:10.1016/B978-0-444-53702-7.00009-9) is a review: benefits of brief (5–15 min) naps "almost immediate" and last "1–3 h"; naps > 30 min can produce sleep-inertia impairment for a short period and then improved performance for longer (up to many hours); early afternoon is the most favourable time; regular nappers benefit more than rare nappers; "accepted cautiously until more comprehensive research programmes are conducted".
- [V] Dutheil et al. 2021 (*Int J Environ Res Public Health* 18(19):10212, doi:10.3390/ijerph181910212, PMC8507757; systematic review + meta-analysis): 11 studies (all laboratory), N = 381 working-aged adults, mean nap 55.4 ± 29.4 min: overall cognitive performance improved after the nap (effect size 0.18, 95% CI 0.09–0.27), especially alertness (0.29, 0.10–0.48); improvements mainly lasted up to 120 min; "conflicting results during the sleep inertia period"; early afternoon naps (before 13:00) did better (0.24, −0.07 to 0.34); nap duration and time since nap did not influence performance; authors call for real work-setting studies.
- STUDY Short naps improve alertness and performance — original: Tietzel & Lack 2001 (N = 12), Brooks & Lack 2006 (N = 24): 10-min nap best after restricted sleep. Later evidence: Hilditch 2016 (N = 31, different lab, night-shift simulation): 10-min nap no inertia and protected performance; Dutheil 2021 meta-analysis (11 lab studies, N = 381): effect 0.18 overall, 0.29 for alertness.
  GRADE B: consistent direction in several labs and a meta-analysis, but small samples, mostly sleep-restricted young adults in the lab, brief follow-up (hours); the "best nap length" (10 vs 20 min) is from one lab's samples; the meta-analysis found nap duration did not matter. — Brooks & Lack 2006; Hilditch 2016; Dutheil 2021
- [✗] "Nap for exactly 20 minutes" — the controlled studies above favoured a 10-minute nap over 5, 20 and 30 minutes (Brooks & Lack 2006: 10 min best overall; 20-min benefits arrived after 35 min), a 30-min nap carried sleep inertia in two independent labs, and the 2021 meta-analysis found no effect of nap duration. Safe rule: "short (about 10–20 min), early afternoon, and allow a few minutes to shake off grogginess before doing anything safety-critical".
- [V] Rosekind et al. 1995 (*J Sleep Res* 4(Suppl 2):62–66, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2869.1995.tb00229.x; NASA Ames review of strategic naps): the abstract reports that a study of planned rest periods in long-haul flights showed in-flight naps promoted performance and alertness in the critical descent and landing phases; sleep inertia "should be a consideration" before nap strategies are implemented. THE ABSTRACT HAS NO NUMBERS: the popular "26-minute nap → +34% performance, +54% alertness" comes from the underlying NASA technical memorandum (1994), which I could not open; the task's N = 13 is unverified (my recollection of ~46 crew is also unverified) → COULD NOT VERIFY; if quoted at all, write "a NASA study of long-haul flight crews".
- [V] Napping and health (observational): Yamada, Hara, Shojima, Yamauchi & Kadowaki 2015 (*Sleep* 38(12):1945–1953, doi:10.5665/sleep.5246): meta-analysis of 11 prospective cohorts, 151,588 participants, mean follow-up 11 years: napping ≥ 60 min/day vs no napping — cardiovascular disease RR 1.82 (95% CI 1.22–2.71), all-cause mortality RR 1.27 (1.11–1.45); napping < 60 min/day not associated with CVD (P = 0.98) or mortality (P = 0.08); J-curve for CVD. Observational: long naps may mark illness, poor night sleep or low activity (reverse causation); "further studies are needed to confirm the efficacy of a short nap".
- STUDY Long naps go with higher mortality risk — original: Yamada 2015 (11 cohorts, 151,588 people; ≥ 60 min/day: CVD RR 1.82, mortality RR 1.27). Later evidence: none opened.
  GRADE C: consistent observational association, but strong alternative explanations (nap as a symptom of disease or sleep disorder) and no trial; do not say "long naps are harmful". — Yamada 2015

### 04-E · Light, timing and screens (光照 与 屏幕)
- [V] Light phase-response curve (PRC 相位反应曲线): Khalsa, Jewett, Cajochen & Czeisler 2003 (*J Physiol* 549(3):945–952, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.2003.040477, PMC2342968): 21 healthy entrained subjects, a 6.7 h bright-light exposure (≈ 10,000 lux fixed gaze alternating with 5,000–9,000 lux free gaze) given at different circadian phases to different people; the melatonin-midpoint PRC was a "characteristic type 1" curve with a peak-to-trough amplitude of 5.02 h: light before the core-body-temperature minimum delays the clock, light after it advances it, none at the critical phase.
- [V] Gooley et al. 2011 (*J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 96(3):E463–E472, doi:10.1210/jc.2010-2098, PMC3047226): retrospective analysis, 116 healthy volunteers aged 18–30 living in a clinical research centre for ≥ 5 days, room light (< 200 lux) vs dim light (< 3 lux) in the 8 h before bedtime: room light delayed melatonin onset in 99.0% of individuals and shortened melatonin duration "by about 90 min"; room light during the usual hours of sleep suppressed melatonin by > 50% in 85% of trials. CORRECTION to the task list ("room light before bed suppresses melatonin ~ 50%"): the > 50% figure is for light during the usual SLEEP hours; for the evening hours before bed the reported effects are the later onset (99%) and the ≈ 90-min shorter melatonin duration.
- [V] Phillips et al. 2019 (*PNAS* 116(24):12019–12024, doi:10.1073/pnas.1901824116, PMC6575863): 55 participants aged 18–30, within-subjects, 5 h evening exposures from < 1 lux (dim control) to 10–2,000 lux: group-level ED50 (light level for 50% melatonin suppression) = 24.60 lux; 10, 30 and 50 lux gave later apparent melatonin onsets by 22, 77 and 109 min; individual ED50 ranged from 6 to 350 lux (26% coefficient of variation) — "the same evening-light environment is registered by the circadian system very differently between individuals". Typical indoor light (30–300 lux) sits in the sensitive part of the curve.
- STUDY Evening light delays the clock, dose-dependently — original: Khalsa 2003 (N = 21 PRC); Gooley 2011 (N = 116, room vs dim light). Later evidence: Phillips 2019 (N = 55 dose–response: ED50 24.6 lux; onset delays 22/77/109 min at 10/30/50 lux; huge individual differences).
  GRADE A for the physiology (several labs and designs agree on direction and rough size; clock-phase and melatonin are the outcomes); it does NOT by itself show that your own evening lamp costs you a measured amount of sleep (individual sensitivity varies 6–350 lux). — Khalsa 2003; Gooley 2011; Phillips 2019
- [V] Light-emitting e-reader vs printed book: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler 2015 (*PNAS* 112(4):1232–1237, doi:10.1073/pnas.1418490112): within-subjects comparison of reading on a light-emitting device vs a printed book in the hours before bedtime: with the e-reader participants "took longer to fall asleep", had reduced evening sleepiness, reduced melatonin secretion, later circadian timing and reduced next-morning alertness. THE ABSTRACT GIVES NO NUMBERS and I could not open the full text: the often-quoted N = 12, "≈ 10 min longer to fall asleep", "≈ 1.5 h later melatonin" and "≈ 55% less melatonin" are [~] unverified here. The article has published comment letters (PNAS 2015;112:946–947; E1513; E1514 — titles not opened).
- [V] Contrasting tablet study: Heath, Sutherland, Bartel, Gradisar, Williamson, Lovato & Micic 2014 (*Chronobiol Int* 31(4):496–505, doi:10.3109/07420528.2013.872121): 16 healthy adolescents (mean 17.4 y, 56% female) used a bright tablet screen (80 lux), a dim screen (1 lux) and an f.lux-filtered screen (50 lux) for 1 h before habitual bedtime: bright vs dim differed on GO/NOGO speed (23 ms) and accuracy (13%) but the magnitudes were "small … suggesting minimal clinical significance"; no significant effects on sleep-onset latency, slow-rolling eye movements, or SWS/REM minutes in the first two cycles.
- STUDY Pre-bed e-reader light worsens sleep — original: Chang 2015 (within-subjects, light-emitting e-reader vs print book). Later evidence: Heath 2014 (N = 16 adolescents; small alertness differences, no sleep-latency effect); no independent replication of the e-reader sleep effects opened.
  GRADE C: a vivid single-lab result with partial non-confirmation in a different sample and device; the melatonin/clock effect is plausible (see Phillips 2019) but the practical sleep cost depends on brightness, distance, content and the person. — Chang 2015; Heath 2014
- [V] Screen use and sleep, observational: Carter, Rees, Hale, Bhattacharjee & Paradkar 2016 (*JAMA Pediatr* 170(12):1202–1208, doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341, PMC5380441): systematic review/meta-analysis of 20 cross-sectional studies, N = 125,198 children and adolescents (mean age 14.5 ± 2.2 y; 6–19 y): bedtime media-device USE vs inadequate sleep quantity OR 2.17 (95% CI 1.42–3.32; I² = 90%), poor sleep quality OR 1.46 (1.14–1.88; I² = 76%), excessive daytime sleepiness OR 2.72 (1.32–5.61; I² = 50%); mere ACCESS to a device at night: OR 1.79 (1.39–2.31), 1.53 (1.11–2.10), 2.27 (1.54–3.35). CHECK passed: the task list's "OR 2.17 inadequate sleep" is the QUANTITY outcome (verified); daytime sleepiness is 2.72, not 2.17; all 20 studies were cross-sectional (no RCT) and heterogeneity is high (I² 50–90%), so these odds ratios show association, not effect.
- [V] Hale & Guan 2015 (*Sleep Med Rev* 21:50–58, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2014.07.007, PMC4437561): systematic review of 67 studies (1999–early 2014) of screen time and sleep in school-aged children/adolescents: adverse association (mainly shorter duration and delayed timing) in 90% of studies; causal association "not confirmed", measurement error, little data on content or multi-screen use. Brautsch et al. 2023 (*Sleep Med Rev* 68:101742, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2022.101742): 60 observational studies of 16–25-year-olds identified, 42 moderate/high-quality analysed: digital media use associated with shorter sleep and poorer quality (general screen use, phone, computer, internet, social media) but NOT for television, game consoles or tablets; bedtime/night use mostly associated with poor sleep; causal direction unclear.
- [V] Size of the screen association: Przybylski 2019 (*J Pediatr* 205:218–223, doi:10.1016/j.jpeds.2018.09.054; preregistered; N = 50,212 US children, 2016 National Survey of Children's Health): each hour of digital screen use was associated with 3–8 fewer minutes of nightly sleep; children meeting the 2010/2016 AAP screen-time guidance had 20–26 more minutes; screen time accounted for "less than 1.9%" of variability in sleep outcomes; authors: "on its own, [it] has little practical effect on pediatric sleep". (Orben & Przybylski 2019, *Nat Hum Behav* 3(2):173–182 — three datasets, n = 355,358 — find a negative but small association between digital-technology use and adolescent WELL-BEING explaining at most 0.4% of variation; that is well-being, not sleep.)
- [V] Screen-curfew intervention: Perrault et al. 2019 (*Sleep* 42(9):zsz125, doi:10.1093/sleep/zsz125): students aged 12–19, actigraphy + diaries over 1 month — 2-week baseline, then a 40-min sleep-education workshop and a 2-week phase in which they were asked to stop screen use after 9 pm on school nights: reduced evening screen time correlated with earlier sleep onset and longer total sleep, and with better next-day vigilance. NOT a randomised trial (no control group; education + curfew bundled; abstract gives no N or minutes) → at most suggestive.
- STUDY Bedtime screen use goes with shorter sleep — original: Hale & Guan 2015 (67 studies, 90% adverse); Carter 2016 (20 studies, N = 125,198; OR 2.17 for inadequate sleep). Later evidence: Przybylski 2019 (N = 50,212; 3–8 min per hour; < 1.9% of variance); Brautsch 2023 (42 studies; not for TV/consoles/tablets); Perrault 2019 (uncontrolled 2-week curfew: longer sleep).
  GRADE B: direction is consistent across observational studies of many ages, but sizes are small (minutes, ~2% of variance), causation is unproven and the one curfew intervention opened was uncontrolled. Worth trying as a cheap self-experiment, not a proven lever. — Carter 2016; Przybylski 2019; Perrault 2019
- [V] ★ Blue-light-blocking glasses: Singh, Keller, Busija, McMillan, Makrai, Lawrenson, Hull & Downie 2023 (Cochrane review, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 8:CD013244, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2, PMC10436683): 17 RCTs (5–156 participants each; follow-up < 1 day to 5 weeks; none low risk of bias in all domains; no meta-analysis possible): eye strain "may be no different" (low certainty); visual acuity "probably little or no effect" (moderate certainty); sleep quality — the authors say it is not known whether blue-light-filtering lenses are equivalent or superior to ordinary lenses for sleep quality (very low-certainty evidence; paraphrase); 6 RCTs (148 participants): 3 reported significantly better sleep scores with blue-light-filtering lenses, 3 no difference; adverse events infrequent (increased depressive symptoms, headache, discomfort, lower mood in some arms). Serum melatonin was not evaluated by any trial.
- STUDY Blue-light-filtering glasses improve sleep — original: marketing and small trials; Cochrane synthesis 2023. Later evidence: none newer opened (as of October 2026 I could not confirm a newer review).
  GRADE C for sleep (mixed, very-low-certainty trials; 3 of 6 positive); eye-strain benefit is probably nil (low-certainty). The Cochrane authors' verdict: effects on sleep "indeterminate". — Singh 2023
- [V] Daylight and the clock: Wright et al. 2013 (*Curr Biol* 23(16):1554–1558, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2013.06.039): electrical lighting and the built environment go with less daytime sunlight, more light after sunset and a LATER circadian clock than camping under a natural summer 14 h 40 min : 9 h 20 min light–dark cycle; after natural light only, the internal biological night began at sunset and ended before wake-time; later chronotypes showed larger advances. Stothard et al. 2017 (*Curr Biol* 27(4):508–513, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2016.12.041): the biological night begins earlier after a week of natural WINTER light than in the modern environment, and a summer weekend camping achieved "∼69% of the shift in circadian timing" seen after a week. Small camping samples (N not in the abstracts), one research group.
- STUDY Natural daylight shifts the body clock earlier — original: Wright 2013 (one week of camping, natural summer light-dark). Later evidence: Stothard 2017 (winter week and summer weekend; weekend ≈ 69% of the week-long shift); same lab; no independent replication opened.
  GRADE B: direction follows from the replicated light-PRC physiology and both camping studies agree, but samples are small and from one group; it shows what outdoor light CAN do, not how much an office worker gets from a morning walk. — Wright 2013; Stothard 2017

### 04-F · CBT-I 失眠的认知行为疗法 and what the behavioural components are (no medication guidance)
- [def] CBT-I = a multicomponent programme (sleep education, stimulus control 刺激控制, sleep restriction 睡眠限制, cognitive therapy 认知治疗, often relaxation); its common elements are sleep diaries, patient investment, and "voluntary waking behaviors to influence sleep, which is a largely involuntary process"; typically 6–8 individual sessions, with brief and internet versions also tested. — Buysse 2013, *JAMA* 309(7):706–716 ("Insomnia" clinical review; NIH author manuscript PMC3632369, doi:10.1001/jama.2013.193; text read). Stimulus control origin: Bootzin 1972 (*Proc 80th Annu Conv APA* 7:395–396) — original not in PubMed/not opened → [~] credited, not verified.
- [V] Protocols as tabulated by Buysse 2013 (Table of behavioural therapies; text read): stimulus control — if unable to fall asleep within 10–20 min leave the bed and bedroom, return only when sleepy again, set an alarm and wake at a regular time every day, do not snooze, do not nap; sleep restriction — set bedtime/rising times limited to the average hours of actual sleep reported, advance bedtime 15–30 min when sleep is > 85% of time in bed, keep a fixed wake time, and if after 10 days sleep efficiency is < 85% restrict a further 15–30 min; "brief behavioural treatment" — limit time in bed to actual sleep time + 30 min, regular wake time, do not go to bed until sleepy, do not stay in bed if awake. (These are one reviewer's summary of the protocols, not a prescription for any individual.)
- [V] Definition and scope: "Insomnia is not defined by a specific sleep amount"; adequate OPPORTUNITY for sleep distinguishes insomnia from sleep deprivation; individuals with insomnia are reliably distinguished from good sleepers by self-reported latency or WASO > 30 min, while polysomnography shows "considerably more overlap", and many patients over-estimate latency/WASO and under-estimate duration ("altered perception of sleep"); prevalence of insomnia disorder ≈ 10–20% (12–20% annual), symptoms 35–50%; chronic course in 40–70% over 1–20 years. — Buysse 2013 (text read). NOTE: the ICSD-3/DSM-5 duration criterion (≥ 3 nights/week for ≥ 3 months) is not in any source I could open → COULD NOT VERIFY; say "persistent (months-long) difficulty with daytime consequences".
- [V] Spielman, Saskin & Thorpy 1987 (*Sleep* 10(1):45–56, PMID 3563247): the original sleep-restriction case series — 35 patients (mean age 46, mean insomnia history 15.4 years) treated for 8 weeks by marked restriction of time available for sleep followed by extension contingent on improved sleep efficiency; total sleep time rose (p < .05) and latency, total wake time, efficiency and subjective insomnia improved (all p < .0001); gains remained significant at a mean of 36 weeks in the 23 who were followed up; "compliance with the restricted schedule is difficult for some patients". Uncontrolled.
- [V] ★ Trauer, Qian, Doyle, Rajaratnam & Cunnington 2015 (*Ann Intern Med* 163(3):191–204, doi:10.7326/M14-2841): 20 RCTs (1,162 participants, 64% female, mean age 56), face-to-face multimodal CBT-I (≥ 3 of cognitive therapy, stimulus control, sleep restriction, sleep hygiene, relaxation) vs inactive comparators, diary outcomes, adults with chronic insomnia WITHOUT comorbid medical/sleep/psychiatric disorders; post-treatment improvements: sleep-onset latency 19.03 min (95% CI 14.12–23.93), wake after sleep onset 26.00 min (15.48–36.52), total sleep time 7.61 min (−0.51 to 15.74 — NOT statistically significant), sleep efficiency 9.91% (8.09–11.73); "changes seemed to be sustained at later time points"; no adverse outcomes reported; accuracy of later-time-point estimates "less clear". CHECK vs the task list: SOL −19 min, WASO −26 min, TST +8 min, SE +10% are right, but the TST gain is not distinguishable from zero.
- [V] van Straten, van der Zweerde, Kleiboer, Cuijpers, Morin & Lancee 2018 (*Sleep Med Rev* 38:3–16, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2017.02.001): 87 RCTs, 118 treatments (3,724 patients) vs non-treated controls (2,579 patients): Hedges' g — insomnia severity index 0.98, sleep efficiency 0.71, PSQI 0.65, wake after sleep onset 0.63, sleep-onset latency 0.57, sleep quality 0.40, number of awakenings 0.29, total sleep time 0.16 (smallest); face-to-face treatment with ≥ 4 sessions more effective than self-help or briefer face-to-face; "quite robust" across comorbid disease or not, younger vs older, medication use or not.
- [V] Furukawa et al. 2024 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 81(4):357–365, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2023.5060, PMC10794978; component network meta-analysis, 241 trials, 31,452 participants, searched to 21 July 2023, comorbid insomnia included): components with a clear contribution to remission — cognitive restructuring (incremental OR 1.68; 95% CI 1.28–2.20), third-wave components (1.49; 1.10–2.03), sleep restriction (1.49; 1.04–2.13), stimulus control (1.43; 1.00–2.05); sleep hygiene education "not essential" (iOR 1.01; 0.77–1.32); relaxation "potentially counterproductive" (0.81; 0.64–1.02); in-person therapist-led delivery best (1.83; 1.19–2.81); best combination (cognitive restructuring + third-wave + sleep restriction + stimulus control, in person) vs in-person psychoeducation: remission risk difference 0.33 (95% CI 0.23–0.43), NNT 3.0 (2.3–4.3) at a control event rate of 0.14; caveat: "potential undetected interactions could undermine the conclusions".
- [V] Sleep restriction as a single component: Miller et al. 2014 (*Sleep Med Rev* 18(5):415–424, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2014.01.006): 21 papers read in full, 4 trials of adequate strength: diary sleep-onset latency, WASO and sleep efficiency improved by moderate-to-large effects, total sleep time by a small one; objective outcomes and daytime functioning "insufficient"; implementation varied too much for "any strong conclusions".
- [V] Cost of restriction: Kyle, Miller, Rogers, Siriwardena, Macmahon & Espie 2014 (*Sleep* 37(2):229–237, doi:10.5665/sleep.3386): 16 patients (mean age 47.1) with psychophysiological insomnia (TST ≤ 6 h) in a within-subject uncontrolled lab study of single-component sleep restriction over 4 weeks: subjective sleep and insomnia severity improved, but PSG total sleep time FELL by an average of 91, 78 and 69 min on nights 1, 8 and 22 (effect sizes 1.60–1.80), PVT lapses rose (effect sizes 0.69–0.78) and reaction times slowed during acute treatment, and Epworth sleepiness rose in weeks 1–3; all returned to baseline by 3 months. Authors' stated implication: "safe and effective delivery" guidelines are needed. So the first weeks of restriction make you sleepier and less vigilant even as the diary improves.
- [V] Guidelines (behavioural first): Qaseem et al. 2016 (American College of Physicians, *Ann Intern Med* 165(2):125–133, doi:10.7326/M15-2175): ACP recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia disorder (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence; paraphrase) (the second recommendation concerns shared decision-making about short-term medication if CBT-I alone is unsuccessful — weak, low-quality; not reproduced here as advice). Edinger et al. 2021 (AASM, *J Clin Sleep Med* 17(2):255–262, doi:10.5664/jcsm.8986, PMC7853203): recommend multicomponent CBT-I (STRONG); suggest multicomponent brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction therapy and relaxation therapy as single-component therapies (each CONDITIONAL); suggest NOT using sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy (CONDITIONAL). Riemann et al. 2023 (*J Sleep Res* 32(6):e14035, doi:10.1111/jsr.14035; European Insomnia Guideline update): CBT-I is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults of any age, including with comorbidities, in person or digitally (evidence level A); light therapy and exercise "may be useful as adjunct therapies" to CBT-I (B); actigraphy is not recommended for routine insomnia evaluation (C).
- [V] Sleep hygiene alone: Chung et al. 2018 (*Fam Pract* 35(4):365–375, doi:10.1093/fampra/cmx122): 15 studies (12 compared with CBT-I, 3 with mindfulness, none with no-treatment or sham): SHE improved pre-to-post with a small-to-medium effect but was less efficacious than CBT-I (medium-to-large difference; ≈ 5% vs 8% sleep-efficiency gains), only subjective measures significant; no data on adherence or cost-effectiveness. Consistent with Edinger 2021 (against SHE alone) and Furukawa 2024 (iOR 1.01).
- STUDY CBT-I improves chronic insomnia — original: Spielman 1987 (restriction case series, N = 35); Morin 1999 (N = 78 randomised trial, 8 weeks with 3/12/24-month follow-up, CBT arm n = 18). Later evidence: Trauer 2015 (20 RCTs, N = 1,162: SOL −19 min, WASO −26 min, SE +9.9%, TST +7.6 min n.s.); van Straten 2018 (87 RCTs, ISI g = 0.98); Furukawa 2024 (241 trials, N = 31,452); guidelines from ACP 2016, AASM 2021, European 2023 (first-line).
  GRADE A: several independent meta-analyses and guidelines agree on direction and a clinically meaningful size, with benefits largely maintained at follow-up; sizes are largest for subjective diary measures and smallest for total sleep time, and effects are weaker/less certain for objective sleep, in comorbid insomnia and in self-help formats. — Trauer 2015; van Straten 2018; Furukawa 2024
- STUDY Delivering CBT-I digitally (dCBT-I) — original: Espie 2019 (N = 1,711, Sleepio vs sleep-hygiene education, 24 weeks); Ritterband 2017 (N = 303, SHUTi vs online education, 1 year). Later evidence: Freeman 2017 OASIS (N = 3,755 students; insomnia d = 1.11); Zachariae 2016 meta-analysis (11 RCTs, N = 1,460, g 0.21–1.09, comparable to face-to-face, maintained 4–48 weeks).
  GRADE A for the main effect on insomnia severity (several teams, thousands of participants; recommended by the 2023 European guideline); B for "as good as a therapist" (van Straten 2018 and Furukawa 2024 find in-person ≥ 4 sessions better than self-help); several trials are by the developers of the programmes tested (Espie is a Big Health/Sleepio cofounder per the COI statement). — Espie 2019; Ritterband 2017; Freeman 2017; Zachariae 2016
- STUDY Sleep hygiene education alone treats insomnia — original: standard sleep-hygiene advice (regular schedule, caffeine and alcohol limits, bedroom conditions); Chung 2018 (15 studies, 12 compared with CBT-I: small-to-medium pre-to-post improvement, ≈ 5% vs 8% sleep-efficiency gain, subjective measures only). Later evidence: Edinger 2021 (AASM suggests NOT using sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy, conditional); Furukawa 2024 (241 trials, N = 31,452: iOR 1.01 [0.77, 1.32], "not essential").
  GRADE C: hygiene advice is common-sense and cheap but, on its own, shows small subjective gains and clearly less than CBT-I; guidelines advise against it as the only treatment, so teach it as a supporting measure, not a cure. — Chung 2018; Edinger 2021; Furukawa 2024
- STUDY Stimulus control and sleep restriction work alone — original: Spielman 1987 (sleep-restriction case series, N = 35, uncontrolled); Bootzin 1972 (stimulus control; original not opened). Later evidence: Miller 2014 (4 adequately powered trials: moderate-to-large diary gains in latency, WASO and efficiency, small in total sleep time; objective outcomes insufficient); Furukawa 2024 (iOR 1.49 [1.04, 2.13] for sleep restriction, 1.43 [1.00, 2.05] for stimulus control); Edinger 2021 (conditional recommendations as single components); Kyle 2014 (N = 16 lab study: PSG total sleep time fell 91/78/69 min on nights 1/8/22 and PVT lapses rose during acute treatment).
  GRADE B: each component helps on diary measures in several independent syntheses, but implementation varies, objective evidence is thin, and sleep restriction makes people sleepier and less vigilant for the first weeks (so it needs guidance and a safety plan for driving); stronger as part of the multicomponent package. — Miller 2014; Furukawa 2024; Kyle 2014
- [V] Digital trials in detail: Espie 2019 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 76(1):21–30, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.2745; N = 1,711 with insomnia symptoms, 77.7% female, mean age 48.0; online, 2 arms; Sleepio-type dCBT vs sleep-hygiene education, both plus usual care; follow-up weeks 4, 8, 24): functional health +1.76 points (95% CI 1.24–2.28) at week 8 and +1.76 (1.22–2.30) at week 24 (PROMIS global health scale 10–50); wellbeing +2.68 → +2.95 (WEMWBS 14–70); sleep-related quality of life −17.60 (−20.81 to −14.39) at week 8 and −18.72 (−22.04 to −15.41) at week 24 on a 1–100 scale (large); the improvement in insomnia mediated 45.5–84.0%. Ritterband 2017 (*JAMA Psychiatry* 74(1):68–75, doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.3249; 303 adults with chronic insomnia, half with a comorbidity; 151 SHUTi vs 152 online patient education; 6-week automated programme): within-group effects d = 0.79 to 1.90 post-treatment; at 1 year SHUTi ISI d = 2.32 (2.01–2.63), latency d = 1.41, WASO d = 0.95; 56.6% (69/122) in remission and 69.7% (85/122) treatment responders at 1 year by ISI. Freeman et al. 2017 (*Lancet Psychiatry* 4(10):749–758, doi:10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30328-0; OASIS; 3,755 UK university students randomised 1:1 to digital CBT-I (n = 1,891) or usual practice (n = 1,864)): at 10 weeks insomnia −4.78 (95% CI 4.29–5.26; d = 1.11), paranoia −2.22 (d = 0.19), hallucinations −1.58 (d = 0.24); insomnia mediated the changes; no adverse events. NOTE: the task list's "Espie 2019 JAMA Psychiatry (SHUTi; sleepio: N = 1,711; ISI −?)" mixes two programmes — Espie 2019 is Sleepio (N = 1,711), SHUTi is Ritterband 2017 (N = 303).
- [V] Durability of the behavioural route (older evidence): Morin et al. 1999 (*JAMA* 281(11):991–999, doi:10.1001/jama.281.11.991; 78 adults, mean age 65, single centre): 8-week treatment, follow-up at 3, 12 and 24 months; time awake after sleep onset fell 55% with CBT vs 16.9% with placebo (46.5% and 63.5% in the other two active arms); the authors conclude that gains from behaviour therapy were sustained at follow-up whereas gains from drug therapy alone were not. Buysse 2013 summarises that acute CBT-I effects over 6–10 weeks "are maintained for up to 3 years of follow-up" (review statement).
- [V] Insomnia and depression: Baglioni et al. 2011 (*J Affect Disord* 135(1–3):10–19, doi:10.1016/j.jad.2011.01.011): 21 longitudinal studies; insomnia at baseline predicted depression at follow-up with OR 2.60 (random effects; 95% CI 1.98–3.42; heterogeneous) and, after adjusting for outliers, OR 2.10 (fixed effects; 1.86–2.38): "Non-depressed people with insomnia have a twofold risk to develop depression"; limitation: intervening variables not always considered.
- [V] ★ See-a-clinician boundaries (sourced; evidence, not advice): (1) Buysse 2013 (text read; paraphrase): evaluation by a sleep specialist is appropriate when there are signs of another sleep disorder — excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring or witnessed apneas, pronounced shifts in sleep timing, or unusual sleep behaviours or injury; "up to 50% of adults with obstructive sleep apnea also complain of insomnia"; polysomnography is not recommended for the routine assessment of insomnia but is appropriate when sleep apnoea or parasomnias are suspected. (2) Riemann 2023: polysomnography should be used to evaluate other suspected sleep disorders (for example periodic limb movement disorder, sleep-related breathing disorders) and in treatment-resistant insomnia (evidence level A; paraphrase). (3) Kapur et al. 2017 (AASM, *J Clin Sleep Med* 13(3):479–504, doi:10.5664/jcsm.6506): a STRONG recommendation against diagnosing obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) in adults from clinical tools, questionnaires or prediction algorithms without polysomnography or home sleep apnoea testing (paraphrase); PSG or home testing for adults with signs and symptoms indicating an increased risk of moderate-to-severe OSA. (4) USPSTF 2022 (*JAMA* 328(19):1945–1950, doi:10.1001/jama.2022.20304): in 2007–2010 an estimated 14% of men and 5% of women aged 30–70 had at least mild OSA (AHI ≥ 5) plus daytime sleepiness, and 13% of men and 6% of women had moderate-to-severe OSA (AHI ≥ 15); untreated OSA is associated with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, type 2 diabetes, cognitive impairment, reduced quality of life and motor-vehicle crashes; the Task Force finds the evidence "insufficient" to recommend screening in the general adult population (I statement — a statement about screening people without symptoms, not about evaluating symptoms). (5) Kyle 2014 (above): restriction temporarily raises sleepiness and lapses — a reason to do it under guidance and not before safety-critical driving/work when drowsy. (6) Baglioni 2011 (above): persistent insomnia raises depression risk; persisting low mood or hopelessness with sleep problems warrants a clinician (see 《心理学》第 17–18 课 and FACTS_T5; if there is any thought of self-harm, contact local emergency or crisis services immediately).
- [~] Not verified from opened sources: that CBT-I's effect sizes persist at 5+ years; that smartphone apps other than the studied programmes work; that sleep restriction is contraindicated in specific conditions (bipolar disorder, epilepsy, parasomnias): this is commonly stated but I found no opened guideline text → COULD NOT VERIFY (say "sleep restriction can leave you sleepier for the first weeks (Kyle 2014); ask a clinician first if you have another sleep, neurological or mood disorder, or drive/operate machinery for work").

### 04-G · Sleep, mood, metabolism, mortality, and school start times
- [V] Insomnia → depression: Baglioni 2011 — see 04-F (OR 2.10 fixed effects [1.86–2.38] after outlier adjustment; 2.60 [1.98–3.42] random effects, heterogeneous; 21 longitudinal studies).
- [V] Spiegel, Leproult & Van Cauter 1999 (*Lancet* 354(9188):1435–1439, doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(99)01376-8): 11 young men, time in bed limited to 4 h per night for 6 nights vs a recovery period of 12 h in bed per night for 6 nights: glucose tolerance lower in the sleep-debt condition (p < .02), thyrotropin lower (p < .01), evening cortisol higher (p = .0001), sympathetic activity higher (p < .02); authors: "The effects are similar to those seen in normal ageing". THE ABSTRACT HAS NO "40% lower glucose clearance": the often-quoted figure is [~] from the full text, not opened here. Design caveat: the comparison condition was 12 h in bed (more than a normal night).
- [V] Sleep extension RCT: Tasali, Wroblewski, Kahn, Kilkus & Schoeller 2022 (*JAMA Intern Med* 182(4):365–374, doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.8098, PMC8822469): single-centre RCT, 80 adults aged 21–40 with BMI 25.0–29.9 and habitual sleep < 6.5 h/night (mean age 29.8; 41 men); one individualised sleep-hygiene counselling session aimed at extending bedtime to 8.5 h vs continuing habitual sleep; energy intake measured objectively (doubly labelled water + body-energy-store change): sleep +1.2 h/night (95% CI 1.0–1.4) vs control; energy intake −270 kcal/day (−393 to −147; P < .001); change in sleep duration correlated inversely with change in intake (r = −0.41; −0.59 to −0.20); no significant change in total energy expenditure, so a negative energy balance and weight reduction in the extension group. Intervention length is not in the abstract I read (unverified here).
- STUDY Sleep extension reduces energy intake — original: Spiegel 1999 (lab sleep debt, N = 11, metabolic/endocrine changes). Later evidence: Tasali 2022 RCT (N = 80, sleep +1.2 h, intake −270 kcal/day). No independent replication of the RCT opened (as of October 2026).
  GRADE C: one well-designed single-centre RCT with a large, objectively measured effect, no independent replication yet, in short-sleeping adults with overweight only; the lab literature (N = 11) is small. Good reason to test sleep extension, not yet a settled lever. — Tasali 2022; Spiegel 1999
- [V] Short and long sleep, mortality and disease (observational): Cappuccio, D'Elia, Strazzullo & Miller 2010 (*Sleep* 33(5):585–592, doi:10.1093/sleep/33.5.585, PMC2864873): 16 prospective studies (27 cohort samples), 1,382,999 participants, 112,566 deaths: short sleep RR 1.12 (95% CI 1.06–1.18), long sleep RR 1.30 (1.22–1.38), both with between-study heterogeneity. Shen, Wu & Zhang 2016 (*Sci Rep* 6:21480, doi:10.1038/srep21480; 35 articles; 146,830 deaths among 1,526,609 participants for nighttime sleep): relative to 7 h/day, RR 1.07 (1.03–1.13) at 4 h, 1.04 (1.01–1.07) at 5 h, 1.01 (1.00–1.02) at 6 h, 1.07 (1.06–1.09) at 8 h, 1.21 (1.18–1.24) at 9 h, 1.37 at 10 h, 1.55 at 11 h — a U-shape with its minimum near 7 h and steeper risk on the LONG side. Jike et al. 2018 (*Sleep Med Rev* 39:25–36, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2017.06.011; 137 prospective cohorts, 5,134,036 participants): long sleep vs normal — mortality RR 1.39 (1.31–1.47), diabetes 1.26, CVD 1.25, stroke 1.46, CHD 1.24, obesity 1.08; hypertension not significant (1.01); "whether the relationship is causal and modifiable" is still open. Cappuccio 2011 (*Eur Heart J* 32(12):1484–1492, doi:10.1093/eurheartj/ehr007; 15 studies, 474,684 participants, 16,067 events): short sleep CHD RR 1.48 (1.22–1.80), stroke 1.15 (1.00–1.31), total CVD 1.03 (0.93–1.15, n.s.); long sleep CHD 1.38, stroke 1.65, CVD 1.41; the authors call both "predictors, or markers".
- STUDY Habitual sleep duration and mortality are U-shaped — original: Cappuccio 2010 (16 cohorts, 1.38 million). Later evidence: Shen 2016 (35 articles; minimum near 7 h; RR 1.04 at 5 h vs 1.21 at 9 h); Jike 2018 (long sleep RR 1.39). Self-reported duration, confounding (illness, depression, sleep apnoea), no trial.
  GRADE C: a replicated observational pattern whose causal reading is open; the SHORT-sleep excess is small (RR 1.01–1.12), the LONG-sleep excess is bigger, and "sleep more to live longer" is not implied. — Cappuccio 2010; Shen 2016; Jike 2018
- [V] Short sleep and obesity (cross-sectional): Cappuccio et al. 2008 (*Sleep* 31(5):619–626, doi:10.1093/sleep/31.5.619; 45 eligible, 30 pooled, 634,511 participants): children OR 1.89 (1.46–2.43), adults OR 1.55 (1.43–1.68); adults −0.35 BMI units per additional hour of sleep (−0.57 to −0.12); authors: "causal inference is difficult". Short sleep is therefore associated with obesity (A-level association, C-level cause); Tasali 2022 above is the only randomised test opened.
- [V] Sleep and inflammation (the evidence is NOT as simple as the popular version): Irwin, Olmstead & Carroll 2016 (*Biol Psychiatry* 80(1):40–52, doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2015.05.014, PMC4666828; 72 studies, n > 50,000): sleep disturbance associated with higher CRP (ES 0.12; 0.05–0.19) and IL-6 (0.20; 0.08–0.31); shorter duration with CRP (0.09; 0.01–0.17) but not IL-6 (0.03; −0.09 to 0.14); the extreme of long sleep with CRP (0.17) and IL-6 (0.11); no association with TNFα; "Neither experimental sleep deprivation nor sleep restriction was associated with CRP, IL-6, or TNFα". Conclusion (paraphrase): sleep disturbance and long sleep, "but not short sleep duration", were associated with higher markers of systemic inflammation.
- STUDY Short sleep causes inflammation — original: popular claim, supported by cohort associations with CRP. Later evidence: Irwin 2016 meta-analysis (72 studies): sleep disturbance and LONG sleep associate with CRP/IL-6; experimental deprivation/restriction did not change CRP, IL-6 or TNFα.
  GRADE C for "short sleep → inflammation" (experiments do not show it in these markers); B for "disturbed sleep goes with higher CRP/IL-6" (small ES 0.12–0.20, observational). — Irwin 2016
- [V] Descriptive and mechanism-level studies (no clinical outcome): Möller-Levet et al. 2013 (*PNAS* 110(12):E1132–E1141, doi:10.1073/pnas.1217154110): 26 participants, 1 week of insufficient sleep (5.70 h, SEM 0.03) vs 1 week of sufficient sleep (8.50 h, SEM 0.11), then blood RNA sampling during total sleep deprivation: 711 genes up- or down-regulated; genes with a circadian profile fell from 1,855 to 1,481; genes responding to subsequent total deprivation rose from 122 to 856. Descriptive molecular signature, no clinical outcome. Shokri-Kojori et al. 2018 (*PNAS* 115(17):4483–4488, doi:10.1073/pnas.1721694115): 20 healthy adults, PET with 18F-florbetaben after a rested night and after one night of total sleep deprivation: β-amyloid burden increased in the right hippocampus and thalamus after deprivation; the increase related to mood worsening, not to APOE genotype; baseline burden in several subcortical regions was inversely related to reported sleep hours. Small, single study; the abstract gives no percentages.
- [V] Adolescent school start times: American Academy of Pediatrics 2014 (*Pediatrics* 134(3):642–649, doi:10.1542/peds.2014-1697; policy statement): the evidence "strongly implicates" start times before 8:30 am as a "key modifiable contributor" to insufficient sleep (paraphrase); urges middle and high schools to aim for start times that allow 8.5–9.5 h of sleep. Minges & Redeker 2016 (*Sleep Med Rev* 28:86–95, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2015.06.002; 6 experimental studies: 3 pre–post no control, 2 RCTs, 1 quasi-experimental): start times delayed 25–60 min; sleep rose 25–77 min per weeknight; some studies found less sleepiness, depression, caffeine use, tardiness. Bowers & Moyer 2017 (*Sleep Health* 3(6):423–431, doi:10.1016/j.sleh.2017.08.004; meta-analysis of 5 longitudinal + 15 cross-sectional comparison studies): later start times associated with longer sleep, less sleepiness (7 studies) and less tardiness (3 studies); "cautious interpretation". Dunster et al. 2018 (*Sci Adv* 4(12):eaau6200, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aau6200; Seattle, start delayed by nearly an hour, pre/post): median daily sleep +34 min, median grades +4.5%, attendance improved (N not in the abstract; the "+4.5%" is the median-grade change). Widome et al. 2020 (*JAMA Pediatr* 174(7):697–704, doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2020.0344; cohort of 455 students in 5 Twin Cities high schools, two delaying start by 50 and 65 min vs three unchanged; wrist actigraphy; difference-in-differences): +41 min (95% CI 25–57) of objectively measured night sleep at follow-up 1 and +43 min (25–61) at follow-up 2; school-night sleep onset did not move later; weekend night sleep −24 and −34 min (less catch-up). Marx et al. 2017 (Cochrane, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 7:CD009467, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD009467.pub2): 11 unique studies, 297,994 participants; six studies on amount of sleep reported significant positive relationships; academic outcomes mixed; the authors rate the body of evidence "very low" quality and say they "cannot be confident about the effects". (Wahlstrom 2014 Minnesota multi-site report and Edwards 2012 Wake County economics paper were not opened — no numbers from them.)
- STUDY Later school start times lengthen adolescent sleep — original: observational/policy comparisons; Dunster 2018 Seattle pre–post. Later evidence: Widome 2020 (N = 455, difference-in-differences, actigraphy: +41/+43 min); Bowers & Moyer 2017 meta-analysis (20 studies); Minges & Redeker 2016 (6 experimental studies: +25 to +77 min); Cochrane 2017 (11 studies, very low-quality evidence).
  GRADE B: the sleep-duration effect (roughly +25 to +77 min per school night) replicates across designs, including an objectively measured controlled cohort; effects on grades/attendance/mood are mixed and the Cochrane quality rating is "very low". — Widome 2020; Minges 2016; Marx 2017

### 04-H · Myths and boundary cases (sleep need, banking, alcohol, counting sheep, glymphatic, trackers)
- [✗] "Everyone needs 8 hours": the National Sleep Foundation panel (Hirshkowitz et al. 2015, *Sleep Health* 1(1):40–43, doi:10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010; 18 experts, RAND/UCLA method) rated 7–9 h appropriate for young adults and adults, 7–8 h for older adults, 8–10 h for teenagers; the panel adds (paraphrase) that sleep needs vary across the lifespan and between people, that durations outside the recommended range may be appropriate, but that deviating far from the normal range is rare. The AASM/SRS joint consensus (Watson et al. 2015, *Sleep* 38(6):843–844, doi:10.5665/sleep.4716; *J Clin Sleep Med* 11(6):591–592, doi:10.5664/jcsm.4758) states that adults should sleep 7 or more hours regularly — [~] the statement text itself was behind the paywall (the abstract only says a recommendation was developed by a modified RAND Appropriateness Method); the evidence paper is Watson 2015, *Sleep* 38(8):1161–1183.
- [V] Individual variation is real but rare at the extreme: a family with a short-sleep phenotype carried a mutation in the transcriptional repressor DEC2 (hDEC2-P385R); transgenic mice carrying it slept less (He et al. 2009, *Science* 325(5942):866–870, doi:10.1126/science.1174443 — the task list's "P384R" is the paper's P385R). Kitamura et al. 2016 (*Sci Rep* 6:35812, doi:10.1038/srep35812; lab): "optimal sleep duration" varies among individuals; "potential sleep debt" (optimal minus habitual) tracked sleepiness better than actual sleep time; "only 1 h of PSD takes four days to recover to their optimal level" (N not in the abstract).
- [✗] "Modern life has cut our sleep a lot": Youngstedt et al. 2016 (*Sleep Med Rev* 28:69–85, doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2015.08.004, PMC4769964): 168 studies (1960–2013), 257 data points, 6,052 healthy normal-sleeping adults aged 18–88 with objectively recorded sleep: "no significant association of sleep duration with study year". Caveat: volunteers in lab/actigraphy studies are not a population sample. Yetish 2015 (04-A): pre-industrial groups slept 5.7–7.1 h.
- [V] "Banking" and weekend recovery — two small lab studies pull in different directions. Rupp, Wesensten, Bliese & Balkin 2009 (*Sleep* 32(3):311–321, doi:10.1093/sleep/32.3.311, PMC2647785): 24 healthy adults aged 18–39; one week of 10 h TIB ("Extended") vs habitual (mean 7.09 h) TIB, then 7 nights of 3 h TIB and 5 recovery nights (8 h TIB): the Habitual group had more PVT lapses and shorter maintenance-of-wakefulness latency during restriction, and the Extended group's PVT speed recovered faster after restriction; no group difference in subjective sleepiness; authors: sleep debt reflects "long-term (days/weeks) accommodative/adaptive changes" (the task list's N = 66 is wrong: 24). Depner et al. 2019 (*Curr Biol* 29(6):957–967, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069): 36 healthy young adults randomised to control (9 h sleep opportunities, n = 8), sleep restriction with no recovery (5 h; n = 14) or restriction with ad libitum weekend recovery (n = 14): weekend recovery sleep (≈ +1.1 h over baseline) did not prevent the next week's circadian delay, after-dinner energy intake rise and weight gain; whole-body insulin sensitivity fell ~13% under sustained restriction and by ~9–27% (whole-body, hepatic, muscle) with weekend recovery ("an ineffective strategy" in the authors' words). Banks 2010 (04-B): one 10-h night did not restore performance after 5 nights at 4 h (N = 159).
- STUDY Banking sleep and weekend catch-up — original: Rupp 2009 (N = 24: a week of 10 h TIB before 7 nights of 3 h reduced later lapses). Later evidence: Depner 2019 (N = 36: weekend recovery did not prevent metabolic dysregulation); Banks 2010 (N = 159: one recovery night insufficient); Kitamura 2016 (1 h of sleep debt ≈ 4 days to repay).
  GRADE C: small lab studies with different designs and outcomes; what is solid is that one long night does not erase a week of short ones and that weekend recovery did not protect metabolic measures in one RCT; "bank hours before a hard week" has one N = 24 lab trial behind it. — Rupp 2009; Depner 2019; Banks 2010
- [V] Alcohol and sleep: Ebrahim, Shapiro, Williams & Fenwick 2013 (*Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 37(4):539–549, doi:10.1111/acer.12006) — a QUALITATIVE review of "all known scientific studies" in healthy volunteers (the task list calls it a meta-analysis — it is not): at all doses alcohol shortens sleep-onset latency, gives a more consolidated first half of the night and more disruption in the second half; delays the first REM period at all doses (the most recognisable effect); reduces total-night REM percentage in most studies at moderate and high doses (no clear trend at low doses); increases slow-wave sleep in the first half of the night.
- STUDY Alcohol speeds sleep onset but fragments the night — original: folk belief that a nightcap helps you sleep (no single original study). Later evidence: Ebrahim 2013 qualitative review of lab studies (healthy volunteers): faster onset and more early SWS, but second-half disruption and delayed or reduced REM; a newer systematic review or meta-analysis could not be confirmed as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: the profile (earlier sleep, worse second half) is consistent across most lab studies, but the review is qualitative, doses/designs vary and I did not open a quantitative synthesis; "alcohol improves sleep" as a net statement is not supported. — Ebrahim 2013
- [V] "Counting sheep": Harvey & Payne 2002 (*Behav Res Ther* 40(3):267–277, doi:10.1016/s0005-7967(01)00012-2): 41 people with insomnia given, on an experimental night, instructions to distract using imagery, general instructions to distract, or no instructions: "imagery distraction" (brief training in an engaging imagery task) was associated with shorter sleep-onset latency and less pre-sleep cognitive activity than no instructions; the prediction that general distraction would be WORSE than no instruction was NOT supported. - STUDY Imagery distraction beats counting sheep — original: Harvey & Payne 2002 (N = 41 insomnia, one night). Later evidence: no independent replication found.

  GRADE C: an active, openly contested mouse literature (directly opposite results in 2013 and 2024), thin and indirect human data; teach as a hypothesis, never as "sleep detoxifies your brain". — Xie 2013; Miao 2024; Eide 2021

### 04-I · "Why We Sleep" (Walker 2017): which popular numbers hold up (treat as [~]: the critique is a blog; each point below was re-checked against the primary paper where one is cited)
- [~] Source of the critique: Alexey Guzey, "Matthew Walker's 'Why We Sleep' Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors" (guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/, 2019; I read ≈ 100,000 of its 104,228 characters through a summarising fetch tool and did NOT read Walker's published reply "Proposed corrections" — so I list only claims I could check in primary papers). - STUDY Shorter sleep means a shorter life (monotonic) — original: popular claim. Later evidence: Shen 2016 (35 articles, U-shape, min ≈ 7 h, RR ≤ 1.07 for 4–6 h); Cappuccio 2010 (RR 1.12 short vs 1.30 long); Jike 2018 (long sleep RR 1.39).
  GRADE D for the monotonic/strong form (a U-shaped observational association with small short-sleep excess; no trial); the modest "short sleepers have somewhat higher risk" claim is graded C in 04-G. — Shen 2016; Cappuccio 2010
- [✗] "Short sleep more than doubles cancer risk" (book p. 3, per the critique): Chen et al. 2018 (*BMC Cancer* 18:1149, doi:10.1186/s12885-018-5025-y; 65 studies from 25 articles, 1,550,524 participants, 86,201 cancer cases): categorical meta-analysis — short sleep OR 1.01 (0.97–1.05), long sleep OR 1.02 (0.97–1.07); dose–response not significant; short sleep associated with cancer in Asians (OR 1.36; 1.02–1.80) and long sleep with colorectal cancer (OR 1.21; 1.08–1.34) but not consistently; "long-term randomized controlled trials and well-designed prospective studies are needed".

Irwin et al. 1996 (*FASEB J* 10(5):643–653, doi:10.1096/fasebj.10.5.8621064; 42 healthy men, early-night deprivation 10 PM–3 AM): lower NK activity, LAK activity and IL-2 production — the abstract prints no percentages. So a "70% drop in NK cells" is a misreading of "activity at 72% of baseline".
- STUDY One short night lowers natural-killer activity — original: Irwin 1994 (N = 23 men, late-night partial deprivation: activity 72% of baseline in the group; reduced in 18 of 23).   GRADE C for "partial sleep loss lowers NK activity" (two studies, one lab, male volunteers; effect ≈ −28% in activity); D for "70% fewer NK cells". — Irwin 1994; Irwin 1996
- [V] "Sleep and testosterone — a man who sleeps 5–6 h has the testosterone of someone 10 years older": the primary study Leproult & Van Cauter 2011 (*JAMA* 305(21):2173–2174, doi:10.1001/jama.2011.710, PMC4445839): 10 healthy men (of 28 who consented; mean age 24.3, BMI 23.5), 3 nights of 10-h bedtimes then 8 nights of 5-h bedtimes (00:30–05:30; total sleep 4 h 48 min vs 8 h 55 min): daytime (08:00–22:00) testosterone 16.5 ± 2.8 vs 18.4 ± 3.8 nmol/L (P = .049), "decreased by 10% to 15%"; the authors note that normal ageing lowers testosterone by 1%–2% per year — the "10 years older" line is an inference from that comparison, not a finding; lower vigor scores; no cortisol difference; a "small convenience sample".
- [V] "Sleeping < 8 h → adolescent athletes get injured far more": Milewski et al. 2014 (*J Pediatr Orthop* 34(2):129–133, doi:10.1097/BPO.0000000000000151; 112 adolescent athletes who completed surveys of 160 invited; injury records reviewed retrospectively): athletes sleeping on average < 8 h were 1.7 times (95% CI 1.0–3.0; P = 0.04) more likely to have had an injury than those sleeping ≥ 8 h; each school grade up also 1.4× (1.2–1.6); Level III evidence. - [~] "Every species sleeps" (book p. 6 per the critique; Walker cites the *Encyclopedia of Sleep*): Geissmann, Beckwith & Gilestro 2019 (*Sci Adv* 5(2):eaau9253, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aau9253): some wild-type flies are "virtually sleepless" at baseline and complete forced sleep restriction is not necessarily lethal in wild-type *D. melanogaster* (paraphrase); the title reads "Most sleep does not serve a vital function". Safe wording: "sleep is very widespread in animals, but whether every species needs it, and whether all sleep is vital, is unsettled".

### Widget constants (04) — two-process sleep-pressure curve and caffeine decay
- [V] FORM of the homeostatic Process S 稳态过程 (McCauley, Kalachev, Smith, Belenky, Dinges & Van Dongen 2009, *J Theor Biol* 256(2):227–239, doi:10.1016/j.jtbi.2008.09.012, PMC2657297, text read): the original two-process model's homeostat is a pair of difference equations, rising in wake S_t = 1 − e^(−Δt/τ_r)(1 − S_(t−Δt)) and decaying in sleep S_t = e^(−Δt/τ_d) S_(t−Δt), with τ_r > 0 and τ_d > 0 the rise and decay time constants; the wake equation tends to an upper asymptote U = 1 and the sleep equation to a lower asymptote V = 0 (paraphrase). DERIVED closed forms for the widget (iterate the equations): awake for t hours from S0: S(t) = 1 − (1 − S0)·e^(−t/τ_r); asleep for t hours: S(t) = S0·e^(−t/τ_d).
- [V] THRESHOLDS and Process C 昼夜过程 (Skeldon, Derks & Dijk 2014, *PLoS One* 9(8):e103877, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0103877, PMC4118955, CC-BY; equations read as images): sleep begins when the homeostat reaches an upper threshold H⁺(t) = H0⁺ + a·C(t) and ends when it falls to a lower threshold H⁻(t) = H0⁻ + a·C(t), where C is a periodic function with a 24-h period; in the simplest case C(t) = sin(ω(t − α)) with ω = 2π/24 h; a five-harmonic form C(t) = 0.97 sin ω(t−α) + 0.22 sin 2ω(t−α) + 0.007 sin 3ω(t−α) + 0.03 sin 4ω(t−α) + 0.001 sin 5ω(t−α) "has also been used". Sleepiness: the paper states that in sleep-deprivation experiments the upper threshold is dropped, the homeostat keeps rising, and sleepiness is taken as "linearly related to the difference between the homeostat and the circadian process".
- [V] ★ A NUMERICALLY VERIFIED parameter set (Skeldon 2014, eq. 15, the "typical" values for the two-process model; read from the printed equation): upper asymptote μ = 21.35, H0⁺ = 15.5, H0⁻ = 14.5, a = 2.9, time constants χ_s = 45 h (decay in sleep) and χ_w = 45 h (rise in wake); wake→sleep form H(t) = μ + (H0 − μ)·e^((t0−t)/χ_w), sleep form H(t) = H0·e^((t0−t)/χ_s); the paper's simulated cycle has the homeostat minimum H_min = 12.51 at t = 15.31 h and maximum H_max = 15.07 at t = 30.67 h, i.e. 15.36 h awake and 8.64 h asleep per 24 h. The paper does not print α; if the wake-up is placed at 07:00 the same fit puts C's minimum near 04:00 and its maximum near 16:00 (my fit, not a published value). ## 05 · habits 习惯 (behavioural techniques only; no medical advice)

### 05-A · What a habit is, how it is measured, how much of life it covers
- [def] 习惯 habit — Gardner 2015 (*Health Psychol Rev* 9(3):277–295, doi:10.1080/17437199.2013.876238, PMC4566897; scoping review of 136 empirical studies and 8 reviews; abstract read): definitions of "habit" have varied, often implicitly; proposed definition (paraphrase) = a process in which a stimulus triggers an impulse to act through a learned stimulus–response association; that impulse can compete or combine with deliberate decisions and need not produce behaviour; most habit research is correlational and self-reported.
- [def] Wood & Neal 2007 (*Psychol Rev* 114(4):843–863, doi:10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843; abstract read), paraphrase: habits come from slowly learned associations between a response and features of the performance context (place, preceding action); once formed, perceiving the context triggers the response without a goal in between; habit associations change slowly and barely shift with current goals or occasional counter-habitual acts; goals matter by motivating the repetition that builds the habit and the exposure to its cues. Wood & Rünger 2016 (*Annu Rev Psychol* 67:289–314, doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417; abstract read), paraphrase: habits form when goals are pursued by repeating the same response in the same context; habits and deliberate goal pursuit work together, habit being the efficient default; people infer from how often they do something that they must have intended it; the review applies habit science to stress, addiction and the design of health and consumer interventions.
- [V] Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 83(6):1281–1297, PMID 12500811; abstract read): two diary studies with hourly reports — during habitual behaviour (done almost daily in stable contexts) people more often thought about unrelated things; during nonhabitual behaviour thoughts matched the action; habitual behaviour went with less stress. The abstract prints no share of life that is habitual: the often-quoted "about 43%" is second-hand here → [~]; see FACTS_T3b STUDY "Share of everyday behaviour that is habitual" (35% and 43% there).
- [def] 自我报告习惯指数 SRHI (Verplanken & Orbell 2003, *J Appl Soc Psychol* 33:1313–1330, doi:10.1111/j.1559-1816.2003.tb01951.x — bibliographic record only): 12 items on a 7-point agreement scale covering history of repetition, lack of awareness, lack of control, efficiency and identity (description taken from Lally 2010's methods); example items "I do automatically", "I do without thinking", "I would find hard not to do". Lally's automaticity subscale = 7 of the 12 items (items 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10), score 0–42, Cronbach's α = .90 at baseline. Gardner, Abraham, Lally & de Bruijn 2012 (*Int J Behav Nutr Phys Act* 9:102, doi:10.1186/1479-5868-9-102, PMC3552971; abstract read): a 4-item automaticity subscale of the SRHI, the SRBAI 自动性指数, was reliable, correlated with behaviour and moderated the intention–behaviour link (habit weakens the role of intention); recommended for studies tracking habit formation or disruption. SRHI/SRBAI are SELF-REPORT: no objective automaticity measure was used in Lally 2010 (the authors say future work needs one).
- [V] Brain basis, one line only: Graybiel 2008 (*Annu Rev Neurosci* 31:359–387, doi:10.1146/annurev.neuro.29.051605.112851; review abstract): routines and rituals become habitual and stereotyped through experience-dependent plasticity in basal-ganglia circuits. The lesson's "habit loop" (cue → routine → reward, popularised by Duhigg 2012, a trade book not opened) is a teaching frame [~]; the experimental evidence for context cueing is in 05-C.

### 05-B · How long a habit takes (the Lally curve, the myths, newer data)
- [V] Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010 (*Eur J Soc Psychol* 40(6):998–1009, doi:10.1002/ejsp.674; full text read, repository copy), DESIGN: students recruited by email; 101 attended, 96 took part (30 men, 66 women; mean age 27, range 21–45; predominantly postgraduates; 65% White); each chose a healthy eating, drinking or exercise behaviour they did not already do and did it once a day after a fixed daily cue (e.g. "running for 15 minutes before dinner"; 27 eating, 31 drinking, 34 exercise, 4 other) for 84 days, logging daily whether they did it plus the 7-item SRHI automaticity score (0–42; baseline median 3, Q1:Q3 0:9); £30 for taking part regardless of results, no reward for the behaviour; 14 stopped logging before day 60 → 82 analysable (median 47 of 84 days logged).

### 05-C · Context cues, friction and environment design (and the Wansink cautionary tale)
- [V] Wood, Tam & Witt 2005 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 88(6):918–933, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.88.6.918; abstract read; N not in the abstract): students transferring to a new university — exercising, newspaper-reading and TV-watching habits survived the move only when aspects of the performance context stayed the same (e.g. still reading the paper with others); disrupted habits fell back under intentional control, so people acted on current intentions; changes in intentions alone did not explain the disruption; nonhabitual behaviour was guided by intentions throughout.
- [V] Neal, Wood, Wu & Kurlander 2011 (*Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 37(11):1428–1437, doi:10.1177/0146167211419863; abstract read; N not in the abstract): two field experiments — habitual popcorn eaters in a cinema ate equal amounts of stale and fresh popcorn and were barely moved by hunger or liking; this held only in the context tied to past eating (the cinema, not a meeting room) and only when eating with the dominant hand (so the response could run automatically); people with weaker habits ate because they liked the popcorn.
- [V] Verplanken & Roy 2016 (*J Environ Psychol* 45:127–134, doi:10.1016/j.jenvp.2015.11.008; abstract via Semantic Scholar): field experiment, 800 participants, half recently relocated and matched to non-movers; an intervention promoting sustainable behaviours (25 self-reported behaviours, baseline and 8 weeks) worked better among recent movers; the authors read the "window of opportunity" as about three months after moving.
- [V] Phillips & Gardner 2016 (*Health Psychol* 35(1):69–77, doi:10.1037/hea0000249; abstract read): 123 healthy adults with baseline and 1-month surveys plus daily diaries — instigation habit (automatically starting the session) was the only unique predictor of exercise frequency, and changes in frequency tracked changes in instigation habit, not execution habit. Single study, self-report.
- STUDY Habits persist only in the context that cues them — original: Wood 2005 (students changing university; habits survived only with an unchanged context); Neal 2011 (two cinema field experiments: stale-popcorn eating persisted only in the cinema and with the dominant hand). Later evidence: Verplanken & Roy 2016 (N = 800, field experiment: new-context window ≈ 3 months, self-report); Wood & Rünger 2016 review.
  GRADE B: the same pattern appears across designs and labs (natural experiment, field experiments, relocation study), but samples are modest, behaviours are self-reported or observed in single settings, and how much a deliberate context change helps someone BUILD a new habit is not measured. — Wood 2005; Neal 2011; Verplanken & Roy 2016
- [V] ★ Hollands, Shemilt, Marteau et al. 2015 (Cochrane, *CDSR* 2015(9):CD011045, doi:10.1002/14651858.CD011045.pub2, PMC4579823; abstract read): 72 randomised studies (1978–2013; 96% food; mostly lab or short-term; unclear or high risk of bias); 86 comparisons from 58 studies (6,603 participants): larger portions, packages or tableware raised consumption, SMD 0.38 (95% CI 0.29–0.46), moderate-quality evidence — adults 0.46 (0.40–0.52), children 0.21 (0.10–0.31); selection of food SMD 0.42 (0.24–0.59; 13 comparisons), adults 0.55 but children 0.14 (−0.06 to 0.34, n.s.); the authors' extrapolation: if smaller sizes were sustained across the whole diet, average daily energy could fall by 144–228 kcal (8.5–13.5% of a 1,689 kcal baseline) in UK children and adults; unclear whether shrinking portions at the small end works as well; no clear implications for alcohol or tobacco. ### 05-D · If-then plans (implementation intentions) and the intention–behaviour gap
- [def] 执行意图 / if-then 计划 implementation intention — Gollwitzer 1999 (*Am Psychol* 54(7):493–503, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493; abstract via OpenAlex): a plan of the form "Whenever situation x arises, I will initiate the goal-directed response y", which hands control of the response to an anticipated cue so that it starts automatically; contrasted with a goal intention ("I intend to achieve z"). The same abstract notes that strong goal intentions are realised more often than weak ones, so the question is how much a plan adds.
- [V] Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 73(1):186–199, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.73.1.186; abstract via OpenAlex): Study 1 (correlational) — difficult goal intentions were completed about three times as often when they came with implementation intentions; Study 2 (experimental, same difficult goal for everyone, half instructed to form plans) — the benefit paralleled Study 1; Study 3 — plans made people start the goal-directed action as soon as the intended opportunity appeared. The task list's "Christmas Eve report 62% vs 32% / 71% vs 32%" is NOT in the abstract → COULD NOT VERIFY.
- [V] ★ Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 (*Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38:69–119, doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1; abstract via Semantic Scholar): meta-analysis of 94 independent tests — implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large size, d = .65, on goal attainment (they helped start goal striving, shield it from distraction, drop failing courses and conserve effort, and raised cue accessibility and response automation). Authored by the concept's originator; N and CI are not in the abstract.
- [V] Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024 (*Eur Rev Soc Psychol*, doi:10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563; abstract via Semantic Scholar; again originator-authored): 642 independent tests — planning worked for cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes, .27 ≤ d ≤ .66 depending on outcome; effects were larger for if-then (contingent) formats, highly motivated participants and rehearsed plans. This is the most recent synthesis I could open as of October 2026; I found no independent, bias-adjusted estimate.
- [V] Adriaanse, Vinkers, De Ridder, Hox & De Wit 2011 (*Appetite* 56(1):183–193, doi:10.1016/j.appet.2010.10.012; abstract read): 23 studies on eating — implementation intentions raised intake of healthy foods (d = .51) and cut unhealthy eating less (d = .29); better outcome measures and weaker control conditions went with larger effects, so some estimates may be inflated.
- [V] Milkman, Beshears, Choi, Laibson & Madrian 2011 (*PNAS* 108(26):10415–10420, doi:10.1073/pnas.1103170108, PMC3127912; abstract read; N not in the abstract): field experiment at a large firm's free on-site flu clinics; all eligible employees got a reminder; control vaccination rate 33.1%; adding a prompt to write down the planned DATE raised it by 1.5 points (not significant); a prompt for DATE AND TIME raised it by 4.2 points (significant) — DERIVED relative gain 4.2/33.1 ≈ 13%.
- [V] Orbell, Hodgkins & Sheeran 1997 (*Pers Soc Psychol Bull* 23(9):945–954, doi:10.1177/0146167297239004; abstract read; no numbers in the abstract): subjects who formed plans about where and when to perform breast self-examination were more likely to do it in the following month (self-report); plans worked by aiding retrieval of the intention and reduced how well past behaviour predicted future behaviour ("mimic the effect of habit").
- [V] Webb & Sheeran 2006 (*Psychol Bull* 132(2):249–268, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249; abstract read): 47 experimental tests with random assignment — a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36).
- [V] Rhodes & Dickau 2012 (*Health Psychol* 31(6):724–727, doi:10.1037/a0027290; abstract read): 11 experiments in physical activity whose interventions did change intention: d+ = .45 (95% CI .30–.60) for intention but d+ = .15 (.06–.23) for behaviour — the authors call the link weak. (The task list describes this paper as a meta-analysis of intervention "fade"; it is about the intention–behaviour link, not durability.)
- [V] Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013 (*Br J Health Psychol* 18(2):296–309, doi:10.1111/bjhp.12032; abstract read): 10 studies, N = 3,899 — 42% intended and were active, 36% intended but were not, 21% neither intended nor were active, 2% were active without intending; the abstract's overall "intention–activity gap" is 46% (36 of the 78% who intended; the statement-of-contribution text says 48%).
- [V] Wang, Wang & Gai 2021 (*Front Psychol* 12:565202, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202, PMC8149892; abstract read): mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII, the WOOP method) — 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, 15,907 participants: g = 0.336 (small-to-medium); larger when delivered through interaction with an experimenter (g = 0.465) than through documents (g = 0.277); the authors note some publication bias, so true effects may be smaller.
- STUDY If-then plans raise goal attainment — original: Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997 (goals completed about 3× as often with plans in Study 1, experimentally paralleled in Study 2); Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 (94 tests, d = .65). Later evidence: Adriaanse 2011 (23 diet studies: d = .51 to eat more healthy food, .29 to eat less unhealthy food); Milkman 2011 (flu shots: +4.2 points on a 33.1% base for date and time, +1.5 n.s. for date only); Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024 (642 tests, .27 ≤ d ≤ .66).
  GRADE B: direction is consistent across hundreds of tests and independent field experiments, but the headline sizes come from the originators' own meta-analyses, effects are smaller for stopping a habit than starting one and in real-world health settings, some control conditions inflate estimates, and no bias-adjusted estimate was found. — Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006; Adriaanse 2011; Milkman 2011; Sheeran 2024
- STUDY Strong intentions often fail to become action — original: Webb & Sheeran 2006 (47 experimental tests: intention d = 0.66 → behaviour d = 0.36). Later evidence: Rhodes & Dickau 2012 (11 physical-activity experiments: intention d+ = .45 → behaviour d+ = .15); Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013 (10 studies, N = 3,899: 36% intended but did not act, an intention–activity gap of 46%).
  GRADE A: three independent syntheses agree that changing intention moves behaviour far less than it moves intention; the size of the gap varies by domain and measure. — Webb & Sheeran 2006; Rhodes & Dickau 2012; Rhodes & de Bruijn 2013
- STUDY Mental contrasting plus if-then plans (MCII) — original: Oettingen's mental-contrasting experiments (not opened); Wang 2021 (21 studies, 24 effect sizes, N = 15,907: g = 0.336). Later evidence: Wang 2021 itself reports some publication bias; no independent preregistered replication found as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: a small-to-medium effect from many studies, stronger with face-to-face delivery and bounded by publication-bias concerns and the small number of studies per setting. — Wang 2021

### 05-E · Willpower as a depleting fuel tank (ego depletion 自我损耗): what failed, what to keep
- [V] Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice 1998 (*J Pers Soc Psychol* 74(5):1252–1265, doi:10.1037//0022-3514.74.5.1252; abstract read; N not in the abstract): four experiments — people made to eat radishes instead of tempting chocolates later quit sooner on unsolvable puzzles; making a meaningful personal choice, suppressing emotion, or an earlier high-regulation task each reduced later persistence or performance; read as one limited resource ("ego depletion").
- [V] Hagger, Wood, Stiff & Chatzisarantis 2010 (*Psychol Bull* 136(4):495–525, doi:10.1037/a0019486; abstract read): meta-analysis of 83 studies finding a significant depletion effect on later self-control performance (the abstract prints no d; Carter & McCullough 2014 report it as d = 0.62), plus effects on effort, perceived difficulty, negative affect, fatigue and blood glucose.
- [V] Carter & McCullough 2014 (*Front Psychol* 5:823, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00823, PMC4115664; abstract read): re-analysing the d = 0.62 meta-analysis with small-study-effect corrections found very strong signs of publication bias and a corrected effect no different from zero; Carter, Kofler, Forster & McCullough 2015 (*J Exp Psychol Gen* 144(4):796–815, doi:10.1037/xge0000083; abstract read): a series of focused meta-analytic tests found "very little evidence" for the depletion effect with the usual lab methods and challenged the resource idea.
- [V] ★ Hagger et al. 2016 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(4):546–573, doi:10.1177/1745691616652873; Registered Replication Report; abstract read): 23 laboratories, total N = 2,141, standardised sequential-task protocol (Sripada et al.): d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15].
- [V] Vohs et al. 2021 (*Psychol Sci* 32(10):1566–1581, doi:10.1177/0956797621989733, PMC12422598; abstract read): preregistered, 36 laboratories, N = 3,531, two procedures: confirmatory d = 0.06 (n.s.); a Bayesian test with an informed prior (δ = 0.30, SD 0.15) found the data 4 times more likely under the null; exploratory full-sample d = 0.08 (significant; Bayes about even); an exploratory hint that depletion was larger in participants reporting more fatigue, with no moderation by trait self-control, willpower beliefs or action orientation.
- [V] Friese, Loschelder, Gieseler, Frankenbach & Inzlicht 2019 (*Pers Soc Psychol Rev* 23(2):107–131, doi:10.1177/1088868318762183; abstract read): weighing the arguments for and against, the authors judge the evidence inconclusive despite several hundred published studies. Inzlicht & Schmeichel 2012 (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 7(5):450–463, doi:10.1177/1745691612454134; abstract read): the "process model" — exerting self-control shifts motivation and attention (so later control feels less worthwhile), instead of draining a fuel. Both are conceptual, not new tests.
- STUDY Willpower depletes like a fuel tank — original: Baumeister 1998 (4 experiments: radishes vs chocolate, then unsolvable puzzles; N not in the abstract); Hagger 2010 (83 studies; d = 0.62 per Carter & McCullough 2014). Later evidence: Carter & McCullough 2014 / Carter 2015 (bias-corrected effect ≈ 0); Hagger 2016 (23 labs, N = 2,141: d = 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15]); Vohs 2021 (36 labs, N = 3,531: d = 0.06, Bayes 4:1 for the null); Friese 2019 (inconclusive).
  GRADE D: two large preregistered multi-lab tests found effects near zero and bias-corrected meta-analyses agree; teach as a caution and as an example of how a famous effect shrinks under replication (same verdict as FACTS_T1's STUDY on the ego-depletion RRR). Keep: tiredness and shifting motivation matter (exploratory hint in Vohs 2021; process model), which is a different claim from a depletable resource. — Hagger 2016; Vohs 2021; Carter 2015

- [V] Temptation bundling 诱惑捆绑 — Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014 (*Manage Sci* 60(2):283–299, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784; abstract via Semantic Scholar; N not in the abstract): participants were randomised to a full treatment (gym-only access to tempting audiobooks), an intermediate treatment (encouragement to listen only at the gym) or control; at first the full and intermediate groups visited the gym 51% and 29% more often than controls, but the effects declined over time (particularly after Thanksgiving); afterwards 61% chose to pay for gym-only access to audiobook iPods.
- STUDY Temptation bundling raises gym visits — original: Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014 (field experiment, gym-only audiobooks: +51% visits at first; +29% for the encouragement-only arm). Later evidence: the same paper — effects declined over time, especially after Thanksgiving; no independent replication opened as of October 2026.
  GRADE C: one randomised field study by the originating team with a decaying effect; plausible mechanism (an immediate reward attached to a delayed-reward behaviour) but thin replication. — Milkman 2014
- [V] Fresh-start effect 新开始效应 — Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014 (*Manage Sci* 60(10):2563–2582, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901; abstract via Crossref): three ARCHIVAL field studies — Google searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments all rose after temporal landmarks (new week, month, year, semester, birthday, holiday); proposed mechanism: landmarks open new mental-accounting periods that set past failures aside.   GRADE B: reliable while the game runs and in several designs, but durability is partial, effects are small-to-moderate and the meta-analysis concerns education, not everyday habits; the 'streak' mechanic itself has no peer-reviewed test opened. — Patel 2019; Sailer & Homner 2020
- [V] Habit-based advice programmes — Lally, Chipperfield & Wardle 2008 (*Int J Obes* 32(4):700–707, doi:10.1038/sj.ijo.0803771; exploratory randomised trial, abstract read): 104 overweight or obese adults; a leaflet on habit formation with simple eating/activity tips plus a self-monitoring checklist (weekly or monthly weighing; n = 33 and 36) vs wait-list (n = 35) for 8 weeks; mean loss 2.0 kg vs 0.4 kg; among those still in the study at 32 weeks the average loss was 3.8 kg (54% lost ≥ 5%), reduced to 2.6 kg (26% ≥ 5%) with last-observation-carried-forward. - STUDY Habit-based advice (10 Top Tips) aids weight loss — original: Lally 2008 (N = 104, wait-list control: −2.0 kg vs −0.4 kg at 8 weeks). Later evidence: Beeken 2017 (N = 537 in primary care: −0.87 kg [−1.47, −0.27] at 3 months; no lasting advantage over usual care by 24 months).
  GRADE B: a small, cheap, short-term benefit confirmed in a pragmatic trial, but both trials come from the same UCL group, the effect is under 1 kg against usual care and did not last. — Lally 2008; Beeken 2017
- [V] Maintenance, in general — Fjeldsoe, Neuhaus, Winkler & Eakin 2011 (*Health Psychol* 30(1):99–109, doi:10.1037/a0021974; systematic review, abstract read): of 157 physical-activity or diet intervention trials, 35% reported maintenance data; of 29 qualifying trials 21 (72%) showed a significant between-group difference both after the programme and at least 3 months later; common features: longer than 24 weeks, face-to-face contact, more than 6 strategies, follow-up prompts (and, oddly, higher attrition and women-only samples) — i.e. maintenance is rarely reported, often achieved when measured, and may be selectively reported.
- [V] "Tiny Habits" 微习惯 and the Fogg model: the Fogg Behavior Model (behaviour occurs when motivation, ability and a prompt coincide; "B = MAP") — Fogg 2009, *Persuasive '09*, doi:10.1145/1541948.1541999 (bibliographic record via Semantic Scholar; text not opened) → [def]/[~]; the Tiny Habits method (anchor to an existing routine, start tiny, celebrate; book 2019) and Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) are practitioner books I did not open. - STUDY Tiny Habits and habit stacking (Fogg, Clear) — original: practitioner models (Fogg 2009 model; Tiny Habits 2019; Atomic Habits 2018) with no controlled test of habit formation found. Later evidence: Front Public Health 2022 RCT (N = 154: gratitude +6.9/+5.6 vs +2.5; outcome = gratitude); Keller 2021 (N = 192: linking to a routine = linking to a clock time for habit formation).
  GRADE C: coherent with context-cue and plan evidence (05-C, 05-D), but the named methods have no independent test of habit formation; the only RCT tested gratitude and was delivered by the method's own coaches. ### Widget constants (05) — the habit-strength curve and a days-to-80%-of-plateau calculator
- [V] FORM (Lally 2010, full text read): automaticity y (SRHI automaticity subscale, 0–42) after x days of daily repetition = a − b·e^(−c·x), Mitscherlich's law of diminishing returns, with a = plateau, b = gap between the plateau and the modelled value at day 0 (so the modelled start is y(0) = a − b), c = rate constant per day, all positive; time to 95% of the plateau = ln(20b/a)/c (the paper's formula as extracted shows ln(a/20b)/c with a lost minus sign; the corrected algebra is mine). The paper chose 95% because curves visibly level off there and 90% sits clearly below the plateau.
- [V] ★ PARAMETERS (Lally 2010 Table 2; the 39 of 82 people whose curve fitted well; median, Q1:Q3, min–max): a = 35 (29:43; 21–48); b = 30 (23:39; 13–51); c = 0.042 per day (0.028:0.064; 0.010–0.170); time to 95% = 66 days (39:102; 18–254); R² = 0.88 (0.84:0.94; 0.72–0.98). Baseline automaticity score in all 96: median 3 (Q1:Q3 0:9); fitted median start a − b = 5. Behaviour presets (medians, not significantly different): plateau 34 / 35 / 35 and days to 95% = 65 / 59 / 91 for eating / drinking / exercise (n = 10 / 15 / 13). Only 7 people ever reached the maximum 42; the model may exceed 42 (plateaus up to 49 were allowed).
- [V] MISSED DAY (Lally 2010): 140 misses in 55 people → next-day automaticity −0.29 points on average (a "very small decrease"), recovering as the behaviour resumes; no longer-term cost found. Widget rule (DERIVED design suggestion, not a published model): subtract about 0.3 points once, keep the day counter running, and show a clear note that the data say nothing about gaps of a week or more.
- DERIVED: days to reach a fraction f of the plateau, t_f = ln(b/((1 − f)·a))/c (0 if the start y0 = a − b already exceeds f·a); at the median parameters (a = 35, b = 30, c = 0.042): 50% → 12.8 d; 80% → 34.6 d; 90% → 51.2 d; 95% → 67.7 d (vs the reported median 66 — a median of ratios is not the ratio of medians); 80% of the GAIN above the start (y0 → a) takes ln 5/c = 38.3 d; the curve reaches the scale midpoint 21 at day 18.1 (a convenience marker only — the paper's "below 21 = not a habit" rule applies to the PLATEAU, not to a day count).
- DERIVED: effect of the starting automaticity y0 (a = 35, c = 0.042, so b = 35 − y0), days to 80% / 95% of the plateau: y0 = 0 → 38.3 / 71.3; 3 → 36.2 / 69.2; 5 → 34.6 / 67.7; 10 → 30.3 / 63.3; 15 → 25.0 / 58.0; 20 → 18.1 / 51.2; 25 → 8.5 / 41.5; 28 → 0 / 33.0 (already above 80% of the plateau).
- DERIVED: effect of the rate c (a = 35, b = 30): c = 0.010 (minimum) → 80% at 145.5 d, 95% at 284.2 d; 0.028 (Q1) → 52.0 / 101.5; 0.042 (median) → 34.6 / 67.7; 0.064 (Q3) → 22.7 / 44.4; 0.170 (maximum) → 8.6 / 16.7. Mixing quartiles of different parameters does not describe real people; use as sensitivity only.
- DERIVED: median-parameter trajectory y(x): day 0 → 5.0; 7 → 12.6; 14 → 18.3; 21 → 22.6; 30 → 26.5; 45 → 30.5; 60 → 32.6; 66 → 33.1; 84 → 34.1; 100 → 34.6; 150 → 34.9. Daily gain b·c·e^(−c·x): 0.94 points/day at day 7 and 0.36 points/day at day 30, so one miss (−0.29) costs about a third of a day's progress early on and about 0.8 of a day at day 30.
- [V] Widget caveats: the curve describes the 39 of 82 analysable volunteers for whom a curve fitted (48%); the paper says about half did not perform the behaviour consistently enough to reach habit status; automaticity was self-reported by volunteers who chose and wanted their behaviour; there is no validated "habit achieved" cut-off on the SRHI; values beyond day 84 are extrapolations for slow formers. Label the widget "what a good fit looked like in one student sample", not a forecast.

### Bet candidates (05) — each answer carries its source and safe wording
- BET-05-1 "Median days for volunteers to reach 95% of their own automaticity plateau?" — Answer: 66 (quartiles 39–102; range 18–254) among the 39 of 82 analysable volunteers whose curve fitted well (Lally 2010). Safe wording: "weeks to months, very variable — not 21 and not exactly 66". [V] grade B.
- BET-05-2 "How much does writing an if-then plan raise goal attainment?" — Answer: d = .65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, originators); .27–.66 across 642 tests (2024); in one field experiment a date-and-time plan raised flu-shot uptake by 4.2 points from 33.1% (Milkman 2011). Safe wording: "a small-to-medium boost, bigger for starting than for stopping". [V] grade B.
- BET-05-3 "What did two large preregistered multi-lab tests find for the ego-depletion effect?" — Answer: d = 0.04 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.15; 23 labs, N = 2,141) and d = 0.06 (36 labs, N = 3,531), against d = 0.62 in the 2010 meta-analysis. [V] grade D for the strong form.
- BET-05-4 "When offered a refundable-deposit contract to quit smoking, what share accept?" — Answer: 13.7%, versus 90.0% for a reward-only programme (Halpern 2015, N = 2,538); among those who would accept either, the deposit programme did better by 13.2 points (3.1–22.8). [V] grade B.
- BET-05-5 "Of 54 four-week gym-nudge programmes, how many left a measurable effect after the programme ended?" — Answer: 8% (the abstract's figure; about 4 of 54, DERIVED) versus 45% that raised visits during the programme (Milkman 2021, N = 61,293). [V] grade B.
- BET-05-6 "After you miss a single day, how much automaticity do you lose?" — Answer: on average 0.29 points on a 0–42 scale the next day, with no longer-term cost found in 140 misses by 55 people (Lally 2010). Safe wording: "one miss is a blip; a week-long gap is untested here". [V] grade C.
- BET-05-7 "How long did it take gym members to form a habit of going (objective data)?" — Answer: about 68–78 days (2–3 months) after the authors' 2023 correction; the originally printed 122–226 d (4–7 months) was replaced; hospital handwashing took about 2 weeks (Buyalskaya 2023). [V] grade B (method-dependent).
- BET-05-8 "How many of Brian Wansink's papers does PubMed flag as retracted?" — Answer: 12 as of October 2026 (including the 2005 JAMA letter on serving-bowl size, retracted 23 October 2018), after Cornell's 2018 finding of academic misconduct. [V] grade D for the findings.
