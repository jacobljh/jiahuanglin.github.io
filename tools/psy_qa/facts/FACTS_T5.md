## 17 · disorders
### 17-A. What counts as a disorder (definition, history, reliability, prevalence, treatment gap)
- [def] ★ WHO/ICD-11 working definition: a mental disorder is a clinically significant disturbance in a person's cognition, emotional regulation or behaviour, usually tied to distress or impairment in important areas of functioning; "mental health conditions" is the broader umbrella (adds psychosocial disability and other states with distress, impairment or risk of self-harm). 精神障碍 = 认知、情绪调节或行为上有临床意义的紊乱，通常伴随痛苦或功能受损。 — WHO fact sheet "Mental disorders" (page dated 11 Sep 2026, opened 2026-10-14), https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-disorders
- [V] ICD-11: adopted by the 72nd World Health Assembly in May 2019 (WHA72.15), in effect from 1 January 2022; as of May 2024, 132 Member States/areas were implementing it. — WHO, https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/classification-of-diseases
- [V] DSM editions (years only): DSM-I 1952 (first manual "for clinical use", variant of ICD-6); DSM-III work began 1974, published 1980 (explicit criteria + multiaxial system); DSM-III-R 1987; DSM-IV 1994 (six-year effort, >1,000 people); DSM-5 2013 (work began 2000, task force 2007, 13 work groups); DSM-5-TR March 2022 (text/references/criteria updates, >200 experts, no conceptual overhaul). — APA, https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/dsm/about-dsm/history-of-the-dsm (DSM-II 1968 and DSM-IV-TR 2000 are not on that page: [~] standard dates)
- [~] Counts of "diagnoses per edition" are NOT safe: secondary summaries give DSM-I 106, DSM-II 182, DSM-III 265, DSM-III-R 292 and DSM-IV either 297 or 410 depending on whether subtypes are counted; I found no primary count (Blashfield et al. 2014 *Annu Rev Clin Psychol* 10:25–51 has them but is paywalled). Safe wording: "the manual grew from roughly a hundred listed conditions (1952) to several hundred (1994), depending on how you count". — Wikipedia pointer only; abstract of Blashfield: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-032813-153639
- [V] ★ Why DSM-III (1980) mattered: Mayes & Horwitz 2005 argue the shift from cause-based, continuous-with-normal "reactions" to discrete symptom-defined disorders was driven by professional politics, government research funding, insurers' demand for proof of treatment, and drug companies' need for specific indications — not by new scientific findings. — *J Hist Behav Sci* 41(3):249–267, https://doi.org/10.1002/jhbs.20103 (abstract opened; a historians' argument, not a graded finding)
- [V] ★ Homosexuality and the DSM: the APA Board of Trustees voted in December 1973 to remove it; a membership referendum then upheld the removal by a 58% majority of ~10,000 voting members; "sexual orientation disturbance" (DSM-II, 7th printing, 1974) → "ego-dystonic homosexuality" (DSM-III, 1980) → removed in DSM-III-R (1987). Drescher: earlier evidence (Kinsey; Hooker 1957, no more psychological disturbance in gay men) had been mostly ignored by psychiatry, and gay activism was "the most significant catalyst" — so data and politics both mattered. — Drescher 2015 *Behav Sci* 5(4):565–575, PMC4695779 (full text opened via Europe PMC), https://doi.org/10.3390/bs5040565
- [~] The exact referendum tally (widely quoted as 5,854 for, 3,810 against, 367 abstaining) and the referendum year (1974) are NOT in Drescher 2015 as I read it; the DSM-II 7th-printing change dated 1974 is stated by secondary sources. Use "58% of ~10,000 voting members" only. — see COULD NOT VERIFY
- [~] WHO removed homosexuality from the ICD (ICD-10), endorsed by the 43rd World Health Assembly on 17 May 1990 — date as given by Wikipedia citing a WHO page; the WHO page itself was not opened.
- [def] Wakefield's "harmful dysfunction" analysis (1992, *Am Psychol* 47:373–388): a condition is a disorder only if (a) an internal mechanism fails to perform the function it was naturally selected for (the factual part) AND (b) this is negatively valued/harmful (the value part). Wakefield 2007 applies it to the validity of symptom-based criteria and to cross-cultural use (US vs Taiwan DSM criteria). 有害功能失调。 — 1992 title confirmed in OpenAlex (https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.47.3.373; abstract not retrievable); 2007 *World Psychiatry* 6(3):149–156 abstract opened (Europe PMC)
- [V] Stein et al. 2010 (*Psychol Med*): the DSM-III/IV definition of mental disorder was reviewed in the run-up to DSM-V; authors accept that no definition draws a perfect boundary but propose a more scientifically valid, clinically useful one. — https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291709992261
- [V] RDoC (NIMH Research Domain Criteria): launched 2009; a research framework, "not meant to serve as a diagnostic guide" and not a replacement for DSM/ICD; treats psychopathology as deviation from normal functions across diagnostic boundaries, with eight units of analysis (genes, molecules, cells, circuits, physiology, behaviors, self-reports, paradigms). Six domains: Negative Valence Systems, Positive Valence Systems, Cognitive Systems, Social Processes, Arousal and Regulatory Systems, Sensorimotor Systems (25 top-level constructs on the NIMH page as fetched). Founding paper: Insel et al. 2010 *Am J Psychiatry* 167:748–751, https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.09091379 (abstract not retrievable) — NIMH, https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/about-rdoc and /constructs
- STUDY DSM-5 field-trial reliability (Regier et al. 2013) — original: 11 North American academic centres; consecutive patients randomly assigned to two blinded clinicians using usual clinical interviews; precise test-retest kappas for 23 diagnoses (15 adult, 8 child/adolescent): 5 "very good" (0.60–0.79), 9 "good" (0.40–0.59), 6 "questionable" (0.20–0.39), 3 "unacceptable" (<0.20); 8 more diagnoses lacked sample size. Per-diagnosis values: major depressive disorder κ ≈ 0.28 and major neurocognitive disorder κ ≈ 0.78 (secondary, Wikipedia-cited) [~]; the accompanying editorial (Freedman et al. 2013) singles out good reliability for borderline personality disorder and ties MDD's questionable reliability to its heterogeneity. Later evidence: no independent re-run of the same design; later work (Chmielewski et al. 2015, not opened) is said to show kappas depend strongly on interview method — not verified here.
  GRADE B: the direction (everyday-clinic agreement on common diagnoses such as depression and anxiety is modest, far below the reliability of a blood-pressure reading) is robust, but absolute kappas depend on method (unstructured clinic interviews vs structured interviews) and on base rates. — Regier et al. 2013 *Am J Psychiatry* 170(1):59–70, https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.12070999 (abstract via Europe PMC); Freedman et al. 2013 https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.12091189
- STUDY Rosenhan "On being sane in insane places" (1973) — original (numbers as summarised by Wikipedia; the Science paper's text was not retrievable, so treat as [~]): 8 "pseudopatients" (Rosenhan + 7), 12 hospitals in 5 US states; 7 admitted with a schizophrenia diagnosis at public hospitals and 1 with manic-depressive psychosis at a private hospital; stays 7–52 days (mean 19); all but one discharged with schizophrenia "in remission"; in the first three hospitals 35 of 118 real patients voiced suspicion; second part (hospital told to expect impostors; 193 patients rated) — Rosenhan actually sent none, yet staff flagged many of the ordinary patients (Wikipedia: 41 "judged likely impostors", 42 "suspect"; I could not confirm the paper's own breakdown). The paper concluded that the setting shapes how behaviour is interpreted and that hospital conditions can be harmful. Later evidence: Cahalan 2019 (*The Great Pretender*, built on Rosenhan's papers) reported inconsistent data, inaccurate or invented quotations from hospital records, and that only 2 of the 8 pseudopatients could be identified (Rosenhan and one graduate student whose account conflicts with the paper) — per Wikipedia's summary of the book; Scull 2023 (*Hist Psychiatry* 34(2):180–195) concludes from Cahalan's records that it was a "spectacularly successful case of scientific fraud" (abstract opened); Spitzer 1975 (*J Abnorm Psychol* 84:442–452, "On pseudoscience in science, logic in remission, and psychiatric diagnosis") was an early logic-and-method critique (title/record only). The paper did help push the APA toward explicit criteria (DSM-III), per Scull's abstract.
  GRADE D: the headline claim ("sane people could not be told from insane in hospitals") rests on data that cannot be verified and is alleged to be partly fabricated; teach it as a cautionary tale about a famous study, not as evidence about diagnosis. — Scull 2023 https://doi.org/10.1177/0957154X221150878 (abstract opened); Rosenhan 1973 *Science* 179:250–258 https://doi.org/10.1126/science.179.4070.250 (abstract opened; numbers not in it)
#### 17-A2. How common? (year-stamped, definition-stamped — never quote one number without its denominator)
- [V] ★ Kessler et al. 2005 (NCS-R, US, 2001–03, N = 9,282 English-speaking adults, structured interview, DSM-IV): LIFETIME prevalence of any DSM-IV disorder 46.4%; anxiety disorders the commonest class (28.8%); "half of all lifetime cases start by age 14". — *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 62(6):593–602, https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.62.6.593 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Kessler et al. 2005b (same survey): 12-MONTH prevalence of any disorder 26.2%; of those cases 22.3% serious, 37.3% moderate, 40.4% mild. — *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 62(6):617–627, https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.62.6.617
- [V] ★ Steel et al. 2014: 174 surveys from 63 countries (1980–2013): pooled 12-month prevalence of common mental disorders 17.6% (95% CI 16.3–18.9), lifetime 29.2% (95% CI 25.9–32.6); heterogeneity across surveys was substantial; North and Southeast Asia lower, English-speaking countries highest lifetime estimates; women higher for mood/anxiety, men for substance use. — *Int J Epidemiol* 43(2):476–493, https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyu038
- [V] ★ McGrath et al. 2023 (WHO World Mental Health surveys, 32 surveys in 29 countries, N = 156,331 adults, 13 DSM-IV disorders): lifetime PREVALENCE (so far) 28.6% of males, 29.8% of females; projected cumulative risk by age 75 = 46.4% of males, 53.1% of females (on this projection roughly half of people will meet criteria for at least one of these disorders by old age); median age of onset 19 (males, IQR 14–32) / 20 (females, IQR 12–36); conditional probability of first onset peaks around age 15. — *Lancet Psychiatry* 10(9):668–681, https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(23)00193-1
- [~] ★ Reconciling "46%" with "29%" (my synthesis, labelled as such): the numbers answer different questions. Steel 2014 (29.2%) and McGrath 2023 (28.6% / 29.8%) are lifetime prevalence among adults at the time of interview, pooled across many countries; McGrath's PROJECTED risk by age 75 is 46–53%, i.e. the same size as the US NCS-R lifetime figure of 46.4%. Plausible further reasons for the gap — the US is among the highest-prevalence countries (Steel: English-speaking countries highest), and "any disorder" counts depend on which disorders the interview covers (McGrath 2023: 13 DSM-IV disorders; the NCS-R class breakdown I could not retrieve — Harvard NCS-R table returned 503). Safe teaching sentence: "about three in ten adults have met criteria by the time they are surveyed; roughly half will by old age".
- [V] Caspi et al. 2020 (Dunedin birth cohort, 1,037 enrolled, 1,013 with mental-health data, assessed repeatedly to age 45): 86% (869/1,013) met criteria for a DSM disorder at some assessment by midlife; 85% of those (737/869) had accumulated comorbid diagnoses; disorders began by adolescence in 59% (600/1,013). Caveat: repeated prospective assessment finds more cases than one-off retrospective surveys. — *JAMA Netw Open* 3(4):e203221, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2020.3221
- [V] US snapshot: NSDUH 2022 — any mental illness among US adults 23.1% (59.3 million; women 26.4%, men 19.7%; 18–25 y 36.2%), serious mental illness 6.0% (15.4 million); 50.6% of adults with any mental illness had treatment in the past year (NIMH page notes 2022 data are not strictly comparable with earlier years; 52% of sampled adults did not complete the interview). — NIMH, https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/statistics/mental-illness
- [V] ★ ADDED — CHINA: China Mental Health Survey (Huang et al. 2019; nationally representative, 157 surveillance points in 31 provinces, face-to-face CIDI/SCID interviews by trained interviewers and psychiatrists, 22 Jul 2013–5 Mar 2015, N = 32,552 adults): weighted prevalence of any disorder EXCLUDING dementia 9.3% over the past 12 months (95% CI 5.4–13.3) and 16.6% over the lifetime (13.0–20.2); anxiety disorders the commonest class (12-month 5.0%, lifetime 7.6%); dementia 5.6% in people aged ≥65. The authors report 2013 prevalence of most disorders exceeded earlier national surveys (1982, 1993, 2002) but was lower than a 2009 estimate. Context: lifetime 16.6% in China vs 29% (pooled global) vs 46% (US NCS-R) — differences in method, disorders covered, and possibly under-reporting/stigma; the abstract does not decide among these. — *Lancet Psychiatry* 6(3):211–224, https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30511-X (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ GLOBAL (latest WHO): nearly 1 in 7 people (1.2 billion) were living with a mental disorder in 2023 (GBD 2023, IHME 2024): anxiety disorders 470 M (146 M children/adolescents), depressive disorders 322 M, bipolar 36 M, schizophrenia ~27 M (1 in 300 people; people with it die ~9 years earlier on average), eating disorders 18 M. — WHO fact sheet "Mental disorders", page dated 11 Sep 2026, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-disorders
- [V] Superseded-but-still-quoted figure: "1 in 8 people / 970 million (2019)" is the GBD 2019/WHO 2022 number; WHO's Sept 2025 release says "more than 1 billion"; the Sept 2026 fact sheet says 1.2 billion for 2023. The rise is mostly population growth and updated estimates, not proof of an epidemic: GBD 2019 reports age-standardised mental-disorder DALY rates essentially flat 1990–2019 (1,581.2 → 1,566.2 per 100,000) while total DALYs rose 80.8 M → 125.3 M (4.9% of all DALYs) with population growth/ageing. — GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators 2022 *Lancet Psychiatry* 9(2):137–150, https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(21)00395-3; WHO news 2 Sep 2025, https://www.who.int/news/item/02-09-2025-over-a-billion-people-living-with-mental-health-conditions-services-require-urgent-scale-up
- STUDY General psychopathology factor "p" (Caspi et al. 2014) — original: Dunedin cohort followed from adolescence to midlife (N not in the abstract); a single general dimension of psychopathology fit the diagnoses better than separate internalizing / externalizing / thought-disorder factors alone, and higher p went with more life impairment, more family history, worse developmental history, poorer early-life brain function. Later evidence: Lahey et al. 2012 (independent team; nationally representative US adult sample; 11 DSM-IV disorders) — a bifactor model with a general factor fit modestly but significantly better than three correlated factors (externalizing, distress, fears), every disorder loaded strongly on the general factor, and it predicted later psychopathology and functioning beyond the specific factors (*J Abnorm Psychol* 121(4):971–977; record + abstract opened via OpenAlex). Whether p is a substantive cause or a statistical consequence of overlapping diagnoses is open (the authors themselves call for tests of shared etiology/neurobiology); HiTOP (Kotov et al. 2017) organises the same covariation into spectra rather than one factor.
  GRADE B: the observation that diagnoses overlap heavily and cluster into broad dimensions is robust; "one general cause" is not established. — Caspi et al. 2014 *Clin Psychol Sci* 2(2):119–137, https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702613497473 (abstract opened); Kotov et al. 2017 *J Abnorm Psychol* 126(4):454–477, https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000258 (abstract opened)
- [V] HiTOP (Kotov et al. 2017): traditional taxonomies have weak reliability/validity — arbitrary boundaries, frequent co-occurrence, heterogeneity within diagnoses, instability over time; HiTOP groups symptoms into syndromes/components, spectra and dimensions based on how they covary; authors say several domains are ready for clinical use, work ongoing. — same DOI as above
- [V] ★ Symptom heterogeneity: Fried & Nesse 2015 — in 3,703 outpatients with MDD (STAR*D level 1) there were 1,030 unique symptom profiles; 501 (48.6%) profiles were endorsed by one patient only, 864 (83.9%) by five or fewer; the commonest profile covered 1.8% of patients; controlling for severity did not remove the heterogeneity. So "depression" is not one tidy syndrome. — *J Affect Disord* 172:96–102, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2014.10.010 (abstract via Europe PMC)
#### 17-A3. The treatment gap (sourced, year-stamped)
- [V] ★ Demyttenaere et al. 2004 (WHO World Mental Health surveys, 14 countries — 6 less developed, 8 developed — 60,463 community adults): 12-month prevalence of any disorder from 4.3% (Shanghai) to 26.4% (USA); among SERIOUS cases, 35.5–50.3% (developed countries) and 76.3–85.4% (less-developed countries) received no treatment in the previous 12 months. — *JAMA* 291(21):2581–2590, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.291.21.2581 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Thornicroft et al. 2017 (WMH surveys, 21 countries; 4,331 respondents with 12-month DSM-IV major depressive disorder out of 51,547): only 16.5% of people with MDD received minimally adequate treatment — about 1 in 5 (22.4%) in high-income countries versus about 1 in 27 (3.7%) in low/lower-middle-income countries; among those who received any treatment, 41.0% got minimally adequate treatment. (The 71.1%/77.9%/52.6% "any visit" figures apply only to people who recognised a need for treatment.) — *Br J Psychiatry* 210(2):119–124, https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.116.188078 (Cambridge page opened)
- [V] Moitra et al. 2022 (149 studies, Bayesian meta-regression, 84 countries, 2000–2019): treatment coverage for MDD 51% (95% UI 20–82) in high-income vs 20% (1–53) in low/lower-middle-income locations; mental-health service use 33% (8–66) vs 8% (<1–36); minimally adequate treatment 23% (2–55) vs 3% (<1–25); data are sparse and the intervals are wide. — *PLoS Med* 19(2):e1003901, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003901 (record + abstract opened via OpenAlex)
- [V] WHO 2026 fact sheet: only ~40% of people with psychosis and about one third of people with depression receive formal mental-health care (sources: Mental Health Atlas 2024 and the 2022 PLoS Med study above); WHO Sept 2025: in low-income countries fewer than 10% of people with psychosis receive care vs >50% in higher-income countries; median government spending on mental health 2% of health budgets (unchanged since 2017); global median 13 mental-health workers per 100,000 people; depression + anxiety cost the global economy about US$1 trillion a year. — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-disorders ; https://www.who.int/news/item/02-09-2025-over-a-billion-people-living-with-mental-health-conditions-services-require-urgent-scale-up
- [V] PTSD treatment gap: in the WMH surveys 53.5% of people with PTSD in high-income countries were in treatment in the prior year vs 22.8% in low/lower-middle-income and 28.7% in upper-middle-income countries (Koenen et al. 2017, below).

### 17-B. Mechanism: loops, attractors, networks (what is established vs what is a framework)
- [~] ★ EVIDENCE STATE OF THE LESSON'S ENGINE ("a prediction locked in by a self-maintaining loop") — my synthesis, to keep writers honest: (1) best supported as a MECHANISM STORY for fear/avoidance (extinction is new learning that avoidance prevents — Bouton) and for OCD checking (healthy-volunteer experiments show checking itself lowers memory confidence), (2) reasonably supported as a DESCRIPTION for depression (rumination, withdrawal, negative appraisals co-vary with and predict episodes, but causal direction runs both ways), (3) a live, unsettled hypothesis for psychosis (predictive-processing accounts: reviews say some studies find too-weak and others too-strong priors), (4) the formal "attractor/network" models are theory plus small tests, with disputed replicability. Say "frame", not "finding".
#### 17-B1. Frameworks and formal models
- [V] Engel 1977 (*Science* 196(4286):129–136): argued that the dominant biomedical model has no conceptual place for the social, psychological and behavioural factors in illness and proposed the biopsychosocial model as a framework for research, teaching and care. 生物-心理-社会模型。 — https://doi.org/10.1126/science.847460 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [def] Diathesis–stress: a vulnerability (diathesis) matters mainly when a stressor arrives; the formal treatment for depression is Monroe & Simons 1991 (*Psychol Bull* 110:406–425; citation only, not opened). 素质—应激。
- [V] Borsboom & Cramer 2013 (*Annu Rev Clin Psychol* 9:91–121): in the network approach, disorders arise from causal interactions among symptoms, possibly with feedback loops; the review covers methods for estimating symptom networks (e.g. which symptom is most central) and simulation models of symptom dynamics, and argues that treating symptoms as indicators of one latent disease has had limited success. 症状网络。 — https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-050212-185608
- [V] Cramer et al. 2016 (*PLoS ONE* 11:e0167490) — a THEORY/simulation, not a test: symptoms (e.g. insomnia, fatigue) activate each other with logistic probability; people with strongly connected symptom networks are more vulnerable and, under external stress, settle into a "depressed" state while weakly connected networks return to a non-depressed state; the model is offered as a first symptom-based, intra-individual process model that can also reproduce spontaneous recovery. — https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0167490 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- STUDY Replicability of psychopathology symptom networks (Forbes et al. 2017 vs Borsboom et al. 2017) — original: Forbes, Wright, Markon & Krueger 2017 estimated MDD/GAD symptom networks in two epidemiological samples (NCS-R n = 9,282; Australian NSMHW n = 8,841): simple association networks were stable, but the three conditional-independence network types replicated poorly — 44% of symptoms came out as "most influential" on at least one centrality index across six networks, and only 13–21% of edges were consistently estimated; they blame measurement error in single symptoms. Reply: Borsboom, Fried, Epskamp et al. 2017 reanalysed the same data and found the networks replicated well across samples and split-halves, attributing the discrepancy to incomplete implementation of the models, ignoring the interview skip structure, and expecting identical structures across samples/methods. Later evidence: the exchange continued in the same journal; I did not open a definitive adjudication (as of Oct 2026 no resolution found in sources I could open).
  GRADE C: networks are a useful descriptive tool, but claims about which symptom is "central" or "drives" the network are not reliably replicated at typical sample sizes, and the dispute is itself unresolved. — Forbes et al. 2017 *J Abnorm Psychol* 126(7):969–988, https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000276 ; Borsboom et al. 2017 126(7):989–999, https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000306 (both abstracts opened via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Critical slowing down before depressive transitions (van de Leemput et al. 2014) — original: using self-recorded emotion time series from a large group of healthy individuals and patients, rising temporal autocorrelation, variance and between-emotion correlation went with a higher probability of an upcoming shift into or out of a depressed state, which the authors read as early-warning signals of a tipping point between alternative stable mood states (N, sampling rate and effect sizes are NOT in the abstract). Later evidence (as of Oct 2026, all small-sample, mixed): Wichers, Smit & Snippe 2020 (six single-subject series, 3×/day for 95–183 days; only ONE person showed a sudden symptom transition; a month earlier autocorrelation r = .51, variance r = .53 and network connectivity r = .42 in "feeling down" had risen; *J Pers Oriented Res* 6(1):1–15); Schreuder et al. 2020 (adolescent general-population cohort, 10×/day for 6 days, subsamples of 166–192 by symptom domain, 1-year follow-up): early-warning signals in "feeling suspicious" predicted increases in interpersonal sensitivity, but none predicted increases in depression, anxiety or somatic complaints (*BMC Med* 18:269); Curtiss et al. 2023 (31 MDD patients, 8 weeks of daily surveys): rising autocorrelation went with later worsening (r = .41, p = .02) but variance (r = −.23, p = .23) and network connectivity (r = −.12) did not (*Psychol Med* 53:3124); Schreuder et al. 2022 (122 at-risk young adults, 6 months of daily ratings): accuracy of predicting the DIRECTION of a transition was 27.9% vs 20.6% chance (p = .09, not reliably above chance in the main analysis; *BMC Psychiatry* 22:49); Tonge et al. 2024 (4 people in remission, 1 relapsed): a warning in the relapsing person but false positives in the others, and the two analysis methods disagreed (*J Behav Ther Exp Psychiatry*); Ludwig et al. 2025 (29 bipolar patients, 10,587 patient days, 30 depressive + 20 (hypo)manic episodes): several measures predicted pre-episode weeks, no single reliable predictor, and none reached the pre-set clinical-utility thresholds (*J Affect Disord* 391); Cui et al. 2025 (narrative review + simulations): later findings were "not consistent across variables or cases" and of limited predictive power, common study methods often do not match the theory's assumptions, so more rigorous evidence is needed (*BMC Psychiatry* 25:261); Helmich et al. 2021 (*Curr Opin Psychol* 41:51–58) give design recommendations. All abstracts opened via Europe PMC.
  GRADE C: an elegant, testable prediction of the "attractor" picture, but support is mixed and mostly from small or single-case time series. — van de Leemput et al. 2014 *PNAS* 111(1):87–92, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1312114110 (abstract via Europe PMC)
#### 17-B2. Depression: cognition, rumination, withdrawal, helplessness
- [V] ★ Beck & Bredemeier 2016 ("A unified model of depression", *Clin Psychol Sci* 4:596–619): depression viewed as an adaptation that conserves energy after the perceived loss of an investment in a vital resource (relationship, group identity, personal asset); tendencies to process information negatively and to react strongly to stress (genes, trauma, or both) give rise to depressogenic beliefs about the self, the world and the future (Beck's "cognitive triad" idea; the 1967/1976 books were not opened); these amplify appraisals of loss, and the appraisals plus biological reactions (autonomic, immune, neurochemical) produce anhedonia and anergia — an "anachronistic" evolutionary program. A model/framework, not a test. 认知模型。 — https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702616628523 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- STUDY Cognitive vulnerability to depression (do negative cognitions come first?) — original: Lewinsohn, Steinmetz, Larson & Franklin 1981 (community sample N = 998 followed 1 year; 63 depressed at baseline, 85 became depressed, 115 previously depressed): depression-related cognitions arose WITH the episode — people who later became depressed did not differ from controls beforehand, and cognitions did not look like permanent residue — but more negative cognitions predicted LESS improvement. Later evidence: Lewinsohn, Joiner & Rohde 2001 (N = 1,507 adolescents, ~1-year follow-up): Beck's diathesis–stress prediction was supported only "at the level of a trend", the hopelessness theory not at all, with a threshold pattern (dysfunctional attitudes related to onset after negative events only when they exceeded a certain level); Alloy et al. 2006 (347 college freshmen with no disorder at start, classified high vs low cognitive risk, followed 2.5 years): high-risk students had 3.5–6.8 times the odds of major, minor and hopelessness depression, predicting first onsets and recurrences alike; the groups did not differ on anxiety disorders that were not comorbid with depression.

- STUDY Rumination prolongs and predicts depression — original: Nolen-Hoeksema 1991 (response-styles theory; lab and field studies): people who ruminate on symptoms, causes and consequences have longer depressions than people who distract or act; rumination biases mood-congruent thinking and interferes with problem solving and goal-directed behaviour; used to explain why women are more often depressed. Later evidence: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky 2008 review — well supported: rumination intensifies depression, increases negative thinking, impairs problem solving, interferes with instrumental behaviour, erodes social support; less consistent: rumination predicts ONSET more reliably than DURATION (duration mainly in combination with negative cognitive styles), and positive distraction lifts mood in experiments but correlational studies do not consistently link it to fewer symptoms; Watkins 2008 (*Psychol Bull*): repetitive thought is not inherently bad — its effects depend on valence, context and level of construal (abstract vs concrete), and it can aid recovery, planning and coping; Aldao, Nolen-Hoeksema & Schweizer 2010 meta-analysis of trait-level strategies (114 studies, 241 effect sizes): rumination had the LARGEST association with anxiety, depression, eating and substance symptoms, avoidance/problem solving/suppression medium–large, reappraisal and acceptance only small–medium (mostly cross-sectional).
  GRADE B: the rumination–depression link is robust across labs and samples, but causal strength, the onset-vs-duration pattern, and the abstract-vs-concrete boundary are disputed. — Nolen-Hoeksema 1991 *J Abnorm Psychol* 100(4):569–582, https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.100.4.569 ; Nolen-Hoeksema et al. 2008 *Perspect Psychol Sci* 3(5):400–424, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x ; Watkins 2008 *Psychol Bull* 134(2):163–206, https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163 ; Aldao et al. 2010 *Clin Psychol Rev* 30(2):217–237, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2009.11.004 (abstracts opened via OpenAlex / Europe PMC)
- [V] Behavioural account of depression: Ferster 1973 ("A functional analysis of depression", *Am Psychol* 28:857–870) proposed analysing depression as reduced frequency of countable behaviours and applying basic behavioural principles to find the conditions that raise or lower them; presented as a complement to clinical knowledge. - STUDY Learned helplessness: "helplessness is learned" vs "passivity is the default" — original: Seligman & Maier 1967 (dogs; uncontrollable shock then failure to escape) and the 1970s human analogues (Hiroto & Seligman 1975, not opened): organisms learn that outcomes are independent of their actions and stop trying. Later evidence: Maier & Seligman 2016 ("Learned helplessness at fifty: insights from neuroscience", *Psychol Rev* 123:349–367) argue the original theory got the mechanism backward: passivity is the default response to prolonged aversive events, driven by serotonergic activity in the dorsal raphe nucleus, while what is learned is CONTROL — detecting controllability engages the medial prefrontal cortex, which inhibits the dorsal raphe and overcomes passivity; the authors speculate this could inform depression treatment.
  GRADE C: the behavioural phenomenon (passivity after uncontrollable stress) is robust in animals, but the textbook mechanism was substantially revised by its own originators, and transfer to human depression is an analogy rather than a tested mechanism. — https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000033 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- STUDY Depressive realism ("depressed people see reality more accurately", Alloy & Abramson 1979) — original: classic lab finding that depressed students judged their control over outcomes more accurately than non-depressed students (original not opened). Later evidence: Moore & Fresco 2012 meta-analysis (75 studies, 7,305 participants): the overall effect is tiny (d = −0.07); dysphoric/depressed people showed a bias effect of d = .14 vs a larger positive bias in non-depressed (d = .29); the effect was larger in studies without an objective standard of reality (d = −.15 vs −.03) and in studies that measured depression by self-report (d = .16 vs −.04 with structured interviews).
  GRADE C for "a small, method-dependent effect exists"; GRADE D for the strong textbook version ("depression makes you sadder but wiser"). — Moore & Fresco 2012 *Clin Psychol Rev* 32(6):496–509, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2012.05.004 (abstract via Europe PMC)

#### 17-B3. Genes, stress and childhood adversity (what the large studies say)
- [V] ★ Heritability of major depression ≈ 37%: Sullivan, Neale & Kendler 2000 (meta-analysis of family, adoption, twin studies): relatives of probands had OR 2.84 (95% CI 2.31–3.49) across five family studies; heritability of liability 37% (95% CI 31–42%); shared (sibling-common) environment ~0% (0–5%); individual-specific environment + measurement error 63% (58–67%) — i.e. neither genes nor environment alone explain depression. — *Am J Psychiatry* 157(10):1552–1562, https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.157.10.1552 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] ★ Stressful life events do cause depression onset, but not all of the association is causal: Kendler, Karkowski & Prescott 1999 (population-based female twins, 24,648 person-months, 316 onsets): odds of onset in the month of a stressful event 5.64 in all subjects, 4.52 within dizygotic pairs, 3.58 within monozygotic pairs; about one third of the association is non-causal because predisposed people place themselves in higher-risk environments. — *Am J Psychiatry* 156(6):837–841, https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.156.6.837
- [V] Polygenicity: a 2019 GWAS meta-analysis pooled 807,553 people (246,363 cases, 561,190 controls) and found 102 independent variants, 269 genes and 15 gene sets (87 of the 102 variants replicated in 1,306,354 people); a 2025 trans-ancestry GWAS reports 697 associations — many tiny effects, no single "depression gene". — Howard et al. 2019 *Nat Neurosci* https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-018-0326-7 ; Adams et al. 2025 (title/first author confirmed via OpenAlex; abstract numbers not opened), DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2024.12.002
- STUDY 5-HTTLPR × stress → depression (Caspi 2003) — original: Caspi et al. 2003, *Science* 301:386–389, a prospective-longitudinal birth cohort (Dunedin): carriers of one or two short alleles of the serotonin-transporter promoter polymorphism showed more depressive symptoms, diagnosable depression and suicidality after stressful life events than long/long people (N is not in the abstract). Later evidence: Risch et al. 2009 (*JAMA* 301:2462–2471; 14 of 26 studies, 14,250 participants incl. 1,769 with depression): published data pooled to OR 1.41 (1.25–1.57) but with individual-level data the interaction vanished (weighted OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.98–1.13); Culverhouse et al. 2018 (*Mol Psychiatry*; 31 datasets, 38,802 participants of European ancestry, shared analysis script): no subgroup showed a significant stress × genotype interaction — stress was a strong risk factor, genotype had no meaningful effect; Border et al. 2019 (*Am J Psychiatry* 176:376–387; 18 historically studied candidate genes, preregistered analyses, N from 62,138 to 443,264): no clear evidence of gene main effects or gene-by-environment effects, candidate genes no more associated with depression than random genes, and many reported associations likely false positives.

- STUDY Serotonin-deficit ("chemical imbalance") theory of depression — original: the monoamine/serotonin hypothesis proposed ~1960s–70s (Jauhar et al.: Coppen's proposal >50 years ago). Response: Jauhar, Cowen & Browning 2023 ("Fifty years on: serotonin and depression") present a narrative review of the case FOR a serotonin role from psychopharmacology, molecular imaging and systems neuroscience, arguing the umbrella review does not settle the question (abstract only opened).
  GRADE D for the simple slogan "depression = low serotonin / chemical imbalance" (the main lines of direct evidence do not support it); GRADE C for the weaker claim that serotonin systems are involved in mood regulation and in how some treatments work (reviews disagree). Do NOT write that antidepressants "don't work" from this: that is a separate question (lesson 18). — Moncrieff et al. 2022 *Mol Psychiatry* 28(8):3243–3256, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-022-01661-0 (abstract via Europe PMC); Jauhar et al. 2023 *J Psychopharmacol* 37(3):237–241, https://doi.org/10.1177/02698811231161813
- STUDY "Biological explanation reduces stigma" (the medicalisation message) — original: the intuition that describing mental illness in biomedical/biogenetic terms (e.g. "chemical imbalance", "it's genetic") reduces blame and therefore stigma. Later evidence: Kvaale, Haslam & Gottdiener 2013 meta-analysis (28 experimental studies; four meta-analyses with N = 1,207–3,469): biogenetic explanations DID reduce blame (Hedges g = −0.324) but increased prognostic pessimism (g = 0.263) and slightly increased perceived dangerousness (g = 0.198, possibly inflated by publication bias); social distance was generally unaffected; the authors warn pessimism may become a self-fulfilling prophecy that hinders recovery.
  GRADE B: consistent in direction across 28 experiments, but effects are small and experimental (vignette-based), not field outcomes. — *Clin Psychol Rev* 33(6):782–794, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2013.06.002 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Adverse childhood experiences (ACE) and adult mental health — original: Felitti et al. 1998 (Kaiser HMO members; questionnaire to 13,494, 9,508 responded = 70.5%; seven categories: psychological/physical/sexual abuse, violence against mother, household substance abuse, mental illness, imprisonment): over half reported ≥1 category, about 1 in 4 reported ≥2; people with ≥4 categories had 4–12-fold higher risk of alcoholism, drug abuse, depression and suicide attempt than people with none (2–4-fold for smoking, poor self-rated health, etc.). #### 17-B4. Fear, avoidance and OCD (the cleanest "loop")
- [V] ★ Extinction is new learning, not erasure (Bouton 2002): when a response fades after a cue is repeatedly presented without its outcome, the original learning is not erased; the new learning sits alongside it, so the cue carries two competing meanings and which one wins depends on context (physical setting, internal state, elapsed time); the newer learning is especially context-dependent, which helps explain several forms of relapse after extinction-based treatment. 消退是新学习而非擦除。 — *Biol Psychiatry* 52(10):976–986, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3223(02)01546-9 (abstract via Europe PMC; the abstract does not name renewal/reinstatement/spontaneous recovery individually)
- [V] Milad & Quirk 2012 ("Fear extinction as a model for translational neuroscience: ten years of progress", *Annu Rev Psychol* 63:129–151): about a decade of mapping the neural circuits of fear-extinction in rodents and humans, with close rodent–human coordination, offered as a template for translational work. — https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.121208.131631 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] LeDoux & Pine 2016 ("Using neuroscience to help understand fear and anxiety: a two-system framework", *Am J Psychiatry* 173:1083–1093): threat responses fall into two classes with different circuits — bodily behaviour/physiological responses vs conscious feelings (self-reported fear, anxiety); conflating them has slowed progress in understanding anxiety and in developing treatments. — https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16030353 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- STUDY Safety behaviours maintain fear (Salkovskis 1991; Sloan & Telch 2002) — original: Salkovskis 1991 cognitive account ("The importance of behaviour in the maintenance of anxiety and panic"; abstract not retrievable); Sloan & Telch 2002: 46 people with pronounced fear of confinement were randomly assigned to one of three 30-minute exposure conditions — guided threat re-evaluation, encouraged safety behaviours, or exposure alone; the safety-behaviour group reported significantly more fear at post-treatment and at 2-week follow-up than the re-evaluation group, and safety-behaviour use hindered between-trial habituation. Later evidence: Meulders, Van Daele, Volders & Vlaeyen 2016 meta-analysis (409 articles screened, 23 in the systematic review, 20 coded for meta-analysis): exposure WITHOUT safety-seeking behaviours vs baseline SMD 0.31 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.66, p = .08), exposure WITH them SMD −0.13 (−0.37 to 0.11, p = .28) — neither significant; the authors say the evidence does not clearly support either removing or adding them and call for larger comparable replications; Blakey & Abramowitz 2016 (critical review): the evidence is limited, mixed and controversial, and inhibitory-learning theory predicts other effects that experiments have largely not tested.
  GRADE C: the logic is coherent and one lab experiment found harm, but the pooled experimental evidence is underpowered and inconclusive — "safety behaviours maintain anxiety" is a theory-driven clinical heuristic, not a replicated finding. — Sloan & Telch 2002 *Behav Res Ther* 40(3):235–251, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-7967(01)00007-9 ; Meulders et al. 2016 *Clin Psychol Rev* 45:144–156, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2016.02.002 ; Blakey & Abramowitz 2016 49:1–15, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2016.07.002 (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- [~] Intolerance of uncertainty: Gentes & Ruscio 2011's meta-analysis relates IU to symptoms of GAD, major depression and OCD, and with a GAD-specific IU definition the link is stronger for GAD than OCD (Semantic Scholar TL;DR only; abstract not retrievable). A transdiagnostic correlate; causal role not tested in what I opened. — *Clin Psychol Rev* 31:923–933, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2011.05.001
- [V] OCD prevalence (NCS-R subsample N = 2,073): lifetime 2.3%, 12-month 1.2%, although more than a quarter reported obsessions or compulsions at some point (sub-threshold experiences are common); greater severity went with poorer insight, more comorbidity, more role impairment and higher treatment seeking; comorbid with anxiety, mood, impulse-control and substance disorders. — Ruscio, Stein, Chiu & Kessler, *Mol Psychiatry* 15(1):53–63 (online 2008), https://doi.org/10.1038/mp.2008.94 (abstract via Semantic Scholar)
- STUDY Repeated checking lowers memory confidence (van den Hout & Kindt 2003) — original: three experiments with healthy volunteers (n = 39, 40, 40) on an interactive virtual gas stove; after repeated checking of the relevant stove (vs checking irrelevant light bulbs) actual memory accuracy did not change, but recollections became much less vivid and detailed and confidence dropped; the third experiment (no pre-test) reproduced this; the authors conclude checking can sustain memory distrust and is counterproductive as a way to reduce uncertainty. - STUDY Goal-directed vs habit imbalance in OCD / compulsivity — original: Gillan et al. 2011 (21 patients with OCD vs 30 healthy controls; instrumental training, outcome-devaluation test, "slips-of-action" test): both groups learned equally well, but patients had poorer knowledge of the outcomes and made more slips of action — weaker goal-directed control, more cue-triggered habits (first experimental evidence of the deficit in OCD, per the authors). #### 17-B5. Psychosis (a framework under test — do not teach it as settled)
- [V] ★ McGrath et al. 2008 (concise overview of 3 systematic reviews): median incidence of schizophrenia 15.2 per 100,000 persons per year (central 80% of estimates 7.7–43.0, about fivefold variation), male:female incidence ratio 1.4:1, median lifetime morbid risk 7.2 per 1,000 (0.72%), median all-cause standardized mortality ratio 2.6 (gap widening in recent decades); migrants had higher incidence and prevalence; urbanicity, economic status and latitude also related. — *Epidemiol Rev* 30(1):67–76, https://doi.org/10.1093/epirev/mxn001 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] Fletcher & Frith 2009 ("Perceiving is believing: a Bayesian approach to explaining the positive symptoms of schizophrenia", *Nat Rev Neurosci* 10:48–58): propose that abnormal error-dependent learning could underlie both hallucinations and delusions, and that both can be explained within a disturbed hierarchical Bayesian framework without separately modelling experience and belief. — https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2536 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] Kapur 2003 ("Psychosis as a state of aberrant salience", *Am J Psychiatry* 160:13–23): dopamine dysregulation makes events and thoughts feel abnormally important; delusions are the person's attempt to make sense of these experiences, hallucinations the direct experience of altered salience; antipsychotics damp the salience; if treatment stops, dormant ideas can regain salience and relapse can follow. — https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.160.1.13 (abstract via OpenAlex)

- STUDY Conditioned hallucinations / overweighting of perceptual priors (Powers, Mathys & Corlett 2017) — original: four groups differing in voice-hearing and treatment-seeking status (N per group not in the abstract) did a Pavlovian task in which a cue predicted a tone, inducing conditioned hallucinations (reporting a tone that was not played); voice-hearers, with or without a psychosis diagnosis, were significantly more susceptible; computational modelling plus fMRI pointed to overweighting of perceptual priors and a mediating brain circuit. Later evidence (as of Sept 2026): SUPPORTIVE — Corlett et al. 2019 review: independent labs find that overly strong priors induce hallucinations in healthy people and that people who hallucinate in daily life are more susceptible; Batkovskyte et al. 2026 (*Schizophr Bull* 52(3); two online experiments, n = 92 and n = 100 healthy adults): higher hallucination-proneness went with a bigger influence of INTERNALLY generated priors (when wrong) and weaker use of external priors in perceiving degraded speech; Martin et al. 2026 (*Sci Rep* 16:19557): threat-relevant images modulated conditioned hallucinations, while trait anxiety and hallucination proneness were NEGATIVELY related to conditioned hallucinations and to prior reliance. ### 18-B. Behavioural activation vs cognitive therapy — is "restructuring thoughts" the unique lever?
- [def] Behavioural activation (BA 行为激活): scheduling and doing activities that bring reward or mastery and counter withdrawal (Cuijpers 2007's "activity scheduling": patients track mood and activities and add enjoyable ones); cognitive therapy (CT 认知疗法) adds work on automatic thoughts and core beliefs (Jacobson 1996's arms). — sources in the next bullets
- [V] ★ The component analysis: Jacobson et al. 1996 randomised 150 outpatients with major depression to BA alone, BA + skills for modifying automatic thoughts (AT), or full CT (also targeting core schemas); four experienced therapists delivered all arms with close adherence, and adherence ratings showed a clear bias in favour of CT; the full treatment did NOT beat the component treatments at termination or at 6-month follow-up; BA and AT were about equally effective at changing negative thinking and attributional style; attributional style predicted outcome strongly in the BA arm but not in the CT arm. — *J Consult Clin Psychol* 64(2), https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.64.2.295 (abstract via OpenAlex; per-arm response rates not opened)
- [V] ★ Dimidjian et al. 2006: randomised, placebo-controlled, N = 241 adults with major depression; BA vs cognitive therapy vs antidepressant medication; among the more severely depressed, BA performed about as well as medication and both outperformed cognitive therapy (the abstract gives no per-arm rates; the medication's name is not in it). — *J Consult Clin Psychol* 74(4):658–670, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.74.4.658 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ COBRA (Richards et al. 2016): UK non-inferiority RCT in primary care and psychological-therapy services (Devon, Durham, Leeds), adults with DSM-IV major depression (excluded: bipolar disorder, psychosis, substance dependence, recent serious suicidality, cognitive impairment, current psychological therapy); 221 randomised to BA delivered by JUNIOR mental-health workers, 219 to CBT delivered by psychological therapists; primary outcome PHQ-9 at 12 months, non-inferiority margin 1.9 PHQ-9 points; BA was non-inferior — modified-ITT mean difference 0.1 (95% CI −1.3 to 1.5; p = 0.89), both groups' mean 8.4 (79% of BA and 86% of CBT participants assessable); per-protocol difference 0.0 (−1.5 to 1.6). The abstract text I opened is cut off before the cost results, so any "BA is cheaper" number is NOT verified. — *Lancet* 388(10047):871–880, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)31140-0 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Meta-analyses: Cuijpers, van Straten & Warmerdam 2007 — 16 studies, 780 participants; activity scheduling vs control g = 0.87 (0.60–1.15); vs other psychological treatments 0.13 (non-significant); vs cognitive therapy 0.02 (10 studies); follow-up differences from CT non-significant. Ekers et al. 2014 — 26 RCTs, 1,524 participants; BA vs control SMD −0.74 (−0.91 to −0.56; 25 trials, 1,088 participants); vs antidepressant medication −0.42 (−0.83 to −0.00; 4 trials, 283 participants); most studies low quality, short follow-up; authors found no evidence of publication bias. — *Clin Psychol Rev* 27(3):318–326, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2006.11.001 ; *PLoS One* 9(6):e100100, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100 (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- [V] 2026 component analysis in task-shared care (non-specialist providers): Papola et al. 2026 (Bayesian individual-participant-data component network meta-analysis; 34 RCTs of task-shared psychosocial interventions for adults with common mental disorders vs control conditions, 30 trials contributing IPD from 10,612 participants, 72.2% women, mean age 36.7; MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO and CENTRAL from inception to 15 March 2023 plus datasets to 29 May 2026). Incremental effect of adding a component to a treatment (scale and units NOT named in the abstract; negative = more symptom reduction): strengthening social support −9.48 (95% CrI −13.29 to −6.60), behavioural activation −4.15 (−7.48 to −0.05), problem management −4.08 (−5.37 to −2.83); relaxation +7.97 (3.35 to 12.11) and, "less clearly", cognitive reframing +5.17 (0.78 to 9.15) were identified as detrimental components; component effects varied with baseline severity and sociodemographics; no GRADE rating and no ethnicity data in the abstract. Caution (mine): a component network meta-analysis estimates added effects under additivity assumptions in brief, non-specialist-delivered packages — it does not show that cognitive restructuring harms in specialist-delivered CBT. — *Lancet Psychiatry* 13(9):747–760, https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00193-8 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY BA performs about as well as cognitive therapy (cognitive restructuring is not the unique lever) — original: Jacobson 1996 (N = 150, three arms; full CT no better than BA alone at termination or 6 months); Dimidjian 2006 (N = 241; BA ≈ medication > CT in more severe depression). Later evidence: Cuijpers 2007 (16 studies; BA vs CT 0.02); Ekers 2014 (26 RCTs; BA vs control −0.74); COBRA 2016 (N = 440, non-inferior, difference 0.1 PHQ-9 points, junior workers delivered BA); Papola 2026 (34 task-shared RCTs: behavioural activation a beneficial component, cognitive reframing — less clearly — a detrimental one); caution: Cuijpers 2019 judge the BA evidence "insufficient" once restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials, as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: direction is consistent over three independent RCT programmes plus meta-analyses, but non-inferiority margins are wide (1.9 PHQ-9 points), the BA literature is of lower average quality, and the low-risk-of-bias subset is thin. — Richards 2016; Ekers 2014; Cuijpers 2019; Papola 2026
- [~] Series-framing note (interpretation, not a finding): BA is the cleanest case of "do the thing, then look at what happens" working without explicit thought-challenging; Jacobson 1996 also found thinking changed in the BA-only arm — consistent with updating through experience, but the data do not show prediction error as the mechanism. Lemmens 2016 (35 mediator studies) says the main obstacle is showing that change in a mediator CAUSES symptom change (see 18-D).

### 18-C. Exposure, extinction and "disconfirming prediction error" — the series' therapy framing, tested
- [def] Exposure therapy 暴露疗法 = planned, repeated contact with a feared cue or situation without the feared outcome happening; it is the clinical proxy of extinction. The basic science (extinction = new, context-dependent inhibitory learning, not erasure; fear returns by spontaneous recovery, renewal, reinstatement, rapid reacquisition — grade A) is in FACTS_T3a §09D; the "learning tracks surprise" rule (Rescorla–Wagner) is in FACTS_T3a §09B. 抑制性学习 inhibitory learning; 预期违背 expectancy violation = the outcome the person expected does not happen. — Craske et al. 2014 definitions as recorded in FACTS_T3a §09D
- [V] Foa & Kozak 1986 (emotional processing theory): fear is a memory structure that serves as an escape/avoidance programme for danger; exposure works because it lets the structure be modified by corrective information; physiological activation and habituation within and across sessions are indicators that processing is taking place; failures are attributed to cognitive defences, high arousal, mood state and erroneous ideation. — *Psychol Bull* 99(1), https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.99.1.20 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- [V] ★ The inhibitory-learning turn: Craske et al. 2008 review evidence that neither the amount of fear reduction within sessions nor the final fear level predicts treatment outcome, argue that performance during training does not reflect learning at the process level, and that whether the secondary inhibitory association can be retrieved depends on context and time more than on fear during training; the strategies are reviewed in the paper but not listed in the abstract. Craske et al. 2014 (eight strategies; a proposal paper with case studies — FACTS_T3a §09D) and Craske, Treanor, Zbozinek & Vervliet 2022 restate it as an "inhibitory retrieval" approach and the OptEx Nexus (fundamental strategies such as expectancy violation and removing safety signals; advanced such as deepened extinction; generalisation strategies such as retrieval cues and multiple contexts); the 2022 abstract reports no data and the authors themselves call for further empirical investigation of the basic premises (paraphrase). — Craske 2008, *Behav Res Ther* 46(1):5–27, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2007.10.003 ; Craske 2022, *Behav Res Ther* 152:104069, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2022.104069 (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- [V] Quirk & Mueller 2008 (review): extinction unfolds in three phases — acquisition, consolidation, retrieval — each relying on particular brain regions (amygdala, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus) and molecular systems; drugs that enhance consolidation or retrieval of extinction may lead to new treatments for anxiety disorders and addiction (abstract-level; no efficacy numbers opened). — *Neuropsychopharmacology* 33(1):56–72, https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.npp.1301555
- [V] Deacon et al. 2013 (RCT; N = 120 participants with elevated anxiety sensitivity — the abstract does not describe them as patients; four single-session conditions: standard brief interoceptive exposure, brief exposure without controlled breathing or long rests, intensive interoceptive exposure designed to optimise inhibitory learning, expressive-writing control): intensive exposure gave larger pre-to-post reductions in anxiety sensitivity and in fear on a straw-breathing task; changes in fear tolerance and in negative outcome expectancies fully accounted for its advantage over brief exposure; gains were maintained at follow-up with NO significant between-group differences; both brief conditions showed high fear sensitisation between trials. — *Behav Res Ther* 51(9):588–596, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2013.06.006 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Exposure-based CBT beats placebo. Hofmann & Smits 2008: 27 randomised placebo-controlled trials in adults with anxiety disorders; completer-sample g = 0.73 for anxiety severity (the interval printed in the record is internally inconsistent — omit it), response OR 4.06 (95% CI 2.78–5.92); strongest for OCD and acute stress disorder, weakest for panic disorder; the CBT advantage did not depend on placebo type, number of sessions or publication year; intent-to-treat analyses existed for only 6 (continuous) and 8 (response) studies. Carpenter et al. 2018 (later, larger; Hofmann and Smits are co-authors, so not independent): 41 RCTs, 2,843 adults (acute stress disorder, GAD, OCD, panic disorder, PTSD, social anxiety disorder) vs psychological or pill placebo: g = 0.56 on target-disorder symptoms, 0.38 other anxiety symptoms, 0.31 depression, 0.30 quality of life; response OR 2.97; large effects for OCD, GAD and acute stress disorder, small-to-moderate for PTSD, social anxiety and panic; in PTSD trials dropout was higher with CBT (29.0%) than placebo (17.2%), with no dropout difference for other disorders. Both bundles are "CBT" — neither isolates exposure. Specific phobia: Wolitzky-Taylor et al. 2008 (33 randomised studies): exposure-based treatment gave large effects vs no treatment and outperformed placebo and alternative active psychotherapies; in vivo exposure beat other forms (imaginal, virtual reality) at post-test but not at follow-up; more sessions predicted better outcomes and multi-session treatment marginally beat single-session on phobia-specific questionnaires; the abstract gives no effect sizes. — Hofmann & Smits 2008, *J Clin Psychiatry* 69(4):621–632, https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.v69n0415 ; Carpenter et al. 2018, *Depress Anxiety* 35(6):502–514, https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22728 ; Wolitzky-Taylor et al. 2008, *Clin Psychol Rev* 28(6):1021–1037, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2008.02.007 (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- [V] One-session phobia treatment: Öst 1989 — 20 consecutive patients with specific phobias, a single session combining in vivo exposure with modelling, mean treatment time 2.1 hours; at an average 4-year follow-up 90% were much improved or completely recovered (an uncontrolled case series, not a trial). — *Behav Res Ther* 27(1):1–7, https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(89)90113-7 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Exposure-based therapy for anxiety disorders beats placebo — original: Hofmann & Smits 2008 (27 RCTs, g = 0.73, response OR 4.06). Later evidence: Carpenter 2018 (41 RCTs, N = 2,843, g = 0.56, OR 2.97; larger for OCD/GAD/acute stress than for PTSD/social anxiety/panic); Wolitzky-Taylor 2008 (33 RCTs, specific phobia: exposure > placebo and > other psychotherapies), as of October 2026.
  GRADE A: direction and a moderate size hold across three meta-analyses of 27–41 randomised trials; cautions — the 2008 and 2018 syntheses share authors, the bundles are "CBT" so exposure is not isolated, the later average is smaller (0.56 vs 0.73), and PTSD dropout was higher with CBT than with placebo. — Hofmann & Smits 2008; Carpenter 2018; Wolitzky-Taylor 2008
- [V] ★ OCD and exposure with response prevention (ERP 暴露与反应阻止) — a case where the effect size depends strongly on comparator and researcher allegiance. Reid et al. 2021, meta-analysis of 36 RCTs, 2,020 patients (537 children/adolescents, 1,483 adults; 1,005 CBT-ERP vs 1,015 controls): CBT-ERP vs all controls g = 0.74 (95% CI 0.51–0.97, k = 36; shrinking with age); vs psychological placebo 1.13 (0.71–1.55, k = 10); vs other active psychological therapies −0.05 (−0.27 to 0.16, k = 8); vs all pharmacological treatments 0.36 (k = 7; the printed lower CI bound is garbled in the record); vs adequate-dose pharmacotherapy 0.32 (−0.00 to 0.64, k = 6). The authors conclude the effect depends on the control condition and raise concerns about methodological rigour and reporting. Öst, Havnen, Hansen & Kvale 2015 (37 RCTs of CBT for OCD published 1993–2014, all using the interview-based Y-BOCS; "effect sizes" — the metric is not named and the abstract gives no CIs, p-values or patient total): CBT vs waiting list 1.31, vs placebo 1.33, vs antidepressant medication 0.55 (CBT significantly better); ERP vs cognitive therapy 0.07 and individual vs group delivery 0.17 (both non-significant); CBT plus medication vs CBT plus placebo 0.25 (not significantly different); the authors note methodological problems in the included trials. — Reid, Laws, Drummond, Vismara, Grancini, Mpavaenda & Fineberg 2021, *Compr Psychiatry* 106:152223, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comppsych.2021.152223 (abstract via OpenAlex) ; Öst et al. 2015, *Clin Psychol Rev* 40:156–169, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2015.06.003 (abstract via Europe PMC, PMID 26117062)
- [V] ★ A second, independent meta-analysis of psychological treatments for OCD points the same way on bias: Wang et al. 2024 (10 authors; 48 RCTs, 55 comparisons, published 1992 to 1 January 2023; the participant total is not in the abstract): psychological treatments vs controls g = −1.14 (95% CI −1.31 to −0.97; I² = 72.2%), robust to outcome measure and outlier removal BUT not significant in a sensitivity analysis limited to low-risk-of-bias studies; 87% of the RCTs were rated high risk of bias; effects were no longer significant at 3–6 or 6–12 months of follow-up; larger effects with more severe baseline symptoms; treatment type, control group and format moderated the effect; dropout did not differ; the authors call for caution and for more rigorous trials. — *Psychol Med* 54(11):2838–2851, https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291724001375 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY ERP for OCD (first-choice psychological treatment) — original: the premise that Reid 2021 tests. Later evidence: Reid 2021 (36 RCTs, N = 2,020: g = 0.74 vs all controls, 1.13 vs psychological placebo, −0.05 vs other active therapies, 0.32 vs adequate-dose medication [−0.00 to 0.64]; allegiance-suspected studies g = 0.95 vs 0.02 in the 8 without); Öst 2015 (37 RCTs: CBT > antidepressants, 0.55; adding medication to CBT 0.25, not significant); Carpenter 2018 (large effects vs placebo for OCD); Wang 2024 (48 RCTs: g = −1.14 vs controls but not significant in the low-risk-of-bias subset; 87% of trials high risk of bias), as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: direction (ERP/CBT beats waitlist- and placebo-type comparators and is at least as good as medication) is supported by independent meta-analyses; the SIZE is disputed — it tracks researcher allegiance, only 8 studies were low risk of bias (Reid 2021), the allegiance contrast rests on 8 studies, and an independent 2024 meta-analysis (Wang) found the pooled effect not significant when restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials — so do not teach "g ≈ 0.7" as settled. — Reid 2021; Wang 2024; Öst 2015; Carpenter 2018
- [V] ★ Does exposure work BY violating expectancies? Recent direct tests (all 2025–2026; correlational/mediational analyses or small trials):
  - Zbozinek, Rosenberg, Treanor & Craske 2026 (RCT, N = 84, social anxiety disorder and panic disorder; two 9-session therapies — inhibitory-retrieval vs habituation-based; three of the four authors, Craske, Treanor and Zbozinek, co-authored the 2022 inhibitory-retrieval model update, so this is a test by the model's own developers): change in expectancy of the feared outcome predicted anxiety at post-treatment and at 3-month follow-up; the expectancy-based learning rate predicted follow-up anxiety (post-treatment only marginally); FEAR change did NOT predict anxiety; the expectancy variables still predicted after controlling for fear change. — *Behav Res Ther* 202:105040, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2026.105040
  - Thaon de Saint André et al. 2025 (N = 516 patients with anxiety disorders, ≥ 10 exposures each): within-exercise threat-expectancy change and fear reduction were only weakly correlated; each independently predicted outcome (faster learning rate; larger relative fear drop → better outcome); unclear whether they are separate mechanisms or facets of one. — *Behav Res Ther* 194:104856, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2025.104856
  - Borchert et al. 2025 (648 patients, 8,416 exposure records; same consortium author list as Thaon de Saint André 2025 and Straube 2026 — the abstracts do not name the trial, datasets probably overlap [~]): positive emotions after exposure (hope, joy) were linked to learning indicators (expectancy violation, expectancy change, prediction-error learning rate); the strongest predictor was post-exposure threat expectancy; higher positive emotion predicted better outcome beyond the learning indicators and partly explained the learning–outcome link. — *Behav Res Ther* 195:104880, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2025.104880
  - Straube et al. 2026 (N = 605, 12 sessions of exposure therapy): general negative expectations → worse outcome, mediated by lower treatment-success expectations; the sequential path through the learning rate during exposure was significant but weak (β = 0.0059; 95% CI 0.0008–0.0134). — *Psychother Psychosom*, https://doi.org/10.1159/000551966 (abstract via Europe PMC)
  - Barnes-Horowitz, Zbozinek & Craske 2025 (RCT, N = 84 university students, public-speaking anxiety, 16 exposures; same laboratory as Zbozinek 2026): anxiety fell in both rehearsal conditions with no difference; a faster expectancy learning rate and a higher expectancy intercept at the first exposure predicted lower follow-up anxiety; relief intercept predicted it, relief rate did not. — *Behav Res Ther* 193:104817, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2025.104817
  - Hilleke et al. 2025 (N = 268, panic disorder with agoraphobia, CBT with in-vivo exposure): patients over-predicted their fear; expected and experienced fear both fell across exposures while the gap between them stayed stable; a decline in EXPECTED fear strongly predicted post-treatment and follow-up outcome; the authors conclude that violating excessive fear expectations does not appear to be necessary for symptom reduction (paraphrase). — *Depress Anxiety* 2025, article 6963860, https://doi.org/10.1155/da/6963860
  - Winkler et al. 2026 (N = 43 adults with social anxiety disorder in CBT, 523 exposures logged by smartphone): exposures did produce prediction error and surprising outcomes were rated more learning-relevant, but larger prediction error did NOT predict lower next-session anxiety or greater overall improvement; the authors conclude the size of prediction error alone is not enough. — *Behav Res Ther* 205:105147, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2026.105147
  - Kooistra et al. 2025 (RCT, N = 60 adults with PTSD, ONE 90-minute session, expectancy-violation focus vs none): no immediate difference in fear response or PTSD symptoms (both fell); the expectancy-violation group showed a larger drop in threat appraisal that appeared to mediate symptom reduction; longer-term benefit untested. — *Eur J Psychotraumatol* 16(1), https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2024.2447183
  - Nadel et al. 2025 (naturalistic, N = 1,968, OCD/anxiety intensive outpatient programme; interrupted time series): switching from an emotional-processing to an inhibitory-learning approach produced NO significant difference in symptom reduction; the authors suggest the two overlap in practice. — *J Clin Psychol* 81(12):1231–1243, https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.70032
  - Already recorded in FACTS_T3a §09D (not re-opened here): Johnco 2025 (N = 249, public-speaking anxiety — exposure organised around expectancy violation, or preceded by cognitive restructuring, beat exposure without explicit expectancy processing; the behavioural-experiment edge was non-significant), Wannemüller 2026 (N = 519, fear of flying — four add-on strategies including expectancy violation; no condition superior), Brudan 2025 (lab super-extinction produced MORE recovery).
  - Zbozinek, Khalsa & Craske 2026 (BMJ review, "New and emerging treatments for anxiety disorders"): lists the inhibitory-retrieval model and behavioural experiments targeting intolerance of uncertainty, with D-cycloserine and scopolamine, as strategies to augment exposure; the abstract has no numbers. — *BMJ* 392:e083441, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-083441
  - Buric et al. 2025 (protocol): evidence that change in dysfunctional cognitions mediates CBT outcome in youth anxiety, OCD and PTSD is mixed and underpowered (introduction, paraphrase). — *BMJ Open* 15(12):e113007, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2025-113007
- STUDY Expectancy violation / prediction error as the mechanism of exposure (the series' "disconfirming prediction error") — original: Craske 2008 → 2014 → 2022 proposals (inhibitory learning; expectancy violation as the first strategy); Deacon 2013 (RCT, N = 120: intensive exposure's advantage statistically accounted for by changes in negative outcome expectancies). Later evidence (2025–2026, above): expectancy change / learning rate predicts outcome in Zbozinek 2026 (N = 84), Thaon de Saint André 2025 (N = 516), Barnes-Horowitz 2025 (N = 84), Hilleke 2025 (N = 268, expected-fear decline) and, weakly, Straube 2026 (N = 605); BUT prediction-error SIZE did not predict outcome (Winkler 2026, N = 43), maximising expectancy violation gave no better immediate outcome (Kooistra 2025, N = 60; Wannemüller 2026), switching a programme to an inhibitory-learning approach changed nothing (Nadel 2025, N = 1,968), and Hilleke 2025 concludes violating excessive expectations is not necessary, as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: that a larger or faster fall in the feared-outcome expectancy during exposure goes with a better outcome — several samples (N = 84–605), correlational/mediational, several from one consortium or from the model's developers, no head-to-head causal test. — Zbozinek 2026; Thaon de Saint André 2025; Hilleke 2025
  GRADE C: that exposure should be DESIGNED to maximise expectancy violation, or that prediction-error size is the unique mechanism — the direct tests are small, mixed or null. — Winkler 2026; Kooistra 2025; Nadel 2025
- STUDY Within-session fear habituation as the mechanism of exposure — original: Foa & Kozak 1986 (habituation as an index of emotional processing).   GRADE C: mixed — fear reduction predicts outcome in some 2025–2026 samples and not in others; the textbook "habituation is the mechanism" is neither established nor refuted. — Craske 2008; Zbozinek 2026; Thaon de Saint André 2025

Later evidence: Johnco 2025 (N = 249; the behavioural-experiment advantage over cognitive restructuring was non-significant — FACTS_T3a §09D); Zbozinek, Khalsa & Craske 2026 list behavioural experiments among augmentation strategies (no numbers), as of October 2026.
  GRADE C: thin (14 small studies, short exposures, allegiance) — explicit prediction-testing may add to plain exposure but this is not established. — McMillan & Lee 2010, *Clin Psychol Rev* 30(5):467–478, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2010.01.003 (abstract via Europe PMC)

### 18-D. Cognitive therapy: lasting effects, and do changes in thinking CAUSE improvement?
- [V] ★ Hollon et al. 2005: patients with moderate-to-severe depression who responded to cognitive therapy (CT; up to 3 booster sessions allowed) or to medication were followed for 12 months after stopping CT, or after continuing medication or being switched to placebo (stopping medication), then for 12 more treatment-free months if they had not relapsed. N = 104 (57.8% of those originally assigned). Relapse in the 12-month continuation phase: 30.8% (CT) vs 76.2% (medication stopped; P = .004) vs 47.2% (medication continued; not significantly different from CT, P = .20). Relapse = ≥ 2 weeks meeting major-depression criteria or HDRS ≥ 14. — *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 62(4):417–422, https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.62.4.417 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Cuijpers et al. 2013 meta-analysis (9 studies, 506 patients; follow-up 6–18 months; acute-phase CBT only vs medication continued or stopped): CBT vs stopped medication — 8 studies, relapse OR 2.61 (95% CI 1.58–4.31; NNT 5) favouring CBT; vs continued medication — 5 studies, OR 1.62 (0.97–2.72; NNT 10), not significant (trend p < 0.1); short-term outcomes similar, with fewer dropouts under CBT; the authors urge caution because few studies were available. — *BMJ Open* 3(4):e002542, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2012-002542
- [V] The review behind the "enduring effects" claim: Hollon, Stewart & Strunk (*Annu Rev Psychol* 57; OpenAlex metadata year 2005): cognitive and behavioural interventions appear to have enduring effects that lower relapse risk after treatment ends, best documented for depression and anxiety disorders; it is unclear whether this comes from changing causal processes, from compensatory strategies, or from other mechanisms; psychoactive medications appear largely palliative (their wording, paraphrased) — an authors' interpretation that fits ANTLER (17-B10: relapse after stopping maintenance medication 56% vs 39%) but does not show medication has no benefit while it is taken. — https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.57.102904.190044 (abstract via OpenAlex)
- STUDY Cognitive therapy has an enduring (relapse-preventing) effect compared with stopping medication — original: Hollon 2005 (N = 104: relapse 30.8% vs 76.2% after stopping medication; 47.2% on continued medication, n.s.). Later evidence: Cuijpers 2013 (9 studies, 506 patients: OR 2.61 vs stopped medication, NNT 5; OR 1.62, n.s., vs continued medication); ANTLER 2021 (17-B10) shows stopping maintenance medication raises relapse, as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: direction holds in a small trial and a nine-study meta-analysis (with overlapping authors); an advantage over CONTINUED medication is not shown; few studies. — Hollon 2005; Cuijpers 2013
- [V] ★ Mediators — do changes in thinking come first and cause symptom change? Lemmens, Müller, Arntz & Huibers 2016 reviewed 35 studies of psychological mediators of psychotherapy for depression: despite growing attention and better designs the field remains methodologically inconsistent; the main obstacle is showing that change in a mediator CAUSES change in depressive symptoms; the authors suggest therapy may work through several interacting mechanisms at multiple levels. The abstract names no specific mediator and gives no temporal-precedence result. Formal criteria for a causal mediator: Cuijpers 2019 (18-A). Not opened — do not use: Burns & Spangler 2000, Lorenzo-Luaces 2015, Kazantzis 2018, Wells 1995. — *Clin Psychol Rev* 50:95–107, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2016.09.004 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Cognitive change is the causal mechanism of CBT — original: Beck's cognitive model (Beck 1979 book; not opened); Jacobson 1996 found thinking also changed in the behavioural-activation-only arm (18-B). Later evidence: Lemmens 2016 (35 mediator studies; the causal link is the main obstacle); Cuijpers 2019 (no common or specific factor meets causal standards); Buric 2025 (youth: mixed, underpowered); Papola 2026 (task-shared packages: cognitive reframing, less clearly, a detrimental component — 18-B), as of October 2026.
  GRADE C: CBT works (18-A), but that it works BECAUSE thinking changes first is unproven — the mediator literature cannot yet separate cause from accompaniment, and a no-restructuring arm also changed thinking. — Lemmens 2016; Cuijpers 2019; Jacobson 1996
### 18-E. Antidepressant medication — what the big analyses say (descriptive only; NOT advice)
- [def] WRITER NOTE (care): report average effects across trials, say individual response varies, and say that starting or stopping a medication is a decision for the person and a prescribing clinician (stopping has its own risks — ANTLER in 17-B10). Do not rank drugs for readers and do not say what anyone should take.
- [V] ★ Cipriani et al. 2018 (network meta-analysis; 21 antidepressants; double-blind RCTs searched to 8 Jan 2016; 522 trials, 116,477 participants): ALL 21 drugs were more effective than placebo for response in acute treatment, odds ratios from 2.13 (95% credible interval 1.89–2.41, amitriptyline) to 1.37 (1.16–1.63, reboxetine); differences between active drugs were smaller (ORs 1.15–1.55 across all trials) with wide credible intervals for most comparisons; certainty of evidence moderate to very low; risk of bias high in 46 trials (9%), moderate in 380 (73%), low in 96 (18%). ADDED (tolerability, the one sourced sentence on side-effect-related outcomes): for dropout from the trial for any reason vs placebo only agomelatine (OR 0.84, 0.72–0.97) and fluoxetine (OR 0.88, 0.80–0.96) had fewer dropouts and clomipramine more (OR 1.30, 1.01–1.68); onset timing and specific side-effect rates are not in the abstract and were not opened. The abstract reports no standardised mean difference — the often-quoted "SMD ≈ 0.3" is NOT in it. — *Lancet* 391(10128):1357–1366, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)32802-7 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Disputed reading, side 1 — Munkholm, Paludan-Müller & Boesen 2019 reanalysed the Cipriani meta-analyses (522 trials, 116,477 participants) and clinical study reports for 19 of the trials: published trials showed larger effects than unpublished (p < 0.0001); trials with a placebo run-in showed larger effects on investigator-rated scales than those without (p = 0.05); outcome data in the review differed from the clinical study reports in 12 of 19 trials (63%); GRADE certainty for placebo comparisons very low (high risk of bias, indirectness, publication bias); mean difference vs placebo on the 17-item Hamilton scale (range 0–52) 1.97 points (95% CI 1.74–2.21); they conclude it is unclear whether antidepressants are more effective than placebo. — *BMJ Open* 9(6):e024886, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-024886 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Disputed reading, side 2 — Stone et al. 2022 (individual-participant data from FDA-submitted placebo-controlled trials of single-drug treatment, 1979–2016; 232 trials, 73,388 participants; scores converted to HAMD-17 equivalents): drug beat placebo by a random-effects mean of 1.75 points (95% CI 1.63–1.86), a gap that grew with baseline severity (P < 0.001) and showed no time trend after adjustment; responses fell into three groups with mean improvements of 16.0, 8.9 and 1.7 points (authors' labels Large, Non-specific, Minimal); Large response 24.5% on drug vs 9.6% on placebo, Minimal response 12.2% vs 21.5% (record prints "12.2.%"); the authors conclude about 15% of participants gain a substantial effect beyond placebo and call for predictors of who they are. — *BMJ* 378:e067606, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2021-067606 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Disputed reading, side 3 — Meyerson, Pieper & Hoyle 2023 (secondary analysis of the same FDA database, 232 trials 1979–2016, restricted to severe depression, HAMD-17 ≥ 20, 57,313 participants; quantile treatment-effects analysis): the drug arm's response distribution was more favourable across the whole range, with the largest drug–placebo gap at the 55th quantile (absolute improvement 13.5%, 95% CI 12.4–14.4) shrinking toward the tails — a broadly distributed, modest shift rather than a small subgroup of large responders; the authors add that if the model's assumptions fail the data could also fit a smaller subset with fuller response. — *JAMA Netw Open* 6(6):e2317714, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.17714 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Improvement in placebo arms is large: Huneke et al. 2024 (umbrella review of 20 meta-analyses, 1,691 randomised trials, 261,730 patients; 18 of 20 meta-analyses low quality on AMSTAR-2): pooled "placebo effects" g = 1.10 (95% CI 1.06–1.15) in depression, 0.90 in alcohol use disorder, d = 1.85 in generalised anxiety disorder, 1.41 in restless legs, 0.32 in OCD, 0.35 in primary insomnia, 0.33 in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders; larger with later publication year, younger age, more sites, larger samples, higher baseline severity and larger active-treatment effects. The abstract does not say how the effect was measured; such pooled figures are usually pre-to-post change inside placebo arms, which includes natural recovery and regression to the mean (my caution) — do not teach g = 1.10 as the causal effect of a sugar pill. — *Mol Psychiatry* 29(12):3915–3925, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-024-02638-x (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Mild depression has almost no direct drug-vs-placebo trials in the review that looked: Urata et al. 2025 (13 authors; PubMed and Embase to November 2024; RCTs of drugs or supplements in diagnosed mild depression; 8 RCTs, 1,049 participants) report that no included RCT directly compared an antidepressant with placebo; the one pooled comparison was St John's wort vs fluoxetine (two trials: response RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.78–1.18; dropout RR 1.08, 0.62–1.88), and single trials of eicosapentaenoic acid and Rhodiola rosea beat placebo. I did not read the full text. Inference (mine): the severity gradient above comes from analyses that pool trials of mixed severity, not from trials restricted to mild depression. — *Neuropsychopharmacol Rep* 45(1):e70008, https://doi.org/10.1002/npr2.70008 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [~] Reading across (my synthesis, not a finding): every large analysis finds a statistically reliable drug–placebo difference that is small on the rating scale (≈ 1.75–2 points of 0–52) — whether the average hides a minority with large benefit (Stone), reflects a broad modest shift (Meyerson 2023, severe-depression subset) or is inflated by bias and unblinding problems (Munkholm) is unresolved; the meaning of "clinically significant" is itself disputed. Teach it as an open dispute with both numbers.
- [V] Severity dependence: Kirsch et al. 2008 (FDA-submitted trials of four newer antidepressants): the drug–placebo difference grew with baseline severity — nearly absent in moderately depressed patients, small even in the very severely depressed, reaching conventional clinical significance only at the upper end; their reading: the severity relationship reflects lower placebo response in very severe depression rather than a greater drug response (abstract gives no numbers). Fournier et al. 2010 (patient-level; 6 studies, 718 patients): d < 0.20 when baseline HDRS < 23; reached the NICE threshold for clinically significant benefit at HDRS 25; benefit may be minimal or absent on average in mild-to-moderate depression and substantial in very severe depression. Stone 2022 (above) also found the severity gradient. — Kirsch 2008, *PLoS Med* 5(2):e45, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0050045 ; Fournier 2010, *JAMA* 303(1):47–53, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.1943 (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Antidepressants vs placebo in major depression — original: Kirsch 2008 (benefit grows with severity; nearly absent in moderate depression); Fournier 2010 (6 studies, N = 718: d < 0.20 below HDRS 23). Later evidence: Cipriani 2018 (522 trials, 116,477 participants: every one of 21 drugs beat placebo, OR 1.37–2.13; certainty moderate to very low); Munkholm 2019 (reanalysis: 1.97 HDRS-17 points, certainty very low, publication/run-in/outcome-data problems); Stone 2022 (232 trials, 73,388 participants: 1.75 points; 24.5% vs 9.6% Large response); Meyerson 2023 (57,313 participants with HAMD-17 ≥ 20: a broad modest shift, minority-responder reading not excluded); Huneke 2024 (placebo-arm improvement g = 1.10 across depression trials), as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: direction (drug > placebo on average) holds in every large analysis and the benefit is larger at higher baseline severity, but the size (≈ 2 scale points), its clinical meaning, bias, and who benefits (a minority with large effects in Stone 2022 vs a broad modest shift in Meyerson 2023) are disputed. — Cipriani 2018; Munkholm 2019; Stone 2022; Meyerson 2023
- [~] Cross-reference: "antidepressants correct a chemical imbalance" is graded D as a slogan in 17-B3 (serotonin umbrella review vs critics); a drug–placebo difference does not show the mechanism. CBT vs medication and combined treatment: Cuijpers 2023 (18-A). NICE (NG222) and APA depression-guideline wording could not be opened — see COULD NOT VERIFY.

### 18-F. Delivery and access (answers to the treatment gap in 17-A3)
- [V] Guided internet CBT ≈ face-to-face CBT: Andersson et al. 2014 — 13 studies, 1,053 participants (social anxiety 3, panic 3, depressive symptoms 2, body dissatisfaction 2, tinnitus 1, male sexual dysfunction 1, spider phobia 1; the in-person comparison was individual therapy in 6 studies and group therapy in 7); post-treatment g = −0.01 (95% CI −0.13 to 0.12); study quality did not change the result; each condition has few studies, so more research is needed. — *World Psychiatry* 13(3):288–295, https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20151 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Self-guided internet CBT for depressive symptoms: Karyotaki et al. 2017 — individual-participant data from 13 of 16 eligible RCTs (3,876 participants; controls = usual care, waitlist or attention control): Hedges' g = 0.27, response OR 1.95 (95% CI 1.52–2.50), NNT 8; greater adherence went with lower symptoms and higher response; no participant- or study-level variable moderated outcome. — *JAMA Psychiatry* 74(4):351–359, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2017.0044 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Smartphone apps: Linardon et al. 2019 — 66 RCTs; app-supported interventions beat control conditions for depressive symptoms g = 0.28 (n = 54 comparisons), generalised anxiety 0.30 (n = 39), stress 0.35 (n = 27), quality of life 0.35 (n = 43), general psychiatric distress 0.40 (n = 12), social anxiety 0.58 (n = 6), positive affect 0.44 (n = 6); NOT significant for panic (g = −0.05, n = 3), PTSD (0.18, n = 4) or negative affect (−0.08, n = 5); CBT-based apps and apps with professional guidance and reminders did better on several outcomes; no significant difference from active interventions but few comparisons (n ≤ 13); the authors call apps a low-intensity option that should not replace professional services; the abstract has no publication-bias result. — *World Psychiatry* 18(3):325–336, https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20673 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Update on apps: Linardon et al. 2024 (6 authors; 176 RCTs of smartphone apps for depressive or anxiety symptoms; about 33,567 participants for depression and 22,394 for generalised anxiety, as the abstract gives them): depression g = 0.28 (NNT 11.5) and generalised anxiety g = 0.26 (NNT 12.4) vs control groups, both significant, holding at follow-up and after removing small-sample and high-risk-of-bias trials; larger when apps included CBT features or chatbot technology (depression) and when generalised anxiety was the target and the app CBT-based or mood-monitoring (anxiety); social anxiety g = 0.52, OCD 0.51, PTSD 0.12, acrophobia 0.90, panic −0.12 (non-significant), which the authors caution rest mostly on small high-risk-of-bias trials; the abstract has no breakdown by control type and no publication-bias adjustment. — *World Psychiatry* 23(1):139–149, https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.21183 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Digital delivery of CBT — original: Andersson 2014 (guided iCBT vs face-to-face: g = −0.01, 13 studies, N = 1,053). Later evidence: Karyotaki 2017 (self-guided, 13 RCTs, N = 3,876: g = 0.27, NNT 8); Linardon 2019 (apps, 66 RCTs: g ≈ 0.28–0.30 for depression and anxiety, nothing significant for panic or PTSD); Linardon 2024 (176 RCTs: depression g = 0.28, generalised anxiety 0.26, holding after removing small and high-risk-of-bias trials); Cuijpers 2023 (unguided self-help CBT g = 0.45), as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: guided formats approach face-to-face effects in small direct comparisons (few studies per condition); unguided and app formats give small effects for depression and generalised anxiety (g ≈ 0.26–0.28 in 176 app trials) and condition-specific effects elsewhere. — Andersson 2014; Karyotaki 2017; Linardon 2019, 2024
- [V] ★ Lay-worker delivery, example 1 — Friendship Bench, Zimbabwe (Chibanda et al. 2016): cluster RCT in 24 Harare primary-care clinics (Sept 2014–May 2015) of 573 adults (286 intervention, 287 control; 86.4% women; median age 33; 41.7% HIV-positive; 90.9% completed 6-month follow-up) who screened positive on the Shona Symptom Questionnaire (SSQ-14); intervention = 6 sessions of individual problem-solving therapy from trained, supervised lay health workers plus an optional 6-session peer-support programme, vs enhanced usual care (standard care plus information, education and support). SSQ-14 at 6 months 3.81 (95% CI 3.28–4.34) vs 8.90 (8.33–9.47), adjusted mean difference −4.86 (−5.63 to −4.10), adjusted risk ratio 0.21 (0.15–0.29); PHQ-9 ≥ 11 in 13.7% vs 49.9% (ARR 0.28; 0.22–0.34). Limits: one country, 6-month follow-up, control = enhanced usual care. — *JAMA* 316(24):2618–2626, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.19102 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Lay-worker delivery, example 2 — Healthy Activity Program (HAP), Goa, India (Patel et al. 2017): RCT in 10 primary health centres, adults 18–65 with PHQ-9 > 14, N = 495 (247 to enhanced usual care + HAP, 248 to enhanced usual care alone; assessors masked); 3-month remission (PHQ-9 < 10) 147/230 (64%) vs 91/236 (39%), adjusted prevalence ratio 1.61 (95% CI 1.34–1.93); BDI-II 19.99 vs 27.52, adjusted mean difference −7.57 (−10.27 to −4.86); HAP also improved disability, days out of work, behavioural activation and suicidal thoughts/attempts (secondary outcomes); incremental cost $9,333 per QALY gained (95% CI 3,862–28,169; 2015 international dollars), 87% probability of cost-effectiveness in this setting; serious adverse events rare and similar (4% vs 4%); the abstract has no 12-month result and does not state the therapy's theoretical basis (it lists "behavioural activation" only as a secondary outcome). — *Lancet* 389(10065):176–185, https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)31589-6 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Later work on the lay-worker model (2025): Patena et al. 2025 (15 authors; systematic review of the Friendship Bench's implementation — 641 articles screened, 7 included, ALL conducted in Zimbabwe and all reporting implementation outcomes; concludes the programme is acceptable, appropriate and feasible there; no effectiveness figures in the abstract) — *Glob Ment Health* 12:e65, https://doi.org/10.1017/gmh.2025.10025 . Beji-Chauke et al. 2025 (13 authors; open-label cluster-randomised hybrid implementation trial in 26 Harare primary-care clinics and their communities, ages 16–19: the standard Friendship Bench delivered by community health workers vs a Youth Friendship Bench delivered by trained university students; uptake 86.6% (187/216) vs 95.6% (220/230); SSQ-14 ≥ 8 at six months similar between arms, adjusted OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.36–1.17, N = 528; total programme costs higher for the youth version) — a comparison of two delivery models, NOT a trial against usual care — *BMC Psychiatry* 25:673, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-025-06755-x . For the 2026 meta-analysis of 34 task-shared RCTs see Papola 2026 (18-B). (abstracts via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Task-shifted (lay-worker) psychological treatment works in low-resource primary care — original: Chibanda 2016 (cluster RCT, 24 clinics, N = 573); Patel 2017 (RCT, 10 centres, N = 495). Later evidence: the two trials agree on direction in two countries with two different programmes (problem-solving therapy; HAP); Papola 2026 (IPD component network meta-analysis of 34 task-shared RCTs, N = 10,612 with IPD — its abstract reports component effects, not an overall effect size); Patena 2025 (all 7 Friendship Bench implementation studies were in Zimbabwe), as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: two independent RCTs agree on direction and large symptom differences, but controls were enhanced usual care, follow-up 3–6 months, the cost-effectiveness figure is from one setting, every Friendship Bench study in the 2025 implementation review was from Zimbabwe, and I did not open a meta-analytic overall effect for task-shared care (Papola 2026 gives component effects). — Chibanda 2016; Patel 2017; Papola 2026

### 18-G. Interventions that cause harm or do not work
- [V] Lilienfeld 2007: argues that some psychological treatments harm significant numbers of people and that the field has neglected the problem; revisits research on deterioration effects, outlines methodological obstacles to identifying potentially harmful therapies (PHTs), and offers a provisional list of PHTs — the abstract does not name them, so the list is NOT verified here; do not reproduce it (recovered-memory therapy and facilitated communication are therefore not used). — *Perspect Psychol Sci* 2(1):53–70, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00029.x (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ "Scared Straight" juvenile-awareness programmes (prison visits meant to deter): Petrosino et al. 2013 Cochrane review — 9 randomised trials in 8 US states, 1967–1992, 946 participants (almost all male, average ages 15–17 per study); 7 studies with official offending data were meta-analysed: random-effects OR 1.72 (95% CI 1.13–2.62), fixed-effect OR 1.68 (1.20–2.36) for later offending (OR > 1 = MORE offending than no-intervention controls); sensitivity analyses 1.47 (1.03–2.11) without the weakly randomised study, 1.96 (1.25–3.08) without the high-attrition study, 1.68 (1.10–2.58) without both; conclusion: the programmes increase delinquency and the authors do not recommend them. — *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2013(4):CD002796, https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002796.pub2 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Project D.A.R.E. (school drug-prevention programme) is ineffective rather than harmful: West & O'Neal 2004 meta-analysis of published outcome evaluations — overall weighted effect r = 0.011, Cohen's d = 0.023 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.08; z = 0.73), not significant; the abstract does not give the number of studies. — *Am J Public Health* 94(6):1027–1029, https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.94.6.1027 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Sexual-orientation change efforts (SOCE 性取向转变努力, often called "conversion therapy") — position of a professional body, read in the PDF (pages 1–6): APA Resolution on SOCE (February 2021). SOCE = a range of techniques used by mental-health professionals and non-professionals with the goal of changing sexual orientation. The resolution states (paraphrase) that SOCE lack a sufficient scientific basis; that diversity in sexual orientation is "normal human variation"; that research since APA's 2009 task-force report supports the conclusions that former participants look back on SOCE as harmful and that there is no evidence sexual orientation can be changed (that orientation can evolve for some people does not mean it can be altered by intervention); that sexual-minority youth and adults who underwent SOCE are significantly more likely to report suicidality and depression than those who did not, with the elevated suicidality, including multiple suicide attempts, persisting after adjustment for other risk factors (it cites Blosnich et al. 2020 and Green et al. 2020, the latter reporting SOCE as the strongest predictor of multiple suicide attempts after adjustment for other known risk factors, page 1224 of that paper as cited in the resolution); that as of December 2020 at least 20 US states, D.C. and more than 70 municipalities prohibited licensed practitioners from using SOCE with minors; and that a SAMHSA 2015 consensus panel described SOCE as coercive and potentially harmful, not part of behavioral health treatment, and recommended ending it for minors. The resolution describes the harms as "associated with" SOCE across a wide range of study designs (I did not open the primary studies), so teach "associated with" — its own wording — not a measured causal size. — APA 2021, https://www.apa.org/about/policy/resolution-sexual-orientation-change-efforts.pdf
- [~] Psychological debriefing after trauma (grade A for no benefit, B for possible harm) is recorded in 17-B7 — do not repeat it here, cite it.
- STUDY Well-meant programmes that backfire — original: Scared Straight was expected to deter (prison visits); psychological debriefing was routine (17-B7). Later evidence: Petrosino 2013 (9 RCTs, 946 participants: OR 1.72 for later offending); West & O'Neal 2004 (D.A.R.E. d = 0.023, n.s.); Rose 2002 / van Emmerik 2002 (17-B7), as of October 2026.
  GRADE A: Scared Straight does not reduce later offending — a Cochrane synthesis of nine randomised trials found no benefit in any analysis. — Petrosino 2013
  GRADE B: that it INCREASES offending (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.13–2.62) — a pooled estimate from seven old (1967–1992), small trials with a wide interval, though every sensitivity analysis gave the same direction. — Petrosino 2013
  GRADE B: D.A.R.E. as evaluated in the published literature has no significant effect on substance use (d = 0.023) — one 2004 meta-analysis whose study count was not readable in the abstract; it supports earlier research. — West & O'Neal 2004

### 18-H. Outcome monitoring and cultural adaptation
- [V] Routine outcome monitoring (ROM): Lambert, Whipple & Kleinstäuber 2018 — 24 studies of the two most-studied systems (the Partners for Change Outcome Management System and the Outcome Questionnaire System): about two-thirds found monitoring-assisted therapy did better than treatment as usual with the same therapists; average effects small to moderate; feedback reduced deterioration and nearly doubled the rate of clinically significant or reliable change among clients predicted to have a poor outcome; the abstract gives no effect-size numbers. — *Psychotherapy* 55(4):520–537, https://doi.org/10.1037/pst0000167 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Progress-feedback (outcome monitoring) improves therapy outcomes — original/later: Lambert 2018 meta-analysis (24 studies, two systems; about two-thirds positive; small-to-moderate effects; larger for clients flagged as not on track). No independent newer synthesis was opened, as of October 2026.
  GRADE B: direction and a small-to-moderate size, concentrated in two systems and in clients predicted to do poorly; a third of studies found no advantage. — Lambert 2018
- [V] Cultural adaptation: Hall et al. 2016 — 78 studies, 13,998 participants (95% non-European American), psychopathology outcomes; culturally adapted interventions vs other conditions (no intervention or other interventions) g = 0.67 (p < .001); adapted vs the UNADAPTED version of the same intervention g = .52; treatment studies g = .76 vs prevention studies g = .25 (p = .03); mood/anxiety measures g = .76 vs general psychopathology measures g = .48 (p = .02); in the 16 studies reporting remission, adapted interventions had 4.68 times the odds of remission (p < .001) — largest vs no intervention (marginal OR 9.80), smaller vs manualised interventions (3.47) and other active non-manualised interventions (3.38); the authors say research has not yet adequately tested whether adaptation affects culture-specific psychopathology; they pooled the studies to reconcile 11 earlier meta-analyses with differing findings. — *Behav Ther* 47(6):993–1014, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2016.09.005 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY Culturally adapted psychological interventions beat unadapted ones — original: the call for culturally responsive interventions (Forehand & Kotchick 1996, as cited by Hall); 11 earlier meta-analyses with differing findings. Later evidence: Hall 2016 (78 studies, N = 13,998: adapted vs unadapted g = .52; vs other conditions g = .67), as of October 2026; no later synthesis opened.
  GRADE B: a medium advantage over unadapted versions in one multilevel meta-analysis that reconciled disagreeing earlier ones; heterogeneity by study type and outcome, and untested for culture-specific problems. — Hall 2016

### 18-I. Myths, widget constants and bets (lesson 18)
- [✗] "The average therapy effect is d ≈ 0.7 (the 76th percentile)": that is the UNADJUSTED figure; bias-adjusted estimates are ≈ 0.3–0.4 (Cuijpers 2010, 2019), a 4,281-analysis multiverse averages g = 0.56 (Plessen 2023), and the number depends on the comparator (18-A, grade B for size, A for direction).
- [✗] "Shedler 2010 showed psychodynamic therapy d = 0.97": the abstract contains no numbers; the strongest real tests are equivalence analyses (Steinert 2017, B) and the long-term claim is C (18-A).
- [✗] "All therapies are equally effective for every problem" (dodo-bird verdict): near-zero average differences for adult depression (Cuijpers 2023 g = 0.06) but not shown for every disorder; and "common factors cause it" is unproven (grade C).
- [✗] "Exposure works because fear habituates within a session" (grade C — Craske 2008 vs 2025–2026 data) and "exposure works by maximising prediction error" (C for the design claim, B for the correlation).
- [✗] "Cognitive restructuring is the active ingredient of CBT": a behavioural-activation-only arm did as well (Jacobson 1996; COBRA 2016), and mediation of cognitive change is unproven (grades B/C).
- [✗] "Talking about a trauma immediately helps" — routine single-session debriefing does not prevent PTSD (17-B7, grade A); "Scared Straight deters" — no benefit (grade A) and probably more offending (grade B); "D.A.R.E. prevents drug use" — no significant effect (grade B).
- [✗] "Sexual orientation can be changed by therapy" — see the APA 2021 resolution (18-G).

### 19-A. Relationships, loneliness and health
- [def] Constructs: social relationships (quantity and quality), objective social isolation (few contacts, living alone) and perceived loneliness (felt gap between wanted and actual connection, 孤独). ### 19-B. Money and well-being (the adversarial collaboration, and what came after)
- [def] Two kinds of well-being: EMOTIONAL (experienced) well-being = frequency and intensity of everyday feelings such as joy, stress, sadness, anger, affection; LIFE EVALUATION = how people think about their life as a whole. — Kahneman & Deaton 2010
- [V] ★ Kahneman & Deaton 2010: > 450,000 responses to the Gallup-Healthways Well-Being Index (a daily survey of about 1,000 US residents): life evaluation rose steadily with log income; emotional well-being also rose with log income but showed no further gain above an annual income of about $75,000; low income intensified the emotional pain of divorce, illness and being alone; education and income tracked life evaluation more, while health, caregiving, loneliness and smoking tracked daily emotions more. — *PNAS* 107(38):16489–16493, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ Killingsworth 2021: 1,725,994 experience-sampling reports from 33,391 employed US adults: both experienced and evaluative well-being rose linearly with log(income), the slope as steep for higher earners as for lower earners; no plateau above $75,000 and no income threshold where the two measures diverge. — *PNAS* 118(4):e2016976118, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016976118 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ The adversarial collaboration — Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2023 re-analysed Killingsworth's experience-sampling data: the flattening appeared only among the least happy people; among happier people happiness kept rising with log income, accelerating in the happiest group; complementary non-linearities add up to the overall linear-log pattern; the authors say Kahneman & Deaton overstated the flattening (their yes/no questions had a ceiling) and Killingsworth missed it. The abstract gives no percentages (the "least happy ≈ 20%" often quoted is NOT in it). — *PNAS* 120(10):e2208661120, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208661120 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] Dispute after 2023 (titles only — the letters have no abstracts): Rohrer & Wenz 2024, "Inappropriate causal assumptions underlie Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers' conclusions" (*PNAS* 121(46):e2313712121, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2313712121); Arslan 2024, "The miseries that remain may yet be alleviated" (*PNAS* 121(46):e2314207121, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2314207121); reply: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2024 (*PNAS*, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322160121). I could not read these; do not state their arguments beyond the Rohrer–Wenz title (the causal reading of income→happiness is challenged).
- [V] Other large analyses: Stevenson & Wolfers 2013 (multiple datasets, rich and poor countries and people within countries): well-being and income roughly log-linear, "does not diminish as incomes rise"; "if there is a satiation point, we are yet to reach it" (abstract; exact wording) — *Am Econ Rev* 103(3):598–604, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.103.3.598 (abstract via OpenAlex). Jebb et al. 2018 (Gallup World Poll, > 1.7 million people, spline regressions): global satiation points $95,000 for life evaluation and $60,000–$75,000 for emotional well-being, later in wealthier regions, and in some regions incomes beyond the satiation point went with LOWER life evaluations — *Nat Hum Behav* 2(1):33–38, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0277-0 (abstract via Europe PMC).
- [V] 2025–2026 follow-ups: Akkiraju & Rao 2025 (2.05 million US adults, Gallup Daily Poll 2008–2017): higher income went with greater life satisfaction but, above a household income of roughly $63,000, with MORE likelihood of stress (the same turning point in gender, race and political subgroups) — *Commun Psychol* 3:27, https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-025-00210-z . Newman, Gordon & Mendes 2025 (71,385 adults in > 10 countries; 29,567 with 3 weeks of daily ratings, 329,543 observations): higher income was associated with higher well-being, education more consistently with better health indicators; individual-level status predicted more than community-level — *Nat Hum Behav* 9(11):2299–2312, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02264-9 . Quispe-Torreblanca, De Neve & Brown 2026 (Gallup World Poll, 109 countries, > 90,000 people): in 80% of countries well-being was more strongly tied to within-country income RANK than to absolute income or relative deprivation, with rank effects smaller where social capital is high — *Nat Commun* 17:2962, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-69729-x (abstracts via Europe PMC). A multi-lab replication of Diener et al. (2010) by the Collaborative Replication and Education Project (CREP) has a 2026 data paper, "Data from CREP Replication of Does Money Buy Happiness, Diener et al. (2010)", whose abstract reports no sample size or result — https://doi.org/10.5334/jopd.156 .
- [V] Focusing illusion: Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz & Stone 2006 — higher-income people are more satisfied with their lives but barely happier moment-to-moment, are more tense, and do not spend more time in especially enjoyable activities; the income effect on life satisfaction also appears temporary; people overestimate the link because they focus partly on conventional achievements when judging lives (abstract, no numbers). — *Science* 312(5782):1908–1910, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1129688 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- STUDY "Happiness stops rising at about $75,000" (the popular reading of Kahneman & Deaton 2010) — original: 450,000+ Gallup-Healthways responses; emotional well-being flat above ≈ $75,000. - STUDY Well-being keeps rising with log income (no satiation) — original: Stevenson & Wolfers 2013; Killingsworth 2021.   GRADE B: the positive, roughly log-shaped association between income and both life evaluation and (for most people) emotional well-being is reproduced in large samples; its shape at high incomes, its causal reading (income vs rank vs reverse causation) and stress at high income are disputed. — Killingsworth 2021; Stevenson & Wolfers 2013; Jebb 2018; Newman 2025

- [V] Buying time, later tests: Whillans & West 2022 (Registered Report; six-week field experiment with working mothers in an urban slum in Kenya; vouchers for time-saving services vs unconditional cash transfers of equivalent value vs a neutral control; the sample size is not in the abstract): in contrast to the preregistered hypotheses, the time-saving, cash and control conditions led to similar increases in subjective well-being (Cohen's d from 0.25 to 0.85 during the treatment weeks and 0.21 to 0.36 at endline), with lower stress and less relationship conflict; exploratory analyses suggested the voucher and cash arms worked through different psychological pathways — *Sci Rep* 12:719, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-04352-y . ### 19-D. Positive psychology: what held up and what did not
- [def] Positive psychology 积极心理学: the movement to study strengths, positive experience and flourishing, launched with Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi's 2000 special-issue introduction, "Positive psychology: an introduction" (*Am Psychol* 55(1), https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.5 — only the title is confirmed; the abstract was not retrievable [~]).
- [V] ★ The positivity ratio 积极比率 (the "3:1" / "2.9013" story): Fredrickson & Losada 2005 predicted that a ratio of positive to negative affect "at or above 2.9" would characterise flourishing mental health; N = 188; a survey identified flourishing, then daily positive and negative emotions were reported for 28 days; the mean ratio was above 2.9 for those classified as flourishing and below it for the rest; the authors concluded general mathematical principles (nonlinear dynamics, building on Losada's team-performance model) may describe positive affect and flourishing. — *Am Psychol* 60(7):678–686, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.60.7.678 (abstract via Europe PMC)
- [V] ★ The takedown: Brown, Sokal & Friedman 2013 find no theoretical or empirical justification for modelling changes in human emotions with differential equations from fluid dynamics, report numerous conceptual and mathematical errors, and conclude that the claim of a "critical minimum positivity ratio of 2.9013" is unfounded (the abstract does not mention the Lorenz equations). Fredrickson's 2013 reply concedes that Losada's mathematical modelling is now questioned and sets it aside, while maintaining that computing positivity ratios is worthwhile and that higher ratios predict flourishing and other benefits "within bounds" (paraphrase of the abstract). A formal correction notice to the 2005 article also exists (publication type "Erratum", 2013; its text was not retrievable); OpenAlex does not flag the 2005 article as retracted — so "retracted" is NOT accurate; "the mathematical basis was withdrawn/unfounded" is. — Brown et al. 2013, *Am Psychol* 68(9):801–813, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032850 ; Fredrickson 2013, *Am Psychol* 68(9):814–822, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033584 ; correction notice https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034435 (abstracts via Europe PMC; notice type via OpenAlex)
- [V] ★ UPDATE (lead, 2026-10-14; read on the APA PsycNet record): the 2013 notice is "Correction to Fredrickson and Losada (2005)", *Am Psychol* 68(9):822, doi:10.1037/a0034435: "the modeling element of this article is formally withdrawn as invalid and, along with it, the model-based predictions about the particular positivity ratios of 2.9 and 11.6. Other elements of the article remain valid and are unaffected by this correction notice." It also says Losada "has chosen not to defend his nonlinear dynamic model" and that Fredrickson's reply conveys she had accepted the modeling as valid but has since come to question it. The article itself is NOT retracted (no retraction flag found). So the precise wording is: the authors formally withdrew the modelling and the model-based 2.9 and 11.6 ratio predictions; the article stays in the record.
- STUDY Positivity-ratio tipping point (flourishing needs a ratio of about 3:1; "2.9013") — original: Fredrickson & Losada 2005 (N = 188; 28 days of daily emotion reports; flourishing group above 2.9). ### 19-E. Meaning and kindness
- [V] Meaning in Life Questionnaire: Steger, Frazier, Oishi & Kaler 2006 — three studies provide evidence for the internal consistency, temporal stability, factor structure and validity of the MLQ, a 10-item measure of the presence of, and search for, meaning in life; a multitrait–multimethod matrix shows convergent and discriminant validity across time and informants against two other meaning scales; advantages claimed: no item overlap with distress, stable factor structure, brevity, measuring the search for meaning; the abstract gives no sample sizes. — *J Couns Psychol* 53(1), https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0167.53.1.80 (abstract via OpenAlex)

- [V] ★ Daily steps and mortality (the "10,000 steps" claim): Lee et al. 2019 (Women's Health Study; 16,741 women, mean age 72.0, mean 4.3 years of follow-up, 504 deaths; mean 5,499 steps/day): the abstract says the popular 10,000-steps-a-day goal has a limited scientific basis (paraphrase); adjusted hazard ratios by step quartile (median 2,718 / 4,363 / 5,905 / 8,442 steps) = 1.00 / 0.59 (0.47–0.75) / 0.54 (0.41–0.72) / 0.42 (0.30–0.60); mortality fell until about 7,500 steps/day and then levelled off; stepping intensity was not clearly related to mortality after accounting for total steps — *JAMA Intern Med* 179(8):1105–1112, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.0899 . ### 19-H. Myths and over-claims (lesson 19)
- [✗] "Money can't buy happiness": too crude — life evaluation and, for most people, experienced well-being rise with log income in large samples (grade B); but "happiness stops at $75,000" is also wrong (grade D, 19-B), and rank, stress at high income and causal direction are open.
- [✗] "Everyone returns to a fixed happiness set point": 24% of a large German panel changed significantly in life satisfaction over about 17 years; adaptation is partial and event-specific (Fujita & Diener 2005; FACTS_T3b §G, grade B for the treadmill).
- [✗] "Lottery winners are no happier": the 1978 study was 22 vs 22, cross-sectional; the Swedish pre-registered study finds durable life-satisfaction gains and no clear happiness gain (FACTS_T3b §G; D for the popular rewrite).
- [✗] "You need a 3:1 positivity ratio to flourish": D (19-D).
- [✗] "Keep a gratitude journal and your depression or anxiety goes away": no source I opened supports "cure"; best evidence is a small average effect on well-being, no better than other positive activities, and non-significant for anxiety versus alternative activities (Davis 2016; grades B/C, 19-D).
- [✗] "Expressive writing heals": average r = .075 and no significant long-term effect on depressive symptoms (19-D, grade C).
- [✗] "Smiling makes you happy": the pen-in-mouth result failed multi-lab replication (grade D); a small effect of overt, voluntary smiling remains (grade B) — FACTS_T3a §07 B4.
- [✗] "Do a dopamine fast to reset your dopamine": no support for the premise (grade D) — FACTS_T3b §10 F.
- [✗] "10,000 steps a day is the proven target": D; more steps go with lower mortality up to roughly 5,000–10,000 depending on age and outcome (19-G; the 2025 meta-analysis puts inflection points at about 5,000–7,000).
- [✗] "Exercise is proven to work as well as therapy or medication for depression": the best network meta-analysis rates the evidence low to very low certainty with one low-risk-of-bias study, and the effect falls from −0.95 to −0.67 in low-risk-of-bias trials (19-G, grade B for direction); a 2026 umbrella review finds most included reviews low or critically low quality, and everyday-life mood links are small (r ≈ .04–.08, Rehder 2026).
- [✗] "The Harvard study proves relationships cause long life": a correlational cohort of 724 selected men that its director says cannot be replicated; the specific "satisfaction at 50 beats cholesterol" claim has no identified peer-reviewed analysis (19-A, grade C).
- [~] "Loneliness is as deadly as smoking 15 cigarettes a day": the comparison is NOT in the abstracts I opened (Holt-Lunstad 2010: "comparable to well-established mortality risk factors"; Harvard Gazette: Waldinger compares loneliness with smoking and alcoholism) — do not give the cigarette number.
