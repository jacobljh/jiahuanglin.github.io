## State at entry
The program is `bbba` (the exact-stage program: street camera, light, scene; 46.8% miss; open pedestrians 39.9%, step-outs 80.4%). The street's close step-outs (a pedestrian who steps out from behind the van at z < 12 m: **call this case R**) occur in about **1 of 142 frames** (check with 10⁶ scene draws; Lesson 5 may have measured it; recompute, do not copy). A training set of N = 1,600 frames holds ≈ 11 of them.
The shuttle's job depends on exactly these: the closer the step-out, the less time to stop (Lesson 11 makes that quantitative; here it is only motivation).

## The one new idea
**A generator chooses where to spend its renders; any allocation other than the street's frequency changes what the learner believes is common, and the importance weight w = p/q (per stratum) is the exact repair — its price is the effective sample size ESS = (Σw)²/Σw² left for everything else.** Over- and under-sampling are one trade-off.

## The argument you must derive
1. **Feel it**: the `bbba` detector's miss rate on case R (a stratified real sample of ≥ 600 close step-outs, generated directly from the street's program: a lab privilege; the real *test set* holds only ~21 of them) vs open pedestrians at the same distances; and the **learning curve of the slice**: miss on R against the number of R examples in training (natural frequency, N from 400 to ~11,000, 1 seed per N: this is how the rare case "feels" more data). Say it plainly: the rare case is rare *in the data*, and that is the whole problem; the program, unlike the street, can choose.
2. **Options, ruled out by arithmetic**: (a) wait for more frames: examples ∝ N, so 80 examples of R need N ≈ 11,000 frames (7× the renders; and the common cases get 7× more data they did not need); (b) throw away the common cases: biases the training mix; (c) draw R on purpose with probability q instead of p: **R examples ∝ qN**. Option (c) is the program's privilege: the program can *construct* a scene of case R (sample from the street's scene law conditioned on R; show how, e.g. rejection at the scene-draw level costs microseconds and one render per kept frame), which a data collection campaign cannot.
3. **What oversampling does to the statistics, derived**: a stratified mixture proposal q(R) = q, q(not R) = 1 − q has density ratio w_R = p/q and w_other = (1 − p)/(1 − q); the weighted training loss is an unbiased estimate of the street's loss; the unweighted one is the loss of a world where R is q/p times as common. Distinguish carefully (and measure) **two different shifts**: (i) *label shift* (changing the pedestrian share of the training set, 50% here vs 2% in the logs): a global prior change, which moves scores by the log prior odds and leaves the *ranking* of frames alone — the exam already handles it by choosing the threshold at the deployment frequency (Saerens et al., 2002; King & Zeng, 2001 only as FACTS verifies); (ii) *a shift inside the positive class* (more R among pedestrians): it changes p(x | pedestrian), the learner trades common cases for R because the loss says R is common, and no threshold repairs that: the weights do.
4. **ESS**: ESS = (Σw)²/Σw², derive it as the sample size of an unweighted sample with the same variance of the weighted mean (Kish, per FACTS); for the two-stratum design ESS/N = 1 / (p²/q + (1 − p)²/(1 − q)) ≈ 1 − q for small p — oversampling is a *reallocation*: the share q of the renders goes to R, the rest keep N(1 − q) effective examples; state both counts (R examples qN, everything else N(1 − q)).
5. **Measure** (the headline experiment; N = 1,600, 3 seeds): q ∈ {natural ≈ 0.7%, 5%, 10%, 25%, 50%} × {unweighted, weighted}; for each: the rare-slice miss (≥ 600 stratified real close step-outs), the overall exam on the real test set (3,000 frames), the miss on *open* pedestrians, ESS, the number of R examples. Also the *same-examples-by-brute-force* control: the natural program at N ≈ 11,000 (one training, 1 seed, several minutes). Report what the lab shows: where the rare slice improves, what the overall exam pays, and whether the weights buy anything beyond calibration. (Do not assume the textbook result; the lab decides, and the lesson says what it decides.)
6. **How to choose q?** A principled allocation, derived: if the rare slice's miss counts c times as much as an ordinary pedestrian's (a cost the lab does not know until Lesson 11 prices it), the loss-weighted stratum sizes are n_s ∝ p_s · c_s · σ_s (Neyman-type allocation; verify the formula in your oracle by brute-force minimisation of the variance); show the measured trade-off curve and what a given c would pick.
7. **Exit, demonstrated**: the rare slice exam depends on **which pedestrians count**. Score the detectors of step 5 on the same stratified close-step-out sample under three rules — ≥ 1 visible pixel, ≥ 6 visible px² (the series' exam), ≥ 15% of the silhouette visible — and show the miss rate moves by tens of points (most close step-outs are tiny slivers while they appear). The program trained to find "any pixel" and the exam asking for "6 px²" are two different tasks (both rules are the program's and the exam's *definitions*); that is the baton. Do **not** go further into labels (Lesson 7): one table, one picture of three slivers.
