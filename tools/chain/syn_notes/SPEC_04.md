## State at entry
The program has the street's camera (use the **exact-stage program `aaba`**, i.e. `SV.hybrid(SV.SIM0, SV.REAL, {sensor:'b'})`, as Lesson 3 showed the calibrated camera is within a point of it; say so in one sentence) and **one** look: noon sun at 55° behind the camera, one brightness, white van, bright shirts, blue sky.
The street's logs show gains between 1.9 and 8, grey, warm and blue skies, coloured vans, any shirt, the sun anywhere. The exact-stage look repair (`abba`) is worth 4.2 points *after* the camera repair (and 8.5 on average over repair orders: Lesson 2); the label-free meter drops from 93.7% to 25.5%.

## The one new idea
**A nuisance is a factor the label does not depend on; invariance is bought by varying it over the range the world varies — and no wider.** Vary what the label ignores and the world varies; hold fixed what the label depends on (where and how visible the pedestrian is) and anything that would make the label *undeterminable from the pixels*.
The range comes from evidence: the pixels of known content (sky, road) in unlabelled frames, read through the calibrated camera (`SV.evidence.linear`).

## The argument you must derive
1. **Feel the entry problem**: 24 street log frames next to 24 program frames of the `aaba` program (both through the same linearisation); the gain histogram of Lesson 3 as a reminder (do not repeat its derivation). Pose: the label is *the same* for two pictures of one scene lit differently (show a pair re-rendered with another `look` seed: identical scene stream, identical label, different pixels).
2. **What a detector learns from one look is look-specific** (the shortcut): a small **cross-look matrix** (train on a point-like look A, test on looks A…D) computed with real trainings (N ≈ 400–800, 1 seed; the looks are named slices of the street's look space such as high sun / low sun / dim / overcast, your choice): diagonal good, off-diagonal bad. A mixed-look detector does well everywhere but pays on each (a number).
   This is *why* variation must be put into the data: the learner cannot be told that the sun is irrelevant, it can only be shown that it varies while the label does not.
3. **What may vary and what must not**, derived rather than listed: for each look parameter (sun elevation/azimuth, ambient fraction, overall brightness `lum`, ground albedo, sky type, pedestrian/van/pole palettes, window and stripe textures) ask two questions — *does the label depend on it?* (no, for every one of them: the label is a function of the scene's geometry and the visibility rule) and *does the street vary it?* (read it from probe pixels). Then show the **limit**: randomising a parameter so widely that pedestrians become undeterminable from the pixels (e.g. `lum` → 0: signal below the calibrated noise floor, the CNR argument of Lesson 3) turns a share of the training frames into **label noise**: the detector is asked to find a pedestrian no pixel shows.
   Measure that share (fraction of training pedestrians whose contrast-to-noise ratio is below your threshold) as the range widens.
4. **The range, from evidence a project has** (unlabelled frames + the exposure log): *probe pixels*, where the content is known (the top rows are sky unless a tree or van covers them — robust medians per frame; the bottom rows are road) and **ratios that exposure control cancels** (channel ratios in the sky identify its type; road/sky ratio is independent of `lum` and gain). With `SV.evidence.linear` the sky level gives `lum` × the sky colour;
   the road gives the product ground × (amb + (1 − amb)·sin(sun elevation)) × lum. **Say plainly which look parameters the probes can separate and which they can only constrain jointly** (the road level confounds ground albedo, ambient fraction and sun elevation): the program then randomises the parameters that matter *jointly* so that the probe statistics match; the principle is *match the distribution of what you can observe*.
   Estimate the ranges from n = 10 … 1,000 log frames (an error-bar-vs-evidence curve, computed live), and let the oracle compare them with the street's true ranges.
5. **How wide?** A sweep of the width of the randomisation (scale s of each range about its centre; s = 1 is the street's range): my probe at N = 1,600, seed 1 (miss / own-frame miss / alarm rate on logs): s = 0 (no variation, centred look) 65.1 / 5.5 / 52%; 0.5: 60.8 / 5.7 / 31%; 1: 62.1 / 6.3 / 27%; 1.5: 64.0 / 18.2 / 19%; 2: 64.3 / 18.1 / 18%; 3: 63.3 / 13.5 / 19% (one seed; **you run 3**).
   Expect a *shallow* U in the exam (≈ 2–3 points) and a **sharp rise of the program's own miss rate** once the range includes unrecoverable frames (my clipped lower limit on `lum` was 0.01): a stopping rule that needs **no real label** — widen until the program's own free-label error starts to climb. And the meter keeps improving while the exam does not: show both. Then the *cost of invariance* as a learning curve (s = 1 vs s = 3, N from ~200 to 1,600): the wide program needs more frames for the same exam.
   Which parameter groups carry the price (sun direction, brightness (`lum`, `amb`), colours (palettes, sky, ground), textures): a Shapley-style table over 4 groups (16 programs × 3 seeds = 48 trainings; skip if over budget and say so — then use add-one / drop-one).
6. **Price** the estimated-range program against the exact-stage `abba` (61.6%) and the one-look program `aaba` (65.8%), 3 seeds; label-free meter as well.
7. **Exit**: with `abba` the program draws one scene family: a repair of what exists is now worth `bbba − abba` = 14.8 points (vs `baaa − aaaa` = 5.5 when the pixels were wrong: the compounding of Lesson 2, seen from the other side). Show what SIM0's scene draws (SIM0.scene: pPed .5, pEmerge 0, z 8–16 m, one van, half the clutter): a histogram of pedestrian distance and visibility in program frames; the street's cases are not in it — but you cannot yet say *which* or *how many* (that is Lesson 5).
