## State at entry
A detector with an exam number (per frame, per pedestrian with ≥ 6 px² visible, at 10% false alarms). The shuttle has a speed, a deceleration and a latency, and a pedestrian who steps out from behind the van moves while the shuttle moves. The exam is **open loop** (it asks about a frame); the shuttle lives **closed loop** (what it does changes what it sees next, and the outcome is a collision or a stopping margin).

## The one new idea
**An action's consequence is the difference between two worlds that differ only in the action; a program can build both worlds from the same random numbers, a log of human driving cannot.** Logs confound (the driver's action depends on what the driver saw, which depends on the situation, which also decides the outcome) and lack positivity (some actions were never taken in some situations); an intervention in the program has neither problem, and sharing the random numbers between the branches (common random numbers) turns the difference of two noisy campaigns into one quiet one.

## The argument you must derive
1. **The episode, first** (the engine, derived from the exam's pieces): time step dt = 0.1 s; shuttle at speed v along +z (constant v₀ before braking), the van and clutter fixed in the world, the camera moving; the pedestrian steps out at lateral speed u from the van's shadow edge (use `SV.drawScene` once per episode, then move `scene.parts` per frame; positions only: the look is fixed, the sensor noise is a fresh stream per frame); one detector score per frame (`SV.score`); a **policy**: brake after m alarms in the last n frames (the alarm threshold is a policy parameter set from validation negatives at a per-frame false-alarm rate you choose and *justify*: at 10 Hz the exam's 10% means one false alarm per second), latency τ, deceleration a. Outcome: collision (the pedestrian is in the shuttle's path when the front passes) with the impact speed, or the **stopping margin** (distance left when the shuttle stops). Choose v₀, a, τ so that the closest step-outs (case R of Lesson 6) are marginal and the far ones are easy, and **say these are the lab's assumptions** (FACTS may give a typical emergency deceleration; use it only as verified, otherwise state your numbers as assumptions). Stopping distance = v₀τ + v₀²/(2a): derive it and compute the detection deadline for a step-out at each distance.
2. **Why logs cannot answer** (build it, then break it): a **human-driver log** generator: the driver sees the pedestrian when the visible width exceeds a perception threshold, reacts after a reaction time, brakes harder when it feels dangerous (close) — the *action depends on the situation*. Collect n episodes. The naive comparison "braked vs not braked" gives a collision difference of one sign; the **stratified** (by step-out distance) comparison gives the opposite sign in every stratum; show both numbers (confounding by situation: the hard situations are where drivers brake). Then **positivity**: for the closest step-outs the log has *no* episode without braking (the driver always brakes there), so no adjustment can estimate what would have happened: the estimate does not exist in the data, not even in principle. (FACTS: Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983 and Pearl as verified; keep the causal vocabulary minimal and correct; do not claim more than the lab shows.)
3. **The intervention**: for each situation (the same scene seed, pedestrian, noise streams), run *both* branches — brake per the policy / never brake — and take the per-situation difference; average over the street's step-out distribution (stratified by distance, with the real frequencies of Lesson 5/6). This is the quantity the logs could not give: the **effect of braking** by distance. State why the program can do this (determinism: Lesson 9; the state can be copied at the decision time) and what it cannot (whether the program's pedestrian moves like a real one: Lesson 12).
4. **Common random numbers**: compare detector A with detector B (or policy 1 with policy 2) on n episodes (a) in two independent campaigns and (b) on the same situations. Derive Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var Y − 2 Cov(X, Y) (FACTS: Glasserman & Yao 1992); measure the correlation of the per-episode outcomes, the variance ratio of the estimated difference (bootstrap), and the **number of episodes needed to resolve a stated difference at 95%** both ways; the ratio is the baton's ⟦k⟧. Show where CRN *fails* (branches that diverge after the decision: the post-decision frames use fresh noise; say how you handled it).
5. **The stopping margin vs the exam**: take two or more detectors with similar frame-level exam numbers (e.g. the natural and oversampled detectors of Lesson 6 if `l06_data.js` provides models, or two of the sixteen programs' models in `tables.js`) and compare their closed-loop collision rate and mean stopping margin on step-outs by distance. Report what the lab shows: where the frame exam ranks them differently from the closed loop; the mechanism (time to first detection, correlation of misses across consecutive frames, the debounce). Include the **correlation of consecutive-frame misses** (measure it) — the quantity Lesson 10's exit said no static set contains.
6. **Exit** (demonstrated): the program's closed-loop numbers (collision rate, margin) now exist and are precise (CRN) — and every one of them is a statement about the program's pedestrian, shuttle and street. Show the **same detector in two worlds** (the program's closed loop vs the street's closed loop, which only the lab can run: a lab privilege, flagged) and the difference between the two collision rates. That difference is what proof must cover; how many *real* episodes it takes to see it is the baton.

## What my probe found (use as a sanity check, not as numbers to quote; `tools/chain/syn_notes/proto/episode_probe.js`, ~0.07 s per episode)
Camera at the shuttle's front, `SV.drawScene(SV.REAL.scene, rng, {ped: true, mode: 'emerge', vis: 0})` once per episode (the pedestrian starts hidden at the van's shadow edge and walks left at u = 1.4 m/s), parts shifted by −s(t) per frame, `SV.sense` with the street's camera and a fresh stream per frame, `SV.score` of the table's `bbba` / `aaaa` model at its `thrReal`, alarm = score > threshold, brake after 2 alarms in the last 3 frames, collision when the front passes the pedestrian's z-plane while |x| < 1.25 m.
**At v₀ = 5 m/s, a = 3.5, τ = 0.3 s almost nothing collides** (the pedestrian has crossed before the shuttle arrives unless z₀ < 12 m: 3% without braking): the lab's *interesting* regime needs a faster shuttle. At **v₀ = 8 m/s, a = 3 m/s², τ = 0.4 s** (200 episodes, z₀ from `drawScene`'s emerge law ≈ 8–30 m): `bbba` detects in 97.5% of episodes, collides in **11%** with braking vs **47%** without (by z₀ bin: 7–12 m 50%, 12–17 m 23%, 17–24 m 4%, 24–40 m 0%); the naive `aaaa` detector (threshold at its own 10% false-alarm rate on street frames, 24.0) detects in 70% and collides in **27%**. So closed-loop outcomes separate detectors strongly and the dangerous situations are the close step-outs; you may choose other parameters but state them as assumptions and keep the regime where braking matters (collision without braking between 20% and 80%).
