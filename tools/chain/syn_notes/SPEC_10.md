## State at entry
The series so far *derives* a repaired program from evidence (Lessons 3–6: camera from a bench session and logs, light from logs, scene from a labelled sample) and shows that the repaired program trains a detector as good as one trained on the street (`bbba` 46.8% vs the street-trained 47.1%); Lesson 9 shows the numbers describe the program faithfully. What no check can say is whether the **estimates** are right: they came from finite evidence, and the street's own exam needs real labels (the series' exam uses 1,500 real validation frames for the threshold and 3,000 real test frames: **4,500 real labelled frames, more than every other real frame in the project**). A project has a small budget M of real labelled frames, unlimited logs and a camera to put on a bench.

## The one new idea
**A real frame is not worth a fixed number of synthetic frames; its worth depends on what it is spent on.** Spent on *calibrating* the program's few parameters, it reshapes every synthetic frame generated afterwards; spent on *fine-tuning* the detector's many weights, it is worth about itself; spent on *grading*, it is irreplaceable and it is the largest bill. The exchange rate is measured, not assumed.

## The argument you must derive
1. **Feel it**: the budget table of the project as it stands after Lesson 9: free (logs, a bench session), M labelled frames, and the exam's price in frames. Pose the three uses and what each one *outputs*: a number (grade), a better program (calibrate), a better detector (train).
2. **Grading, derived first because it is the unavoidable one**: the miss rate is a binomial proportion over the pedestrians that count; its 95% interval has half-width ≈ 1.96·√(p(1 − p)/n_ped); Wilson (1927) for small n (FACTS verifies the attributions). With 44% of test frames holding an exam pedestrian, tabulate the frames needed for ±5, ±3, ±2, ±1 points at p ≈ 0.47 and p ≈ 0.80. Then the harder question — telling a program that is 3 points better from noise (paired vs unpaired comparison on the same frames) — and the cost of the **threshold** (the 10%-false-alarm operating point is itself estimated from M₀ real pedestrian-free frames: its error moves the miss rate; measure how much with M₀ = 50, 150, 500, 1,500). Conclusion in frames: the series' exam costs thousands; every claim of this lesson is measured with the big lab sets and **priced** in what a project would have to spend to know it.
3. **Training on real frames** (three strategies, each a curve of real exam miss vs M, M ∈ {0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1,600}, 3 seeds, the fixed detector): (R) real only — train on the M frames; (F) fine-tune — the naive program's N = 1,600 synthetic frames **plus** the M real ones, real frames weighted by w (choose w by a rule stated and defended, e.g. total real weight = total synthetic weight, not tuned on the test set); (C) **calibrate** — rebuild the program from evidence with the estimators of Lessons 3–5 (camera from the bench session: free of labels; light from the 1,000 logs: free of labels; scene from the M labelled frames) and train on N = 1,600 frames of the calibrated program; (C+F) both. Report the four curves and where each crosses the others. Expected shape (the lab decides): C is already ≈ 62% at M = 0 because camera and light need no labels, and reaches ≈ 47–49% once M is a few hundred; R needs far more frames to get there; F helps early and saturates.
4. **The exchange rate, defined and measured**: for a target exam miss, the number of real frames each strategy needs; the ratio between strategies (how many real frames of R does one real frame of C replace?); and the worth of a *synthetic* frame in real frames: flat zero for the naive program (Lesson 1's wall), about one for the calibrated program (it matches the street: the N = 1,600 detector equals the one trained on 1,600 real frames) — compute both from your curves. Then the leverage of calibration: M real frames of C buy a program that makes *unlimited* frames.
5. **The best use of M, as a rule with a break-even**: at what M does each use become the best; the calibrate-then-fine-tune order; what to do with the *last* real frames (hold them out for grading: the order of spending is *calibrate, then grade, then fine-tune*, or whatever your curves say). A table, not a slogan: budget (frames) → recommended split → measured exam miss and the grading CI it affords.
6. **Road not taken · learn a refiner**: translate synthetic pixels to look real with a learned model (verified descriptions only: SimGAN, CyCADA per FACTS), why it is tempting (no physical model needed), and what it cannot give: nothing checks that the *label* survived the translation without real labels (the self-regularisation term is a prior, not a check). One measured toy if you can afford it: a pixel-wise "refiner" fitted to match real frame statistics (e.g., a per-channel gain and noise fit from logs, which is Lessons 3–4 in disguise) vs the physical calibration: same exam. Otherwise prose only; say which.
7. **Exit, demonstrated** (see the baton note): the arithmetic of detection per frame vs per episode; one number: if the shuttle has k frames before it must brake, the probability of no detection is 0.47ᵏ with independent misses and 0.47 with perfectly correlated ones; the exam cannot say which, because the test set holds single frames.
