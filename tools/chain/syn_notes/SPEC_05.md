## The one new idea
**A case is a cell of the situations the exam asks about (here: distance × how much of the pedestrian is visible); a learner can only be right where its data has mass, so count the street's mass per cell with a small labelled sample and the ground plane, compare it with the program's own (exactly known) mass, and put the program's probability where the street's is.** Coverage = the share of the street's pedestrians whose case the program ever draws.

## The argument you must derive
1. **Feel it**: a table *distance bin × (open / stepping out)* of the miss rate of the detector trained on the program (`abba`) vs the detector trained on the street (`bbba`) — computed on the real test set with lab truth (flag it as a lab privilege) — and the program's own mass in each cell. The detector fails exactly where its training data has no mass: a learner does not extrapolate by what we mean (Lesson 1's bias, now located). Say that a *case missing from the data cannot be repaired downstream* (Lesson 2's road-not-taken, now cashed).
2. **A ruler for cases.** What can a project see? A **labelled sample**: frames in which a labeller outlined every pedestrian (and every vehicle: say what the labelling spec costs per frame, and how many labelled frames a project can afford; Lesson 10 returns to this). From masks alone derive (a) **distance**: with the camera at height hc and the horizon on row V0, a ground point at row v is at z = f·hc/(v − V0): the mask's **foot row** gives z. Derive the law; derive its **error**: a half-pixel error in the foot row is Δz/z = z·Δv/(f·hc), i.e. 4% at 8 m and 10% at 24 m: the ruler loses resolution linearly with distance (compute and tabulate) — and say what that means for the half-pixel conventions of Lesson 7;
   (b) **how much of the pedestrian is visible**: visible width of the mask against the width a standing pedestrian has at that distance (a typical shoulder width 2r ≈ 0.5 m gives w = f·2r/z); a hidden pedestrian shows less. Define the case table from (a) and (b) using only labelled masks, and **apply exactly the same ruler to the program's own frames** (its masks are free) — then compare, cell by cell, the street's frequency p(case) and the program's q(case).
3. **How many labelled frames does a ruler need?** Estimation error of the coverage, of the step-out share and of the distance range against the number of labelled frames (25 … 800: draw repeatedly with different seeds, live in the page; 3,000 test frames hold the truth). Derive the arithmetic: a case with per-frame probability p is absent from n frames with probability (1 − p)ⁿ, and about 3/p pedestrians are needed to see it once with 95% confidence (FACTS: check the rule-of-three attribution before you name it). Apply to the cases you found: common cases need a few dozen frames, the **rarest case you can name needs hundreds** — this is the entry to Lesson 6.
4. **The label-free meter is blind to pedestrian cases** (it saw the *background* of the scene, not the people): run the five components of the scene stage one at a time on top of `abba` — distance range, step-out, body size, van, clutter — and report both the exam gain and the meter. My earlier measurements (3 seeds, N = 1,600, `abbb` base 61.6%; yours must be recomputed in your builder; the numbers are in `tools/chain/syn_notes/proto/results/scene.jsonl` for comparison):
   distance range −5.0 points (meter unchanged, ≈ 25%), step-out −2.7 (meter unchanged), distance + step-out together −9.9, clutter −1.4 (meter 25% → 15%), van ≈ 0 (meter 25% → 15%), body size −0.4; all five = `bbba` 46.8%. The exam sees the *pedestrian* cases, the meter sees the *background*, and neither alone is enough. **The two repairs compound again** (5.0 + 2.7 < 9.9), the third time in the series.
5. **Repair from the evidence and price it.** Set the program's pedestrian distance range and step-out probability from the ruler (and, if you have room, the background from the meter: tune clutter/van until the unlabelled-frame alarm rate stops falling; the exam does not move for these, say so), train 3 seeds at each evidence amount (n labelled frames = 50, 200, 800), compare with the exact-stage `bbba` and with `abba`. Expected: ≈ 47%, within about a point of `bbba`, once n is a few hundred; with a small n the range is mis-set (a distance range cut short by one nearest outlier): show it.
6. **Exit** (demonstrated with numbers): the repaired program draws every *kind* of case — but a kind is not a frequency. Compute on the street's program (lab privilege: simulate it 10⁶ scene draws, no rendering) how often the case that matters most for stopping occurs: **a step-out at z < 12 m: about 1 frame in 142**. In 1,600 training frames that is ≈ 11 examples. Measure the `bbba`-detector's miss rate on a **stratified real sample** of close step-outs (generate them directly: stratified sampling from the street's program, flagged as a lab privilege; use ≥ 400 so the number is stable) against open pedestrians at the same distance, and show how few of them the training set holds. That is the baton.
