## State at entry
Lesson 2 found the label stage worth **−0.1 points** (`aaab` reproduces `aaaa` to the last digit; `bbba` 46.8 vs `bbbb` 47.1) and called it "a stage whose cost is not paid in this exam". Lesson 6's last table showed that the same detectors' miss rate on the close step-outs moves by tens of points with the visibility rule. Both are true, and they are one fact seen twice: **a label stage changes the *number you report* far more than the *detector you train*.** This lesson explains why, measures it clause by clause, and writes the definitions down as a contract.
The program's label rule (`SV.SIM0.label = {kvis: 1}`: any pixel counts) and the street's (`{kvis: 1, rel: 0.15}`: at least 15% of the silhouette visible) both decide the frame label `y` (`SV.labels`); the **exam** is a third definition (`SV.EXAM.minArea = 6`: only pedestrians with ≥ 6 visible px² count in the miss rate). `sample()` returns `area` (visible px²) and `full` (silhouette px²).

## The one new idea
**A label is a definition — a function of the scene and the renderer's outputs, with a clause for every question the picture does not answer (which pixels, how much of them, how far, aligned how, measured at what resolution) — and a reported number is comparable across two pipelines only if their definitions agree clause by clause.** Two places the definition enters, which must not be confused: the **training label** (what the detector is taught) and the **scoring rule** (what counts as a hit or a miss). The lesson measures each clause's effect on both.

## The argument you must derive
1. **Feel it**: one detector, three scoring rules, three miss rates (use the stored scores of the `bbba` model on the real test set; the numbers of Lesson 6's exit table are the entry). Then Lesson 2's puzzle: why did the label stage move *no detector*? Because the stage defines `y` only when the pedestrian is partly hidden — a pedestrian who is fully visible has the same label under every rule — and the program's *own* scene (SIM0's, or even the repaired one for most frames) hides few pedestrians. Show the share of frames whose label is rule-dependent (a number; compute it for SIM0's scene and the street's scene).
2. **The clauses**, derived from "what a pixel mask of a pedestrian answers and what it leaves open":
   (a) *which pixels* — **modal** (the visible mask) or **amodal** (the whole silhouette, including the part behind the van). Teaching target: a detector can only fire where pixels show evidence; an amodal training target labels cells that show only van. Retrain with amodal cell labels and measure (3 seeds): what the detector learns, where its false alarms go (the van's edge) and the exam. Do not assume the sign; the lab decides. (FACTS: Zhu et al. 2017 / KINS only as verified.)
   (b) *how much counts* — the visibility rule: any pixel, ≥ k px², ≥ r × silhouette (r = 0.15 the street's, a "reasonable"-style 0.65 as FACTS allows for Caltech), and **who sets it**: the training label's rule vs the exam's. A table: for each rule the number of exam pedestrians and the miss rate of one detector; the *rank of two detectors* under different rules (look for a pair whose order flips: Lesson 6's natural vs oversampled detectors are a good candidate if their stored scores are available in `l06_data.js`; otherwise the 16-program models in `tables.js`). A flip is the sharpest form of "the exam is a definition".
   (c) *how far* — depth (along the axis) vs range (along the ray): derive range = depth·√(1 + tan²φ + ρ²); 1.155 at the field edge; compute the share of exam pedestrians whose distance **bin** changes when range is used instead of depth (bin edges at 8, 12, 16, 20 m) and what that does to a case table (Lesson 5's ruler measures depth).
   (d) *aligned how* — the half-pixel: pixel centres at +0.5 (this lab, graphics APIs) vs at integers (calibration toolboxes: FACTS has the verified wording). A mask built under the wrong convention is shifted by half a pixel in both axes. Measure: mean mask IoU of exam pedestrians between the two conventions (thin objects: it is far from 1), the foot-row distance error (4%–10%: Lesson 5) and the effect of *training* on half-pixel-shifted labels (1–3 seeds).
   (e) *measured at what resolution* — the coverage that decides a label is an area integral: 2×2 rays per pixel vs 8×8: the sliver of Lesson 1 measured 15 vs 19 px². The fraction of exam pedestrians whose ≥ 6 px² decision flips between the two (a number), and a note that the flip is a *threshold* effect (it matters only near the rule's boundary).
   (f) *which instant* — one paragraph, no experiment: a label belongs to a moment; the exposure window and the label timestamp are two clocks (Lesson 3's motion formula says when it matters); the lab's frame has one.
3. **Which clauses bite where**: a 2×2 table — rows: the clauses; columns: *changes what is learned* (retrained detectors), *changes what is reported* (same detector, other rule) — filled with your measured numbers. Expected shape (verify): reporting effects are large and cheap to measure; learning effects are small except where the label contradicts the pixels (amodal, misalignment).
4. **The contract**: write the definitions as a checkable specification (a short table: clause / the lab's setting / how it is tested / what a violation looks like), and show the *test* for two clauses as code (the core-JS listing): e.g. a conformance check that renders an analytic sliver of known area and verifies the label rule, and one that renders a pedestrian at a known distance and verifies depth vs range. Lesson 8 will run the same tests against a renderer nobody here wrote.
5. **Exit, demonstrated**: the clauses are all settled in `street.js`, where one person chose every convention. List the settings a *production* renderer has for the same questions (the Blender conventions verified in FACTS L08: axes, lens/sensor fit, pixel filter, colour management, depth pass, index mask) and show, with the lab's own pixel filter as a worked example, that one such default (a pixel filter wider than a pixel) changes an *edge profile* and therefore the area of every sliver: compute the extra blur σ and the change in ≥ 6 px² decisions for an exam pedestrian. Do not run Blender (Lesson 8 does).

## Evidence you implement (private `l07_labels.js`, namespace `SV.l07`)
Label variants as functions of a sample (`cellLabels(sample, 'modal' | 'amodal' | {shift: [dx, dy]})`, `scoreRule(sample, rule)`), a **retraining helper that takes alternative cell labels** (write your own `train` variant on top of the public pieces `SV.featureMap`, `SV.fitHead`; do not change `SV.train`), a depth/range conversion, a coverage-resolution re-render (`SV.render(scene, look, {SSh: 8, SSv: 8})`), and the stored-score exam: `SV.L07.scores` = per-frame score, `area`, `full`, `y`, `mode`, true `z` for the real test set under 2–3 models, so that the page can recompute the exam under any rule instantly.
Labels here are the *program's own* (free), so you may use `scene`, `cov` and `id` of program frames; for real frames the page may use only what Lesson 5's labelled sample shows (visible masks).

## Measurements and the builder (≤ 40 trainings)
Retrain: modal (= the table's `bbba` cells), amodal, shifted by 0.5 px, shifted by 1 px, label rule `rel` ∈ {0, 0.15, 0.5} for the training label; 3 seeds each (≈ 15–20 trainings, N = 1,600, program `bbba`). Everything else is scoring (no training): store the per-frame scores of 2–3 models on the 3,000 real test frames (≈ 3 floats per frame) in `l07_data.js`.
