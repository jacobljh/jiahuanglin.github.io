## 0 · Wrong-number traps and DO-NOT-WRITE list

Read this first. "USE THIS WORDING" bullets resolve a DISCREPANCY (a commonly quoted number/wording that is wrong or ambiguous, or two sources that disagree). Everything not listed here and tagged [VERIFIED] in a lesson section is safe to cite by author + year + venue (no URLs in the lessons). Items tagged [UNVERIFIED] or [LIKELY] below need the hedge given.

**Sensor / camera (L03)**
- DO NOT WRITE: "EMVA 1288 release 3.1 (2012)" (it is 2016) or "release 4.0 of 22 June 2021" (a PDF-metadata date an automatic summary gave me; the cover says 16 June 2021). USE THIS WORDING: "EMVA Standard 1288, Release 4.0 Linear, 16 June 2021 (supersedes Release 3.1, in force 30 Dec 2016)".
- DO NOT WRITE: "saturation capacity = full-well capacity". USE THIS WORDING: "saturation capacity (electrons at the top of the usable range; the standard stresses it is normally below the physical full well because the output clips at 2^k − 1)".
- DO NOT WRITE: "the photon-transfer slope is the read noise". USE: slope = conversion gain K (DN per electron); intercept = K^2·sigma_d^2 + 1/12 DN^2 (read noise plus quantisation). Foi et al.'s a, b are the same line.
- DO NOT WRITE: "sRGB is a gamma of 2.2" (flat statement). USE: "sRGB is a linear toe (12.92·L below 0.0031308) plus a 2.4-exponent curve with offset 0.055; it approximates a 2.2 power law in mid and high tones only (largest linear-light gap vs V^2.2 is 0.0085 at V = 0.75; at V = 0.1 the sRGB value is 1.59x larger)". Thresholds: 0.04045 on the code side, 0.0031308 on the linear side; never 0.03928 (draft value).
- DO NOT WRITE: "Rose criterion: SNR of 5 gives 95% detection" or "exactly 5". USE: "Rose's rule of thumb (1948; book 1973): a feature is reliably seen when its signal is about five times the noise standard deviation (some texts say 4-5)". Status [LIKELY]; primary papers paywalled.
- DO NOT WRITE: "10-90% edge width = 2.35 sigma" (that is FWHM, 2.3548 sigma). USE: 10-90% width of a Gaussian blur = 2.563 sigma (= 2·1.2816), computed.

**Randomisation papers (L04)**
- DO NOT WRITE: "Tobin et al. used no real data at all" without the footnote. USE: "trained only on simulated images (no real task images); most runs initialised the backbone from ImageNet-pretrained VGG-16, and random initialisation did about as well with enough data. Average error 1.5 cm on 480 real webcam images (60 per object); 0.3-0.5 cm in simulation."
- DO NOT WRITE: "domain randomisation beats real data" for Tremblay 2018. USE: "synthetic-only was competitive with the hand-built Virtual KITTI (AP@0.5 on KITTI easy: Faster R-CNN 78.1 vs 79.7; R-FCN 71.5 vs 64.6; SSD 46.3 vs 36.1); pre-training on 100K randomised images and fine-tuning on all 6,000 real images gave 98.5 AP, 2.1 above real-only."
- DO NOT WRITE: "Meta-Sim / Learning to Simulate / SimOpt all match the real distribution". USE: SimOpt matches real trajectories; Learning to Simulate maximises validation accuracy of the task model; Meta-Sim does distribution matching plus a task meta-objective.
- DO NOT WRITE: "data augmentation guarantees invariance" (Chen, Dobriban & Lee 2020 prove an averaging/variance-reduction statement for transformations that leave the data distribution approximately invariant).

**Benchmarks and coverage (L05)**

USE: "near 80 px or more, medium 30-80, far under 30; 'reasonable' = at least 50 px tall and at most 35% occluded (no or partial occlusion); log-average miss rate over nine FPPI points evenly spaced in log space in [1e-2, 1e0]; matching uses the full box, not the visible box".

**Rare case (L06)**
- DO NOT WRITE: "Liu (1996) defines the effective sample size". USE: "Kish (1965) design effect 1 + CV^2 of the weights; n_eff = (sum w)^2 / sum w^2; used for importance sampling following Kong (1992); see Owen, ch. 9, eq. 9.13/9.15. It is a heuristic diagnostic."

- DO NOT WRITE: "Kahn & Harris (1951) invented splitting" without a hedge [LIKELY, not opened]. USE "goes back to Kahn and Harris (1951)" or omit.

USE: one retraining round on false positives found in 1,218 person-free photos (initial 12,180 random patches); about a 5% improvement at 1e-4 FPPW; more rounds made little difference. ## L01-02 · Pipeline/prices

**a. Shapley value** [VERIFIED] — L. S. Shapley, "A value for n-person games", in H. W. Kuhn & A. W. Tucker (eds.), *Contributions to the Theory of Games, Vol. II* (Annals of Mathematics Studies 28), Princeton University Press, 1953, pp. 307-317.
 Player i's value = the average, over all orderings of the players, of its marginal contribution v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S), S = players already present. Efficiency: the values sum to v(N) (with v(∅) = 0; so in general to v(N) − v(∅)). The four axioms are efficiency, symmetry, additivity, null player.
 Traps: say "average marginal contribution over all orderings", not "over all subsets" without weights (the subset form carries the weights |S|!(n−|S|−1)!/n!). Do not attribute SHAP (Lundberg & Lee 2017) to Shapley 1953.

**b. "Reality gap"** [VERIFIED, with a wording caveat] — N. Jakobi, P. Husbands, I. Harvey, "Noise and the reality gap: the use of simulation in evolutionary robotics", in *Advances in Artificial Life* (ECAL 1995), Lecture Notes in Artificial Intelligence 929, Springer, 1995 (DOI 10.1007/3-540-59496-5_337).
 What they did: simulated a two-wheeled Khepera (IR + ambient-light sensors; empirically fitted sensor/motor models); evolved controllers for obstacle avoidance and light seeking at three simulation-noise levels (zero, observed, double observed); downloaded them to the real robot; the sim/real behavioural correspondence depended on the noise level, very good with realistic noise.
 Argument: simulations must be built from measured data and validated, and noise must be modelled; extra noise blurs the simulation's inevitable deficiencies (the paper calls this an envelope-of-noise).
 Traps: the phrase "reality gap" appears in the TITLE; the body never defines it (I read the full text). So write "the sim-to-real 'reality gap' (Jakobi, Husbands & Harvey 1995)" and do NOT write "they defined the reality gap as ...". The "radical envelope-of-noise hypothesis" is the later Jakobi 1997 paper (Adaptive Behavior 6(2)) — [UNVERIFIED by me]; the 1995 paper only says "envelope-of-noise". Their scoring of correspondence was subjective (the authors marked it).

## L03 · The camera as a measurement

**a. Photon transfer and EMVA 1288** [VERIFIED, read the standard's text] — EMVA Standard 1288 "Standard for Characterization of Image Sensors and Cameras". Releases (reference list of the 4.0 document): A2.01 (2007), 3.0 (2010), 3.1 (2016; "in force" from 30 Dec 2016), and **Release 4.0 Linear, dated 16 June 2021 (cover page)**; Release 4 is split into two documents, Linear (this one, with the photon transfer curve) and General (no linear-response assumption, no photon transfer curve; I did not read it or check its date).
 Model (Release 4.0 Linear): mean DN  mu_y = mu_y,dark + K·mu_e, K = overall system gain in DN/e-. Shot noise is Poisson: var of electrons = mean electrons. Read/amplifier noise is signal-independent with variance sigma_d^2 (electrons^2). ADC quantisation adds sigma_q^2 = 1/12 DN^2.
 Photon-transfer relation (their Eq. 15): sigma_y^2 = K^2·sigma_d^2 + sigma_q^2 + K·(mu_y − mu_y,dark). Slope vs (mu_y − mu_y,dark) = K; offset = K^2·sigma_d^2 + 1/12 (so dark noise in electrons = sqrt(offset − 1/12)/K). The standard names this the photon transfer method and cites Janesick et al. 1985 and Janesick 2007.
 Saturation: saturation capacity mu_e,sat = eta·mu_p,sat = electrons at the saturation exposure (the top of the usable range, where clipping is still negligible). The standard says explicitly it "must not be confused with the full-well capacity" and is normally LOWER, because the signal clips at 2^k − 1 before the pixel physically saturates. Max SNR = sqrt(mu_e,sat) (Eq. 55).
 Two-image trick (Eq. 18): take two images at the same exposure; sigma_y^2 = (1/(2NM))·sum over pixels of [(y0 − mu0) − (y1 − mu1)]^2. The difference cancels the fixed pattern (spatial nonuniformity), which is the same in both frames; divide by 2 because the variance of a difference is the sum of two variances. (Release 4 added the subtraction of the frame means; earlier releases did not.)
 Procedure: at least 50 about equally spaced exposures from dark to saturation (9 allowed for production), two images at each, plus two dark images at each exposure time used.
 **Janesick, *Photon Transfer: DN → λ*, SPIE Press, Bellingham WA, 2007 (doi 10.1117/3.725073)** [VERIFIED]; earliest cited method paper: Janesick, Klaasen, Elliott 1985, Proc. SPIE vol. 570 (as cited by EMVA).
 Traps: it is "K" (DN per electron) in EMVA and "conversion gain" (e-/DN or µV/e-) in other texts — define the unit. Do not call saturation capacity "full well". Release 3.1 is 2016 (in force 30 Dec 2016), Release 3.0 is 2010.

**b. Poissonian-Gaussian noise model** [VERIFIED] — A. Foi, M. Trimeche, V. Katkovnik, K. Egiazarian, "Practical Poissonian-Gaussian noise modeling and fitting for single-image raw-data", *IEEE Trans. Image Processing* 17(10):1737-1754, 2008.
 Model: observed z(x) = y(x) + sigma(y(x))·xi(x), with signal-dependent (Poissonian) plus signal-independent (Gaussian) parts; var z = a·y + b, an affine function of the noise-free mean y (a >0 scales the Poisson part, b >= 0 is the Gaussian variance). They fit a and b from a single raw image (local mean/std estimates over regions assumed flat, then a global maximum-likelihood fit); the paper also treats clipped (over/under-exposed) raw data [clipping detail from memory, LIKELY].
 Bridge (DERIVED): a <-> K (DN/e-); b <-> K^2·sigma_d^2 + sigma_q^2. So "fit variance = a·mean + b" IS the photon-transfer line.
 Trap: Foi's "a" is not the quantum efficiency; both a and b are in the units of the raw data (DN).

**c. sRGB transfer function** [VERIFIED; constants seen on Wikipedia + W3C sRGB page + checked for continuity numerically] — IEC 61966-2-1:1999 (Amendment 1 in 2003); W3C-hosted proposal 'A Standard Default Color Space for the Internet - sRGB' (Stokes et al., 1996; author list from memory).
 Decode (display code V in 0..1 -> linear light L): L = V/12.92 if V <= 0.04045; else L = ((V + 0.055)/1.055)^2.4.
 Encode (L -> V): V = 12.92·L if L <= 0.0031308; else V = 1.055·L^(1/2.4) − 0.055.
 Checked: 12.92·0.0031308 = 0.040450 and ((0.04045+0.055)/1.055)^2.4 = 0.0031308: the two pieces meet. W3C text: the curve "closely fit[s] a straightforward gamma 2.2 curve" with a slight offset (invertible in integer math).
 My numbers (DERIVED, computed): vs a pure V^2.2, the largest absolute gap in linear light is 0.0085 (at V = 0.75); at V = 0.1 sRGB gives 0.0100 vs 0.0063 for 2.2 (x1.59); at V = 0.01 the ratio is x19. A single-exponent least-squares fit gives 2.18 on V in [0.2,1], 2.07 on [0.05,1], 1.95 on [0.01,1]. So "about 2.2" is right in the mid/high tones and wrong in the shadows, where the real curve is linear.
 Trap: the exponent in the formula is 2.4, not 2.2; 0.04045 (code side) vs 0.0031308 (linear side) are DIFFERENT thresholds; the older 0.03928 is the draft value (do not use). 8-bit code 118 ~ 18% linear grey (0.181).

**d. Rose criterion** [LIKELY — the standard secondary phrasing is consistent, the primary papers are paywalled] — Albert Rose, "The sensitivity performance of the human eye on an absolute scale", *J. Opt. Soc. Am.* 38(2):196-208, 1948 (earlier 1946); book *Vision: Human and Electronic*, Plenum Press, New York, 1973.
 Usual phrasing: a feature is reliably (near-100%) detected when its signal is about 5 times the noise standard deviation, i.e. SNR >= ~5 ("4-5" appears in some texts; the derivation suggests ~4 but 5 fits observers better).
 Photon-noise form used in teaching (DERIVED): a flat object of A pixels and contrast C = dS/S on a background of N photoelectrons per pixel has SNR = C·sqrt(A·N); Rose's rule asks for SNR ~ 5.
 Trap: it is a rule of thumb for an ideal-observer-ish photon-limited case, not a law; say "about 5", never "exactly 5" and never 95%.

**e. Motion blur extent** [DERIVED] — blur length (px) = image speed (px/s) × exposure time. For a point at depth Z moving laterally (perpendicular to the optical axis) at speed v, pinhole image speed = f_px·v/Z, f_px = focal length in pixels. For motion along the optical axis the image speed of a point at image radius r from the principal point is r·v_z/Z (zero at the centre). A rolling shutter adds a skew = image speed × the readout time between first and last row.
 Trap: "exposure time" is the integration time, not the frame period.

**f. Gaussian PSF, edge spread, 10-90 width** [DERIVED + numerically checked] — for a Gaussian line-spread function of std sigma the edge spread function is ESF(x) = Phi(x/sigma) = 0.5·[1 + erf(x/(sigma·sqrt 2))].
 10%-90% rise distance = 2·Phi^-1(0.9)·sigma = 2·1.28155·sigma = **2.5631·sigma**, so the constant 2.563 is correct (computed). FWHM of the LSF is 2.3548·sigma (a different number: do not mix them).
 Reading sigma off a measured edge: sigma = (10-90 width)/2.563.

## L04 · Light and randomisation

**a. Tobin et al. 2017** [VERIFIED, read the paper] — J. Tobin, R. Fong, A. Ray, J. Schneider, W. Zaremba, P. Abbeel, "Domain randomization for transferring deep neural networks from simulation to the real world", IROS 2017 (arXiv 1703.06907).
 Task: localise the (x, y, z) position of one of 8 geometric objects on a table from one uncalibrated monocular webcam image (224 x 224), VGG-16-style CNN, L2 loss, MuJoCo renderer; table height fixed => effectively 2-D.
 Randomised per sample: number and shape of distractors (0 to 10); position and texture of all objects on the table; textures of table, floor, skybox, robot; camera position/orientation/field of view (position in a 10 x 5 x 10 cm box, viewing angle offset up to 0.1 rad); number of lights (position, orientation, specular); type and amount of noise added to images. Textures: random RGB, gradient between two random RGBs, or checker of two random RGBs (not photorealistic).
 Headline: "localize objects to within 1.5 cm (on average)" on 480 REAL webcam images (60 per object: 20 alone, 20 with distractors, 20 partially occluded); e.g. cone 1.3 ± 1.1 / 1.5 ± 1.0 / 1.4 ± 0.6 cm; simulated-training error 0.3-0.5 cm (they overfit the simulation).
 Ablations: performance is poor with fewer than 1,000 distinct random textures (at 10,000 images); improves up to about 50,000 images; random camera and distractors matter; noise matters little.
 Real data: NO real images of the task in training. But careful: the abstract says the transfer is without pre-training on real images, while most runs initialised the conv layers from ImageNet-pretrained VGG; they found random initialisation about as good with enough data. Say: no real task images were used; an ImageNet-initialised backbone was used in most runs.

**b. Tremblay et al. 2018** [VERIFIED, read the paper] — J. Tremblay, A. Prakash, D. Acuna, M. Brophy, V. Jampani, C. Anil, T. To, E. Cameracci, S. Boochoon, S. Birchfield (NVIDIA), "Training deep networks with synthetic data: bridging the reality gap by domain randomization", CVPR 2018 Workshops (Workshop on Autonomous Driving), arXiv 1804.06516.
 Task: 2-D bounding-box detection of cars, evaluated on KITTI (500 random KITTI images; AP at IoU 0.5; only objects with box height > 40 px and truncation < 0.15, i.e. KITTI "easy").
 Randomised (Unreal Engine 4 plug-in, 1200 x 400 images): number/type of cars (36 sedan/hatchback models), number/type/colour/scale of distractors (cones, pyramids, spheres, pedestrians, trees ...), textures of cars and a background photo (Flickr 8K), camera azimuth 0-360 deg and elevation 5-30 deg, camera pan/tilt/roll within ±30 deg, 1-12 point lights plus an ambient planar light, ground-plane visibility; new "flying distractors". 100K images (<= 14 cars each).
 Results (Table 1, AP@0.5, real KITTI subset): Faster R-CNN 78.1 (DR) vs 79.7 (Virtual KITTI, 2.5K images); R-FCN 71.5 vs 64.6; SSD 46.3 vs 36.1. Fine-tuned on all 6000 real images: DR-pretrained 98.5 AP = +2.1 over real-only (96.4 implied) and +1.6 over Virtual-KITTI-pretrained.
 In words: synthetic-only is competitive with a hand-built photoreal-ish VKITTI, and synthetic pre-training + real fine-tuning beat real-only. Traps: the network "has never seen a real image (beyond pretraining of the early layers on ImageNet)"; synthetic-only was worse at high recall (distribution mismatch, e.g. parked-car context ignored); results are for the "easy" subset only.

**c. Structured Domain Randomization** [VERIFIED at abstract level] — A. Prakash, S. Boochoon, M. Brophy, D. Acuna, E. Cameracci, G. State, O. Shapira, S. Birchfield, "Structured domain randomization: bridging the reality gap by context-aware synthetic data", ICRA 2019 (arXiv 1810.10093).
 One line: instead of placing objects uniformly at random, sample the scene from problem-specific probability distributions (road, lanes, cars on lanes, etc.) so that context/structure is preserved while appearance is still randomised; on KITTI car detection it beat plain domain randomisation, and synthetic + real exceeded real alone.

**d. Adapting the randomisation from real data** [VERIFIED at abstract level]
 - Chebotar, Handa, Makoviychuk, Macklin, Issac, Ratliff, Fox, "Closing the sim-to-real loop: adapting simulation randomization with real world experience", ICRA 2019 (pp. 8973-8979; arXiv 1810.05687). Optimises the distribution of simulation parameters so that simulated roll-outs match a few real-world roll-outs (a discrepancy between simulated and real trajectories/observations); policy training and distribution update are interleaved. Shown on swing-peg-in-hole and cabinet opening.
 - Ruiz, Schulter, Chandraker, "Learning to simulate", ICLR 2019 (arXiv 1810.02513). Optimises simulator parameters by policy-gradient RL, with the reward = validation accuracy of a task model trained on the generated data; the stated goal is maximising task accuracy rather than mimicking the real data distribution.
 - Kar, Prakash, Liu, Cameracci, Yuan, Rusiniak, Acuna, Torralba, Fidler, "Meta-Sim: learning to generate synthetic datasets", ICCV 2019 (arXiv 1904.11621). A network modifies scene-graph attributes sampled from a probabilistic scene grammar to minimise the distribution gap (to real/target data) while also maximising downstream task performance (meta-objective, small labelled validation set).
 Trap: the SIGNALS differ: SimOpt = real trajectories; Learning-to-simulate = labelled validation accuracy; Meta-Sim = distribution matching + validation task loss. Do not call all three "matching the real distribution".

**e. Invariance by augmentation** [VERIFIED at abstract level] — S. Chen, E. Dobriban, J. H. Lee, "A group-theoretic framework for data augmentation", *J. Machine Learning Research* 21(245):1-71, 2020 (jmlr.org v21/20-163; the pages 1-71 and venue seen, the article number 245 is from memory = [LIKELY]).
 Claim (abstract): data augmentation is an averaging operation over the orbits of a group that keeps the data distribution approximately invariant; it yields variance reduction; analysed for exponential families, linear regression, certain nets.
 Trap: do not say "augmentation guarantees an invariant model"; the theorem is about averaging over transformations that leave the data distribution (hence the label relation) invariant; if a transformation changes the label (e.g. flipping a "6"), it breaks the premise.

## L05 · Cases and coverage

**a. Caltech Pedestrian benchmark** [VERIFIED, read the TPAMI paper] — P. Dollár, C. Wojek, B. Schiele, P. Perona, "Pedestrian detection: a benchmark", CVPR 2009 (pp. 304-311, pages from memory); and "Pedestrian detection: an evaluation of the state of the art", *IEEE TPAMI* 34(4):743-761, 2012.
 Dataset (2012 paper): about 10 hours of video from a moving car; 250,000 frames (137 roughly minute-long segments) annotated, 350,000 bounding boxes, about 2,300 unique pedestrians; about 50% of frames contain no pedestrian, 30% two or more.
 Annotations: for occluded pedestrians TWO boxes — full extent (BB-full, the person including the hidden part) and visible region (BB-vis). Matching in the evaluation uses the FULL box, never the visible one. Always-ignored regions: boxes under 20 px high, truncated by the image border, labelled 'Person?' (ambiguous) or 'People' (crowd).
 Scale split by pixel height: **near = 80 px or more, medium = 30-80 px, far = 30 px or less**; about 69% of pedestrians are medium; log-average height about 50 px; medium is the automotive-relevant range.
 Occlusion: per pedestrian track, never occluded 29%, occluded in some frames 53%, occluded in all frames 19% (so over 70% are occluded at least once). Occlusion fraction = 1 − visible area / full area; 0% label = "box cannot represent the visible region" (invalid); partial = 1-35% occluded; heavy = 35-80%; over 80% = full. Fig. 5(c) pie as extracted from the figure text (LIKELY, approximate): invalid 11%, partial 10%, heavy 35%, full 44% of occluded instances.
 **"Reasonable" setting: pedestrians at least 50 px tall under no or partial occlusion** (so at most 35% occluded, i.e. at least 65% visible; the 65% is the devkit's visible-fraction cut, consistent with "partial = up to 35%").
 Metric: miss rate vs false positives per image (FPPI); the summary is the **log-average miss rate: the average of the miss rate at nine FPPI values evenly spaced in log space over [1e-2, 1e0]** (the minimum miss rate reached is used if a curve ends early). Evaluated on every 30th frame. In the 2012 evaluation the best detectors (MultiFtr+Motion, ChnFtrs, FPDW) scored log-average miss rates of 51-57% under "reasonable"; these are 2011-12 numbers, do not present them as current.
 Traps: the near/medium/far cuts are 80 and 30 px (NOT 100 and 50; a fetch summary said 100/50 and was wrong). 50 px is the REASONABLE cut, not a scale bin.

**b. KITTI 2D object detection difficulty levels** [VERIFIED, cvlibs.net evaluation page] — A. Geiger, P. Lenz, R. Urtasun, "Are we ready for autonomous driving? The KITTI vision benchmark suite", CVPR 2012.
 Easy: min box height 40 px, max occlusion level "fully visible", max truncation 15%. Moderate: 25 px, "partly occluded", 30%. Hard: 25 px, "difficult to see", 50%. Overlap needed for a true positive: cars 70% IoU, pedestrians and cyclists 50%.
 Label fields (devkit): truncated = float 0 (not truncated) to 1 (truncated), the object leaving the image boundaries; occluded = integer 0 fully visible, 1 partly occluded, 2 largely occluded, 3 unknown; bbox = left, top, right, bottom pixel coordinates (0-based); dimensions = height, width, length in metres; location = x, y, z in camera coordinates in metres.
 Trap: Tremblay 2018 used "height > 40 px and truncation < 0.15" = the Easy filter only.

**c. Coverage by counting** [DERIVED; numbers recomputed] — a case with per-frame probability p appears at least once in N independent frames with probability 1 − (1 − p)^N. For confidence c: N = ln(1 − c)/ln(1 − p) ≈ −ln(1 − c)/p. For c = 0.95, −ln 0.05 = 2.9957, so **N ≈ 3/p** ("rule of three"): p = 0.1 -> N = 28.4 (3/p = 30); p = 0.01 -> 298 (300); p = 0.001 -> 2994 (3000). For 50%: N ≈ 0.693/p.
 Trap: independence — frames from the same drive/scene are correlated, so the real coverage is below the formula; the formula is a best case.

## L06 · The rare case

**a. Effective sample size of weights** [VERIFIED] — Kish (1965), *Survey Sampling*, Wiley: weighting design effect deff = 1 + CV^2 of the weights; n_eff = n/deff = (sum w)^2 / (sum w^2). In importance sampling the same expression is used as a diagnostic (Owen, *Monte Carlo theory, methods and examples*, ch. 9, eq. 9.13 n_e = (sum w_i)^2/sum w_i^2; eq. 9.15 n/(1 + cv(w)^2), which is identical when cv uses 1/n instead of 1/(n−1)). Owen credits the delta-method derivation to A. Kong (1992), "A note on importance sampling using standardized weights", Technical Report 348, Dept. of Statistics, University of Chicago. Kong, Liu & Wong (1994), "Sequential imputations and Bayesian missing data problems", *JASA* 89, use n/(1 + cv^2) [from a secondary summary; title and venue from memory: LIKELY]. Liu (1996), "Metropolized independent sampling with comparisons to rejection sampling and importance sampling", *Statistics and Computing* 6(2):113-119, exists [VERIFIED] but I did NOT verify that it contains the ESS formula: do not cite it for the formula.
 Properties: equal weights -> n_eff = n; one dominant weight -> n_eff -> 1. Caveat in Owen: it is a heuristic (the derivation treats f(X_i) as independent of the weights), it depends only on the weights not on f, and "the point at which n_e becomes alarmingly small is hard to specify". Say "a diagnostic", not "the exact variance inflation".

**b. Importance sampling identity** [VERIFIED, read Owen ch. 9] — mu = E_p[f(X)] = E_q[f(X) p(X)/q(X)] (eq. 9.1); p/q is the likelihood ratio, q the importance distribution, p the nominal distribution. Estimator mu_hat = (1/n) sum f(X_i) p(X_i)/q(X_i), X_i ~ q, is unbiased. Support condition (§9.1): q(x) > 0 whenever f(x)p(x) ≠ 0 (q need not be positive everywhere).
 Self-normalised version (§9.2, eq. 9.6): mu_tilde = sum f(X_i) w_i / sum w_i, usable when p (and q) are known only up to constants; consistent but biased (a ratio estimator); needs the stronger condition q(x) > 0 whenever p(x) > 0. Owen's argument for the unbiased version: it is unbiased and has a simple variance estimate. Section titles: 9.1 Basic importance sampling, 9.2 Self-normalized importance sampling, 9.3 Importance sampling diagnostics. The book is a free online draft (artowen.su.domains/mc/), cite as "Owen, Monte Carlo theory, methods and examples, chapter 9".
 Trap: if the oversampling distribution q misses a region where p f is nonzero, no weight can repair it (the estimator is silently biased low).

**c. Prior shift and the logit correction** [VERIFIED, read both papers]
 - Saerens, Latinne & Decaestecker (2002), "Adjusting the outputs of a classifier to new a priori probabilities: a simple procedure", *Neural Computation* 14(1):21-41. If only the class priors change and p(x | class) stays the same, the corrected posterior is the old posterior multiplied by (new prior / old prior), renormalised over classes (their eq. 4). They also give an EM procedure to estimate the new priors from unlabelled data. In the two-class case this is the log-odds shift: new logit = old logit + ln[(pi'/(1 − pi')) / (pi/(1 − pi))] (new prior odds over old prior odds; DERIVED from eq. 4).
 - King & Zeng (2001), "Logistic regression in rare events data", *Political Analysis* 9(2):137-163. For choice-based (case-control) samples with population fraction of ones tau and sample fraction ybar, the slope estimates are consistent as they are and the intercept is corrected as **beta0_hat − ln[ ((1 − tau)/tau) · (ybar/(1 − ybar)) ]** (their eq. 7); the alternative is weighting with w1 = tau/ybar for ones and w0 = (1 − tau)/(1 − ybar) for zeros. They attribute prior correction to Prentice & Pyke (1979) and Manski & Lerman (1977). "Rare events" there means dozens to thousands of times fewer ones than zeros.
 Key assumption (Saerens): the training examples were chosen on the basis of the class label only, not of x. Trap: hard-negative mining (L06d) selects negatives by x (the score), so the correction does NOT apply to it; and the formula assumes calibrated posteriors.

**d. Hard-negative mining** [VERIFIED, read both papers]
 - Dalal & Triggs (2005), "Histograms of oriented gradients for human detection", CVPR: initial negatives = 12,180 patches sampled randomly from 1,218 person-free photos; positives 1,239 images (2,478 with reflections). A preliminary detector is trained, then run exhaustively over the 1,218 negative photos; its false positives ("hard examples") are added and the detector retrained once. This retraining improved the default detector by about 5% at 1e-4 false positives per window (miss-rate scale); extra rounds "make little difference".
 - Felzenszwalb, Girshick, McAllester & Ramanan (2010), "Object detection with discriminatively trained part-based models", *IEEE TPAMI* 32(9):1627-1645 (volume/pages from memory; title/authors verified): data-mining of hard negatives for SVM/latent SVM; hard = inside the margin or misclassified, i.e. y·f(x) < 1; keep a cache, drop easy examples (outside the margin), add hard ones; they prove the process terminates and converges to the exact optimum of the full training set. They note the idea was used by Dalal & Triggs and goes back to earlier bootstrapping methods.
 Trap: "hard negative mining" in the 2005 paper is a single retraining round on false positives found in person-free photos, not the margin-based cache of the 2010 paper.

**e. Rare-event simulation** [Rubino & Tuffin: VERIFIED; splitting origin: LIKELY]
 - G. Rubino & B. Tuffin (eds.), *Rare Event Simulation using Monte Carlo Methods*, Wiley, 2009 (ISBN 978-0-470-77269-0); covers importance sampling and splitting (chapters by L'Ecuyer, Mandjes, Tuffin; L'Ecuyer, Le Gland, Lezaud, Tuffin).
 - H. Kahn & T. E. Harris (1951), "Estimation of particle transmission by random sampling", National Bureau of Standards Applied Mathematics Series 12, pp. 27-30 [citation VERIFIED via a bibliographic record; a historical note finds the term "importance sampling" already in it]. That it is the origin of splitting (with Russian roulette) is the standard attribution in the splitting literature; I did not open it: say "goes back to Kahn and Harris (1951)" at most.

## L07 · What a label means

**a. Modal vs amodal** [VERIFIED]
 - Y. Zhu, Y. Tian, D. Metaxas, P. Dollár, "Semantic amodal segmentation", CVPR 2017 (arXiv 1509.01329): annotators outline and name every salient region with its full extent including occluded parts, plus a partial depth order; datasets: 500 BSDS images (multiple annotators) and 5,000 COCO images.
 - L. Qi, L. Jiang, S. Liu, X. Shen, J. Jia, "Amodal instance segmentation with KINS dataset", CVPR 2019, pp. 3014-3023: KITTI images (14,991: 7,474 train / 7,517 test) annotated with amodal instance masks, semantic labels and relative occlusion order; the modal (visible) mask can be inferred from the amodal ones plus the order. Method: multi-level coding.
 Wording: modal/visible = pixels actually imaged; amodal = full object extent including the occluded part.

**b. Box/visibility fields in real datasets** [VERIFIED] — Caltech (L05a): full box + visible box, ignore regions; KITTI (L05b): truncated (0..1 float: fraction of the object outside the image) and occluded (0/1/2/3 integer). Note KITTI's 2D box is the amodal-ish extent of the object clipped to the image; do not call it the visible box.

**c. Pixel-centre conventions** [partly VERIFIED]
 - OpenCV: pixel centres sit at integer coordinates, so (0,0) is the CENTRE of the top-left pixel. Evidence: OpenCV source `initIntrinsicParams2D` sets cx = (imageSize.width − 1)·0.5 and cy = (imageSize.height − 1)·0.5 as the default principal point (read in calibration.cpp, 4.x branch); a forum contributor (crackwitz, not confirmed as a maintainer) states that pixel centres are integers and that the ideal principal point is ((w−1)/2, (h−1)/2). I found NO official one-sentence statement in the docs: tag [LIKELY, source-code evidence].
 - Blender (and OpenGL-style graphics): continuous image coordinates are corner-based: `bpy_extras.object_utils.world_to_camera_view` returns normalised coordinates where "(0, 0) is the bottom left and (1, 1) is the top right of the camera frame" (docstring read in the installed 5.2.2). So pixel i spans [i, i+1) and its centre is at i + 0.5; the optical axis (ndc 0.5) sits at u = W/2, a pixel BOUNDARY for even W (measured: edge at ndc 0.5 of a 64-px image split pixels 31|32).
 - Conversion (DERIVED): u_opencv = u_blender − 0.5 (same for v). A projection that places the principal point at W/2 in "OpenCV centre-based" pixels is therefore half a pixel off from Blender's. The half-pixel matters when the label is a pixel-accurate mask or box edge.
 - Blender row order: `Image.pixels` is bottom row first (measured: the lit upper half of an 8x6 render appeared in rows 3-5); EXR files written by Blender store the top row first (measured: Position.Y is positive in file row 0); PNG files are top row first.
 - COCO bounding box: `bbox = [x, y, width, height]`, x, y = top-left corner, origin at the top-left of the image, values in pixels (floats allowed). Evidence: the official pycocotools code builds the corners as x1 = bb[0], x2 = bb[0] + bb[2], y1 = bb[1], y2 = bb[1] + bb[3] ([VERIFIED in cocoapi coco.py]; the cocodataset.org format page text could not be retrieved by my tool: tag the sentence-level wording [LIKELY]). Traps: KITTI boxes are [left, top, right, bottom] (corners), COCO is [x, y, w, h], PASCAL VOC/YOLO differ again.

**d. Depth vs range** [DERIVED; Blender part VERIFIED empirically]
 - Pinhole: a z-buffer/depth image stores z, the coordinate along the optical axis; LiDAR range is Euclidean distance r = z·sqrt(1 + tan^2(theta_x) + tan^2(theta_y)) (so r/z = sqrt(1 + (x/z)^2 + (y/z)^2)). At the image corner of a 50 mm / 36 mm landscape camera (half-FOV tangents 0.36 and 0.2025) r/z = sqrt(1 + 0.1296 + 0.0410) = 1.082; a 64x36 render's corner pixel centre gives 1.0791.
 - Blender Cycles/EEVEE Depth (Z) pass = z along the camera axis (measured: a wall at z = −6 gives Z = 6.0000 at all four corners, ray length would be 6.474; EEVEE gave 6.000006). Mist pass uses the Euclidean distance (measured, see L08f). So Depth and Mist differ in geometry.

**a. World and camera axes** [CHECKED 5.2.2; handedness: DOC/standard] — Blender's world is right-handed with +Z up (manual: Scene layout / Navigating; sentence not extractable). An identity-rotation camera (rotation_euler = 0) looks along world −Z, with image-up = world +Y and image-right = world +X (measured from `matrix_world`: forward (0,0,−1), up (0,1,0), right (1,0,0)); i.e. it looks straight DOWN at the ground. A level, forward-looking camera needs rotation_euler = (90°, 0, 0): then forward = +Y, up = +Z, right = +X (DERIVED, R_x(90°)).
 Relation to OpenCV (DERIVED): camera frame of Blender is x right, y up, z backwards; OpenCV is x right, y down, z forward, so x_cv = x_bl, y_cv = −y_bl, z_cv = −z_bl.

**b. Lens, sensor, field of view, sensor_fit, shift** [CHECKED 5.2.2; API strings read from the build — cite "Python API: bpy.types.Camera"]
 Defaults: lens 50 mm, sensor_width 36 mm, sensor_height 24 mm, sensor_fit 'AUTO', clip_start 0.1, clip_end 1000, shift (0,0), type PERSP.
 Field of view: angle = 2·atan(sensor / (2·lens)); measured 39.5978° for 36 mm / 50 mm (equals 2·atan(0.36)); 26.9915° for 24 mm / 50 mm.
 sensor_fit enum text: AUTO = "Fit to the sensor width or height depending on image resolution"; HORIZONTAL fits the sensor width; VERTICAL fits the sensor height. **Measured semantics (via world_to_camera_view)**: AUTO puts `sensor_width` along the LONGER image side (landscape or square: horizontal; portrait: vertical); HORIZONTAL puts sensor_width across the image width; VERTICAL puts sensor_height across the image height. `sensor_height` matters only for VERTICAL.
 Focal length in pixels (DERIVED from the measurement): f_px = lens · (image side in px that carries the fit) / (sensor size of the fit): AUTO -> lens·max(W,H)/sensor_width; HORIZONTAL -> lens·W/sensor_width; VERTICAL -> lens·H/sensor_height. Square pixels assumed (pixel_aspect 1).
 TRAP: `Camera.angle`, `angle_x`, `angle_y` depend only on lens and sensor sizes, NOT on the render resolution: for a 1080x1920 portrait AUTO render they still report angle_x = 39.6°, while the true horizontal FOV is 2·atan(0.36·1080/1920) = 22.9° and 39.6° is the vertical one. Compute K from resolution, not from `angle_x`.
 shift_x / shift_y (API text "Camera horizontal/vertical shift"): measured in units of the FIT side (AUTO: the longer image side S = max(W,H)); with the camera at the origin, the optical axis point (0,0,−5) lands at c_x = W/2 − shift_x·S and c_y = H/2 + shift_y·S in top-left pixel coordinates (corner-based): e.g. 1920x1080, shift_x = 0.1 -> c_x = 768; shift_y = 0.1 -> c_y = 732; for 1080x1920 shift_x = 0.1 -> c_x = 348. So a positive shift_x moves scene content LEFT, a positive shift_y moves it DOWN in the image.
 Principal point at shift 0: (W/2, H/2) in corner-based pixels (see L07c for the −0.5 OpenCV conversion).

**d. Pixel filter and edge sharpness** [CHECKED 5.2.2] — Cycles: `scene.cycles.pixel_filter_type` ∈ {'BOX','GAUSSIAN','BLACKMAN_HARRIS'}, default **BLACKMAN_HARRIS**; `scene.cycles.filter_width` default **1.5** (px; 0.01 was accepted). Manual page: Render > Cycles > Render Settings > Film (snippet: lower widths give crisper renders, higher widths are softer and reduce aliasing).
 Measured step edge (orthographic, 1 unit = 1 px, emission step 0.05 -> 1.0, 256 spp; share of the step seen in the pixel):
 – edge exactly on a pixel boundary: width 0.01 -> [0, 1] (hard, aliased); Blackman-Harris 1.0 -> [0.035, 0.965]; **Blackman-Harris 1.5 (default) -> [0.12, 0.88]**; Blackman-Harris 3.0 -> [0.04, 0.27, 0.73, 0.97] over four pixels; Gaussian 1.5 -> [0.09, 0.91]; Box -> exact pixel-area coverage.

## L10 · A little reality

**a. Domain adaptation bound** [VERIFIED by agreeing secondary sources; theorem statement not read in the primary] — S. Ben-David, J. Blitzer, K. Crammer, A. Kulesza, F. Pereira, J. W. Vaughan, "A theory of learning from different domains", *Machine Learning* 79(1-2):151-175, 2010.
 Form: for a hypothesis h in the class H, **target error <= source error + (1/2)·d_HΔH(source, target) + lambda**, where d_HΔH is the HΔH-divergence between the two input distributions (how well a classifier from H can tell which domain an input came from, measured through disagreement regions; estimable from unlabelled samples with a finite-sample VC term) and lambda is the error of the best single hypothesis on both domains together (the "combined error of the ideal joint hypothesis"; it cannot be estimated without target labels).
 In words: the target error of h is at most its source error, plus half the divergence between the two input distributions as seen by the class H, plus lambda (the irreducible error if the labelling functions of the two domains are incompatible). Trap: the bound is for binary classification with 0-1 loss; if lambda is large no method can do well.

A refiner network makes simulator images look real using UNLABELLED real images and an adversarial loss, while preserving the annotation information from the simulator (their phrase); three changes: a self-regularisation term (keeps the refined image close to the synthetic input so the label stays valid; implemented as an L1 term, details from memory: LIKELY), a local adversarial loss, and updating the discriminator with a history of refined images. **d. Estimating a miss rate (a proportion)** [citations VERIFIED; formulas DERIVED/standard]

American Statistical Association* 22(158):209-212, 1927 [pages 209-212 verified; issue number from memory]. C. J. Clopper & E. S. Pearson, "The use of confidence or fiducial limits illustrated in the case of the binomial", *Biometrika* 26(4):404-413, 1934 [volume and pages verified].
 - Standard error of a proportion: se = sqrt(p(1 − p)/n). Sample size for a 95% interval of half-width d: n ~ 1.96^2·p(1 − p)/d^2: p = 0.5, d = 0.05 -> 384; p = 0.1, d = 0.02 -> 864; p = 0.1, d = 0.05 -> 138; p = 0.02, d = 0.01 -> 753; p = 0.01, d = 0.005 -> 1,521 (computed).

**a. Common random numbers** [VERIFIED] — P. Glasserman & D. D. Yao, "Some guidelines and guarantees for common random numbers", *Management Science* 38(6):884-908, 1992. Abstract-level claim: CRN is a widely used variance-reduction technique for comparing stochastic systems whose effectiveness is provable only in a limited class of systems; the paper shows the roles of monotonicity and continuity and gives classes of systems where CRN helps and is even optimal (distributional comparisons, structural comparisons, sensitivity analysis).

## L12 · Proof

**a. Rule of three** [VERIFIED citations; statement checked numerically]
 - J. A. Hanley & A. Lippman-Hand, "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators", *JAMA* 249(13):1743-1745, 1983. B. D. Jovanovic & P. S. Levy, "A look at the rule of three", *The American Statistician* 51(2):137-139, 1997.
 Statement: if an event has not occurred in n independent trials, the 95% upper confidence bound for its probability is about 3/n (interval 0 to 3/n); exact one-sided Clopper-Pearson form 1 − 0.05^(1/n); 3/n comes from −ln 0.05 = 2.996. Numerical check: n = 30 -> exact 0.0950 vs 0.1000; n = 100 -> 0.0295 vs 0.0300; n = 300 -> 0.00994 vs 0.0100; n = 10 -> 0.259 vs 0.300 (the rule overstates by 16%, so it is good for n above about 30). The two-sided 95% upper limit is larger (n = 100: 0.0362).
 Trap: independence of trials; and "3/n" is an UPPER bound for the rate, it says nothing about the lower end.

**b. Kalra & Paddock 2016** [VERIFIED, read the RAND report] — N. Kalra & S. M. Paddock, "Driving to safety: how many miles of driving would it take to demonstrate autonomous vehicle reliability?", RAND Corporation, report RR-1478-RC, 2016; journal version *Transportation Research Part A* 94:182-193 (2016) [journal reference from the task brief, not checked: LIKELY].
 Key findings: to demonstrate reliability in terms of fatalities and injuries AVs would have to be driven hundreds of millions of miles and sometimes hundreds of billions of miles; fleets would need tens or hundreds of years; test-driving alone cannot give sufficient evidence for fatalities and injuries.
 Numbers: US 2013 had 32,719 fatalities = **1.09 fatalities per 100 million miles** (and 2.3 million reported injuries = 77 per 100 million miles; nearly 3 trillion miles driven per year).
 - To show with 95% confidence a fatality rate at most 1.09/100M miles: **275 million failure-free miles** (= 3/1.09e-8, the rule of three; exact 274.8M); a fleet of 100 vehicles driving 24 h a day, 365 days a year at 25 mph needs **about 12.5 years**.
 - To ESTIMATE the fatality rate to within 20% with a 95% CI: 96 fatalities, **8.8 billion miles** (about 400 years for the 100-car fleet).
 - To show it is statistically significantly LOWER than the human rate when the true AV rate is 20% lower (0.872/100M), 95% confidence, no power requirement: **about 5 billion miles** (about 225 years). With the usual 80% power (beta = 0.2): **more than 11 billion miles** (11.34 billion; 518 years). For a 5% improvement: 215 billion miles (nearly 10,000 years).
 **DISCREPANCY guard**: popular quotes mix these up ("11 billion" = 20% better WITH 80% power; "5 billion" = same without power; "275 million" = merely not worse, zero failures; "hundreds of billions" = small improvements). Always state which of the four questions is being answered. All are for the 2013 US human rate; the human rate changes by year.
