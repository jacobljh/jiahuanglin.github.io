## State at entry
Every number of Lessons 1–11 is a statement about the program: the program trains the detector (Lessons 1–6), defines its labels (7), is rendered (8), is checked (9), is calibrated by a few real frames (10), and decides whether the shuttle stops (11). The *street* is where the system will run. The lab has one privilege the project lacks: it can run the street's closed loop for free.

## The one new idea
**Proof about the street can only come from the street, and its price is set by the rarity of failure: n failure-free real trials bound the failure probability below ≈ 3/n; a program's job is therefore not to prove but to predict, rank and aim — and it is as good an evaluator as its rankings agree with the street's, which must itself be paid for in real events.**

## The argument you must derive
1. **What would count**: a claim of the form *P(collision | a close step-out) < ε*; the only evidence about the street is real events. With n independent real trials and no failure the exact one-sided 95% bound solves (1 − ε)ⁿ = 0.05 → n = ln 0.05 / ln(1 − ε) ≈ 3/ε (**rule of three**: FACTS: Hanley & Lippman-Hand 1983; Jovanovic & Levy 1997); with f > 0 failures use the exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson) bound. Tabulate n for ε = 10⁻¹ … 10⁻⁶ and for f = 0, 1, 3. Translate to **real hours** under an *explicitly assumed* event rate (e.g. a close step-out once per T hours of driving: state T as an assumption, show the table for three values) and compare, in one sentence each, with the published scale of the same argument for road vehicles (FACTS: Kalra & Paddock 2016: quote only the verified numbers and their wording).
2. **Why the program cannot replace the trials** (derived, not asserted): an estimate from the program is an estimate of the *program's* failure probability p_sim; it says something about the street's p only through the agreement between the two, and that agreement is itself a rate to be checked on real events: to confirm that |p − p_sim| < δ you need real events in proportion to 1/δ² (or 1/ε when the claim is about a rare p). Show it by simulation for the lab's own step-out episode: the width of the interval for p − p_sim against the number of real episodes.
3. **What the program is good for, measured — the sim-as-evaluator experiment**: the sixteen detectors of `SV.TABLES.models` (one per program `aaaa` … `bbbb`) are *sixteen candidate systems*. Run each in the closed loop of Lesson 11's engine in five worlds: the naive program (`aaaa`'s generator), `aaba`, `abba`, `bbba` and **the street** (lab privilege). For each world rank the sixteen systems by collision rate (and by margin); compute the rank agreement with the street's ranking (Spearman 1904; Kendall 1938; FACTS verifies the attributions); also the **open-loop exam's** agreement with the street's closed-loop ranking (the frame miss rate of Lesson 1) — Codevilla et al. 2018 / Dauner et al. 2023 as verified. Expected shape (the lab decides): a naive evaluator ranks the candidates badly (it calls the naive program's own detector the best); each repair of the series lifts the evaluator's agreement (the price list of Lesson 2 applies to the evaluator as well); the open-loop exam is better than the naive world and worse than the repaired one, or not; report what you measure.
4. **The cost of validating the evaluator**: with the street's per-episode outcomes for the sixteen systems stored, resample n real episodes per system (n = 20 … 400, many draws, seeded) and plot the Spearman agreement between the program's ranking and the ranking *estimated from n real episodes*, and the probability that the estimated ranking picks the right best system. The number of real events needed to *trust a repaired evaluator* (agreement above 0.9 with probability 0.9) is the second bill, next to the proof's.
5. **Aiming** (the third use, short and computed): with the evaluator you can search for where the system fails: show on the step-out distribution which situations (distance bins) carry the program's collisions (a one-line table) and compute how much a *stratified* real test that allocates trials to those situations tightens the bound compared with trials drawn at the street's natural frequencies (Neyman / proportional allocation by the program's predicted risk; derive the variance ratio; verify by simulation). The program cannot raise the *proof*; it can lower the real trials needed to *find* failures.
6. **The ledger** (the series in one table, every row a number your oracle can check from the shared tables and your own data, with units in frames, afternoons, labelled frames or real events): for each lesson's repair — what it bought (points of miss rate: Lesson 2's prices), what evidence it needed and what that costs; the exam (grading) in frames (Lesson 10); the evaluator's validation (step 4); the proof (step 1). Order by cost; say which line dominates. Keep rows that you cannot verify out of the table (prose is fine; numbers need data-n).
7. **Exit, demonstrated**: the ledger's total is not a number of renders: renders are cheap and unlimited here; it is a number of real events, labelled frames and human attention. Compute the share of the ledger that is *real* and show how it scales if the failure of interest is 10× rarer; the next question is how to spend such a budget when what is cheap (frames) is not what is needed (consequences), and how a model of the street is trained then.
